What Your Restaurant Architect Does During Construction

Zelta Design · 4 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

During construction the architect can answer the contractor's questions, review shop drawings and product submittals, handle changes that affect the approved permit drawings and support inspections through closeout. How much of that is included depends on the architect's contract: some proposals include it, others bill it as additional services. The contractor, not the architect, runs the job site.

Once your restaurant’s permit is issued, the architect’s job changes from drawing to answering. During construction the architect can respond to the contractor’s questions, review shop drawings and product submittals, handle changes that touch the approved permit set, and support the inspections that lead to closeout. This phase is called construction administration.

Two things to settle before work starts. First, what is included: some architects’ contracts include construction support, others bill it as additional services when you ask for it. Read the proposal (see what’s in a restaurant architect’s proposal). Second, who does what: the architect does not run the site, set the schedule or direct the trades. The general contractor does. The architect’s role is to keep what is built matched to what the brand, the landlord and the building department approved.

What construction administration covers

In practice the phase has five parts:

  • Answering questions from the contractor in writing.
  • Reviewing submittals, the product data and shop drawings the contractor sends before ordering or fabricating.
  • Handling changes that affect the permit drawings, including filing revisions when the building department needs them.
  • Supporting inspections with drawings, details or letters inspectors ask for.
  • Closeout support, the paperwork that feeds the final sign-offs.

Questions from the field: RFIs

An RFI, a request for information, is a written question from the contractor when something in the drawings is unclear, conflicts with something else or does not match what they find on site. Our drawings tell the contractor to raise an RFI whenever there is a discrepancy, or anything that could affect structural stability, rather than deciding in the field.

A complete RFI reduces back and forth. It should include:

  • The sheet and detail number the question is about.
  • The condition found, with a photo and measurements if it comes from the site.
  • What the contractor proposes, if anything, so the answer can be a yes.
  • What is affected: other trades, schedule, cost or inspections.
  • When the answer is needed to avoid holding up work.

A good answer is written, specific and filed, so everyone on the job sees the same answer. If the answer changes the approved drawings, it also says whether a revision must be filed.

Submittals and shop drawings

Before the contractor orders equipment or builds anything fabricated, they send submittals: product data sheets and shop drawings, the fabricator’s detailed drawings of what they will make. Typical restaurant submittals include the hood and fire suppression system, walk-in coolers, rooftop units, storefront glazing and signs.

Each one is checked against the design and the permit. A few patterns from our records:

  • Deferred submittals are planned, not forgotten. Confirm which items the building official accepts as deferred, list them on the permit drawings, and obtain approval before installing those items.
  • Brands control alternates. On our Taco Bell in Manville, New Jersey, the brand’s construction representative asked to be told at the preconstruction meeting if the specified alternate exterior materials could not be obtained. A product not on the brand’s list needs the brand’s written approval first.
  • Sheet numbers matter. When a revised set renumbers sheets, the contractor needs to know, so shop drawings and RFIs point at the right sheet.

Changes after the permit

A change made in the field can put the work out of step with the approved drawings, and inspectors check against those drawings. Many building departments require a revision filed and approved before they will pass the affected inspection, and some towns are strict that approved exterior elevations be built exactly as drawn.

Inspectors can also require changes. On a northern Virginia fit-out, the fire marshal required the open space above a walk-in cooler to be closed off, and the building inspector required a field revision documenting the as-built enclosure. We drew the detail to match what was actually installed and sealed the revision.

Brand and landlord approvals work the same way. A change after approval usually has to go back through their review (see how franchise brand approvals work). The rule we give contractors is simple: ask before you build something different from the drawings.

Concealed conditions

Some facts cannot be known until demolition opens the space, such as the roof framing under a new rooftop unit, an old slab trench or a different electrical panel than the survey showed. Good drawings say what is unknown and how it will be confirmed, rather than guessing. When the contractor uncovers something, it comes back as an RFI, and the architect answers it, bringing in the structural or MEP engineer (mechanical, electrical and plumbing) where needed and revising the permit drawings if the change requires it. See rooftop units and structural checks.

Inspections

The contractor schedules most inspections, and each trade has its own. The architect’s part is making sure the inspector has what is needed: the approved drawings on site, any revision letters, and equipment documents. For a new rooftop unit in Queens, Erik Liepins, RA, our Principal Architect, advised keeping the equipment’s certificate of compliance on site, because the fire department may ask for it on a visit.

Closeout handoff

When the work is done, the architect’s closeout role is to hand over clean evidence:

  • Final approved drawings, with every filed revision.
  • RFI answers and approved submittals.
  • Any letters inspectors or the landlord asked for.
  • A list of open sign-offs and who owns each.

The certificate of occupancy and the health permit have their own steps; see getting a certificate of occupancy.

What owners can do

  • Confirm before construction what support your architect’s contract includes.
  • Make sure the contractor builds from the latest approved set, with a copy on site.
  • Send every design question through one channel, in writing.
  • Tell the architect before agreeing to any substitution or field change.

Questions

Common Questions

What is an RFI in construction?
An RFI, or request for information, is a written question from the contractor to the architect when the drawings are unclear, conflict with each other or do not match what is found on site. The written answer becomes part of the project record.
Can my contractor change the design during construction?
Not on their own. A change that affects the approved drawings, such as rerouted ductwork or a moved wall, often needs a revision filed with the building department before the inspector will sign off. Ask the architect before the work is built.
Is construction administration included in the architect's fee?
It depends on the contract. Some proposals include a defined amount of construction support, others list contractor questions, submittal review and site visits as additional services billed when requested. Check the proposal before construction starts.
Does the architect supervise the contractor?
No. The contractor controls how the work is done, the schedule and site safety. The architect answers questions, reviews submittals and checks that the work follows the approved design, within the scope set in the architect's contract.
