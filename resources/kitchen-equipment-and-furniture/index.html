Restaurant Equipment and Furniture: Who Specifies and Buys It

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In a franchise restaurant the brand's equipment and furniture package decides most of what goes in, the owner buys it through the brand's vendors or approved sources, and the architect and engineers design the space, power, gas, plumbing and exhaust around it. Independent restaurants use a foodservice consultant or dealer for the same job. The equipment list has to be settled before the engineering is finished, and deliveries have to be timed to inspections.

In most franchise restaurants the brand decides what equipment and furniture go in, the owner buys them, and the architect and engineers design the building around them. In an independent restaurant, a foodservice consultant or equipment dealer usually plays the brand’s part. Either way, the list has to be fixed before the engineering is finished, because every fryer, oven and cooler sets a power, gas, plumbing or exhaust requirement.

This guide covers the three groups of items in a restaurant, who handles each, and how to keep deliveries from holding up your inspections.

Three lists, three owners

A restaurant’s contents fall into three groups, and each usually has a different owner. Mixing them up is how items get missed.

  • Kitchen equipment. Cooking line, hood, refrigeration, walk-in, sinks, dish and prep equipment. For a franchise this comes from the brand’s equipment package and its vendors.
  • Furniture, fixtures and decor. Tables, chairs, booths, millwork, artwork and decorative lighting. Often called FF&E, short for furniture, fixtures and equipment. Brands usually have a standard package here too.
  • Owner items. Point of sale, cameras, speakers, menu screens, safes and networking. These are chosen by the owner and installed by the owner’s vendors or the general contractor.

Our proposals exclude kitchen equipment selection and specification, because on a franchise project that follows the brand’s equipment package. What we design is everything the equipment needs to work.

What the architect does with the list

The architect places every item and makes sure the building can serve it. That means the floor plan and equipment schedule, then the engineers’ power, gas, water, drain and exhaust for each item.

Owners add their own requests on top of the brand plan, and each one changes the drawings. On one recent project, the operator asked for room for four hot wells, an undercounter freezer at the front counter, a hand sink placed behind the drink fountain and speaker wiring at the front of the house. Each is small, but each is a power, plumbing or layout change that is easiest to make before the engineering is drawn.

For a franchise, the hood and its exhaust fans usually follow the brand’s prototype specification, with fans on the roof. Our guide to kitchen engineering covers the hood, grease and gas side.

Gas, electric or propane

The fuel each piece of equipment runs on is one of the earliest decisions, and changing it later redoes the engineering. Gas equipment needs gas piping and often a larger meter; electric equipment adds load to the electrical service.

On one project, the owner first planned to open on propane, which needs conversion kits for the gas equipment, then chose all-electric equipment instead. Both are workable, but each choice changes the electrical and gas design, so the decision belongs at the start.

Approvals that depend on the equipment

Several reviewers look at the equipment, not just the building. Getting the spec sheets early keeps these reviews moving.

  • Health department. Plan review usually asks for an equipment schedule and manufacturer spec sheets. The FDA Food Code, which most states adopt in some form, treats food equipment certified for sanitation by an accredited program as meeting its equipment rules. See health department plan review.
  • Landlords. On one project in a major shopping center, the landlord required all equipment to be NSF certified or equivalent, or alternates had to be found.
  • Fire and building. The hood’s fire suppression system is usually designed by the hood or equipment vendor, and walk-ins bring their own reviews (see walk-in coolers and freezers).

Furniture and finishes

Brands often specify furniture and finishes as tightly as the kitchen. On one project, the brand asked us to specify its standard furniture line, because it simplified purchasing, lead times and warranty.

The same brand had practical rules about materials. It told us acoustical fabric was not appropriate for its stores because of how much grease is in the air, and that all finishes had to pass the brand’s cleaning-chemical testing before they could be ordered. Rules like these exist because restaurant finishes are scrubbed daily.

Furniture also affects code. The seat count drives the occupant load and restroom count, and some seating must be accessible (see accessibility in restaurants and occupant load and seating).

Buying: brand vendors and equivalents

Owners often ask whether, once the drawings are done, the brand sends them to vendors for pricing, and whether they can buy the same specification elsewhere. The answer depends on the brand.

Many brands work through a purchasing program or approved vendors and allow equivalents for some items. Before you swap anything, confirm with the brand, and make sure the substitute matches the dimensions, power, gas and plumbing the drawings were engineered for. An equivalent that needs a different circuit is a change to the permit drawings. The same goes for walk-ins: owners ask whether they can upsize a walk-in by going custom instead of a quick-ship model, and the answer depends on the space, the floor, the condenser route and the brand.

Timing deliveries to inspections

Equipment and furniture have to arrive when the contractor and inspectors need them, not before or after. Several approvals depend on equipment being installed.

  • Health review and delivery. On one recent project, we aimed to mail the health department submission early enough to clear review before a planned equipment delivery.
  • Furniture. Vendors ask when furniture is needed on site. Give them a date tied to the end of finishes, and order early enough to cover their lead time.
  • Decor and signs. On one project, the artwork vendor needed to survey and install a wall panel before the signs went up, so the two vendors had to be sequenced.

Owner technology

Owner items need wiring designed into the drawings even if someone else installs the device. On one project, cameras and speakers were specified by the owner and installed by the general contractor. If the owner hired a separate installer, the contractor was still responsible for running the wiring. Settle this in writing before construction pricing.

Questions

Common Questions

Does the architect choose the kitchen equipment?

Usually not. For a franchise, the brand's equipment package sets it, and for an independent restaurant a foodservice consultant or dealer usually does. The architect lays out the space and coordinates the engineering around the chosen equipment.

Can I buy the same equipment from a different vendor if it is cheaper?

Ask the brand first. Many brands allow approved equivalents, but some items are tied to the brand's supply agreements, warranties or kitchen procedures. Anything you swap must match the dimensions, power, gas and plumbing the drawings were engineered for.

Does restaurant equipment need to be NSF certified?

Health departments generally accept food equipment certified to a recognized sanitation standard, and NSF is the most common certifier. Some landlords require it in their lease criteria too. Check the spec sheets before you buy.

When does the furniture need to be on site?

Near the end of construction, once finishes are complete, but it often has to be ordered much earlier because of lead times. Ask the vendor for the lead time when the drawings are approved, and give the contractor a delivery date to plan around.
