Restaurant Patios and Outdoor Dining: Approvals and Design

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant patio is approved by whoever controls the ground it sits on: the zoning office and site plan review on private land, a city license on a public sidewalk, and the landlord in a shopping center. Outdoor seats can also count toward the occupant load, exits, restroom count and health seat count, so plan the patio with the building, not after it.

Adding a patio or sidewalk seating is approved by whoever controls the ground it sits on. On your own lot or pad, that is the zoning office and often site plan review. On a public sidewalk or roadway, it is usually a city license. In a shopping center, the landlord approves it first. The building, fire and health officials can each have a say as well, because outdoor seats can add people the building has to serve.

The design matters as much as the permit. A good patio sits where diners are clear of cars and the ordering line, has shade and power where it needs them, and does not break the restroom count or the exit plan the building was approved under.

Who approves it

The approval path depends on where the seats sit.

  • Your own lot. On a freestanding or pad site, a patio is usually part of the site plan. A new patio on an existing building can be a site plan amendment, a zoning permit or a building permit, depending on the town.
  • A public sidewalk or street. Cities license this space separately. In New York City, sidewalk and roadway cafes are licensed by the Department of Transportation through its Dining Out NYC program, with its own design rules and application steps.
  • A shopping center. The lease and the landlord’s criteria decide whether you can have outdoor seats at all, how many, and what furniture and barriers are allowed.

Some towns pull every review into one checklist. Framingham, Massachusetts asks for a site plan of the dining area by a registered architect or engineer showing exits, fire lanes, hydrants, barriers, heaters, tents and accessible paths, reviewed by the fire department and board of health before it goes to the licensing board.

Outdoor seats count

Patio seats are real seats to most reviewers, so they belong in the code analysis from the start.

  • Occupant load and exits. If patio guests leave through the building, the model code adds them to the building’s occupant load when sizing exits. Framingham’s checklist says the design load must combine interior and exterior seats. Our occupant load guide explains why 50 people is the number to watch.
  • Restrooms. The same checklist requires the plan to show the total occupant load and the toilet count. A patio can push you past the next fixture threshold, as our restroom guide explains.
  • Health review. Health departments record seat counts. On a recent Memphis store, our health plan review submission listed the interior seats and the patio seats separately.
  • Parking. Where parking is tied to seats, patio seats can count too. Ask before you add them.

Accessibility

The patio has to be reachable, and some of its seating usable, by a person in a wheelchair. The 2010 ADA Standards require an accessible route to all dining areas in a restaurant, including outdoor dining areas (section 206.2.5), and at least 5 percent of the seating at dining surfaces to be accessible, dispersed through the space (sections 226.1 and 226.2).

In alterations there is a limited exception: an accessible route is not required to all parts of an existing outdoor dining area where the same services and decor are provided in an accessible space open to everyone. A new patio is new work, so design it accessible from the start: a level route from the entrance, a door that works, and accessible tables on the patio itself. Our accessibility guide covers the rest of the restaurant.

Designing the patio

The best patios keep diners out of the way of everyone else. Our published stores show a few patterns:

  • Away from the line. At Dave’s Hot Chicken in Greenville, North Carolina, red shade sails cover the patio, and the layout keeps entry, ordering and pickup in one sequence so patio diners stay clear of the line.
  • Wrapped and visible. At Dave’s Hot Chicken in Florence, South Carolina, a covered patio wraps the building, and glass on two sides of the dining room makes the patio seating visible from the road at night.
  • Beside the drive-thru. Dave’s Hot Chicken in Sumter pairs a drive-thru lane with a covered patio, and our Taco Bell in Morristown adds an outdoor patio to a freestanding drive-thru store.
  • At the front. On our Taco Bell in Manville, the building was mirrored from its first layout, which moved the patio to the front.

Details that make it work:

  • Separation from cars. Keep the patio clear of the drive-thru lane and parking aisles, and use railings, planters or barriers where it sits near traffic. Our drive-thru design guide covers the lane.
  • A door that serves it. Diners need a short path from the counter. Plan trash at that door too: on one store, the brand had no double-stream interior trash unit, so two single-stream units went at the patio door.
  • Shade, power and light. Shade structures, lighting, heaters and fans each need power and sometimes their own approval. Heaters and tents often go to the fire department.
  • Signs and fencing. Umbrellas with logos, banners and fences can count as signs or structures under the local code.

Sidewalk seating in cities

City storefronts can still have outdoor seats, but the space usually belongs to the city. At Caroline’s Donuts on the Upper East Side, black bistro tables sit both inside and along the sidewalk. At PopUp Bagels in Winter Park, yellow stools line the sidewalk for grab-and-go customers, keeping the small interior for ordering and pickup. Our Dunkin’ on the West Shore Expressway service road on Staten Island has a few outdoor tables along the storefront.

For seating in the public right of way, expect a license with its own clearance rules for pedestrians, a site plan of the tables and barriers, and annual renewals or seasonal limits, depending on the city.

Alcohol

If you serve alcohol, confirm your license covers the patio before you build it. Outdoor areas are often outside the premises described on the original license, and adding them can mean a separate application to the state or local liquor authority, with its own rules on enclosure and supervision.

Before you add a patio

  1. Who controls the ground: you, the city or the landlord?
  2. Is outdoor seating allowed in this zone, and does it need site plan or board approval?
  3. How many seats, and what does that do to exits, restrooms and parking?
  4. Is there an accessible route to the patio and accessible seating on it?
  5. Where do shade, heaters, lighting and power go, and who approves them?
  6. Does your liquor license need to change?

Questions

Common Questions

Do I need a permit for outdoor seating at my restaurant?

Usually. On private property, outdoor seating is typically a zoning or site plan item, sometimes with a building permit. On a public sidewalk, most cities require a separate license, and in a shopping center the landlord's approval comes first.

Do patio seats count toward my restroom requirement?

They can. Some towns require the occupant load and the restroom count to combine interior and exterior seats, and a patio can push a restaurant past the next fixture threshold. Check before you add seats.

Does a restaurant patio have to be accessible?

Yes. The 2010 ADA Standards require an accessible route to outdoor dining areas, and at least 5 percent of seating at dining surfaces must be accessible, dispersed through the restaurant. Limited exceptions apply to existing outdoor areas in alterations.

Can I serve alcohol on my patio?

Only if your liquor license covers it. Outdoor areas are often outside the originally licensed premises, so ask your state or local liquor authority what it takes to add the patio before you build it.
