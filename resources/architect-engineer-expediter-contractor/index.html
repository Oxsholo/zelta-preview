Restaurant Architect, Engineer, Expediter or Contractor?

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

On a restaurant project the architect designs the space and leads the sealed permit drawings, engineers usually design and seal the building systems, a civil engineer handles site work, an expediter files and tracks permits, and the contractor builds and in many places pulls the permits. Who seals which sheet depends on the state and the scope, and who coordinates whom depends on what each contract says, so assign both in writing before work starts.

A restaurant project has more parties than most owners expect. The architect designs the space and leads the sealed permit drawings. Engineers usually design and seal the building systems. A civil engineer handles the site if there is site work. An expediter, if you hire one, files and tracks permits. The contractor builds, and in many places also pulls the permits. Fire sprinklers, the hood’s fire suppression, kitchen equipment and signs usually come from specialist designers and vendors.

Two things are worth settling in writing before the first contract is signed: who seals which drawings, and who coordinates whom. Neither is automatic. This guide goes through each role and where the handoffs happen.

The owner, the brand and the landlord

These three make the decisions everyone else works from. The owner signs the lease, makes the layout and equipment decisions and hires the contractor. The brand supplies the prototype drawings and equipment package and approves the design in stages (see how franchise brand approvals work). The landlord delivers the space under the lease’s work letter and often has to approve drawings, and sometimes sign or authorize the permit application, before the town will act. See landlord work versus tenant work.

The architect

The architect designs the space and is responsible for the permit drawings as a whole. In our projects that means the site survey, the layout adapted from the brand’s prototype, the code analysis, the sealed architectural drawings, coordinating the engineers’ sheets so they agree, filing or supporting the permit filing, and answering plan review comments. Construction support is a separate scope item to confirm in the proposal (see what your architect does during construction).

Whether a restaurant needs an architect’s seal at all, and from whom, is set by state law; see do I need an architect to open a restaurant.

The engineers

Engineers design the building systems. A restaurant usually needs:

  • Mechanical: heating and cooling, the kitchen hood exhaust and make-up air (outdoor air replacing exhausted air).
  • Electrical: service size, panels, kitchen equipment power, lighting and emergency lighting.
  • Plumbing and gas: sinks, water heater, grease waste, gas piping.
  • Structural: roof framing for new rooftop units and exhaust fans, and any new openings.

Who may seal those sheets depends on the state. New Jersey’s Bulletin 96-2, for example, assigns most assembly buildings, the class most restaurants fall in, to an architect, but lets either an architect or an engineer sign and seal “engineering systems” such as structural, electrical, plumbing and ventilation. Other states draw the line differently, and local reviewers may ask for engineer-sealed sheets on particular systems. Ask your architect what your jurisdiction expects.

Coordination works the same way. Some architects hire the engineers and deliver one coordinated set; others leave the owner to contract them separately. Our proposals include structural and MEP engineering as their own sections. If the engineers are under separate contracts, write down who is responsible for making the sheets agree and for answering comments that cross disciplines.

The civil engineer and land surveyor

A civil engineer designs the site: grading, drainage, parking, the drive-thru lane, utility connections and often the location and size of an outdoor grease interceptor. A licensed land surveyor prepares the boundary and topographic survey the civil engineer works from.

For a fit-out inside an existing building you rarely need either. For a new building or a drive-thru that changes the lot, you do. Our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, a freestanding drive-thru, had its own civil engineer for the site while we carried the building. See building a restaurant on a pad site.

Fire protection designers and the hood vendor

Fire sprinklers and fire alarms are commonly designed by fire protection designers or by the installing contractors, and filed as their own submissions. Fire officials can be specific about who prepares them. Some fire marshal offices expect sprinkler shop drawings from a licensed fire protection contractor with a certified designer, not from the architect or engineers, so confirm the credentials before you hire the subcontractor (our Virginia guide covers how fire marshal review works there). On a Jersey City project, the drawings made the general contractor responsible for designing, filing and installing the fire alarm system.

The kitchen hood’s fire suppression system is usually designed by the hood or equipment vendor. See sprinklers, fire alarms and the fire marshal.

Kitchen equipment and sign vendors

For a franchise, kitchen equipment usually comes from the brand’s equipment package, so the brand and its vendors decide the equipment and the architect and engineers design around it. Independent restaurants often use a foodservice consultant or equipment dealer. Either way, the equipment list has to be settled before the engineering can be finished.

Signs are normally designed, permitted and installed by a sign company under its own sign permit, after the landlord and the town approve the design (see restaurant signage).

The permit expediter

An expediter files applications, tracks them through each office and follows up on comments on the owner’s behalf. The value is legwork and local knowledge, especially in large cities and busy counties. In Prince George’s County, Maryland, one operator engaged an expediter to handle permit processing while we supplied the drawings and comment responses. On an Orlando area store, the expediter handled the county portal while we supplied sheet lists and revision notes so each upload matched the right sheet.

Whether you need one depends on the office. Where the architect can file directly and the process is straightforward, an expediter adds a handoff. Where the offices are many and slow, a good one saves the owner time. See what actually speeds up a restaurant permit.

The general contractor

The contractor builds the project, hires the trade subcontractors, schedules inspections and controls the site. In many places the contractor also files or pulls permits, so you may need to choose the contractor before the permit can issue. Tennessee requires a state contractor license for jobs of $25,000 or more, and in Memphis the reviewer asked for the contractor’s local registration and a valuation letter on its letterhead (see our Tennessee guide). In North Little Rock, Arkansas, the general contractor pulls the permit, and many cities add their own license or bond requirements. On a Connecticut outlet center project, each trade contractor submitted its own trade permit after the main land use approval.

Questions

Common Questions

What is the difference between a permit expediter and an architect?
An architect designs the project and seals the drawings. An expediter's role is to file applications, track them through the offices and chase answers. The expediter depends on the architect for the drawings and the responses to plan review comments.
Can my general contractor handle the permits?
In many places the contractor must be part of it. Some cities issue the building permit to the licensed contractor or require its license and registration before issuance. The contractor still needs sealed drawings from the architect and engineers.
Do I need a civil engineer for a restaurant?
For a fit-out inside an existing building, usually not. For a new building on a pad site, a drive-thru that changes the lot, or a grease interceptor in the parking area, you normally do. The civil engineer prepares the site plan and leads the site review.
Who designs the fire sprinklers and the hood fire suppression?
Usually specialists. Sprinkler and fire alarm systems are commonly designed by fire protection designers or the installing contractors, and the hood suppression system by the equipment vendor, each filing its own approvals where required.
