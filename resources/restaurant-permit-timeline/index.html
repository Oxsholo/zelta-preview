How Long Does It Take to Design and Permit a Restaurant?

Zelta Design · 4 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

There is no single answer, because a restaurant is rarely one permit. Most need several separate approvals, such as building, health, zoning or fire, each with its own reviewer, order and comment cycle. The pace is set by how many approvals your site needs, which ones must come first, and how quickly comments and owner information come back.

Designing and permitting a restaurant takes as long as its slowest required approval, and most restaurants need more than one. The design and drawing work is the part you and your architect control. The review time belongs to the town, the health department and any other agency involved, and it varies by jurisdiction, by season and by how complete the filing is.

So the honest answer is not a number. It is a list: which approvals your site needs, which ones have to come first, and what is likely to slow each one down. That list is what we build for every store before we start, and it is what makes a schedule realistic.

Why one number does not work

Our own permit board, which tracks every filing on every active project, shows why. In October 2026 most projects on it had at least two separate filings, almost every filing sat with a different reviewing office, and the filings covered more than a dozen kinds of approval: building permits, health plan reviews, zoning and site plan approvals, change of use approvals, fire marshal and sprinkler reviews, a grease program application, a historic certificate and a land use permit on tribal land. Each of those has its own reviewer and its own pace.

The stages, in order

A typical restaurant project moves through five stages:

  1. Survey. We measure and photograph the existing space, note the panels, plumbing and rooftop units, and collect the landlord’s drawings.
  2. Schematic design. Layout options and a code analysis up front, then brand review if you are a franchisee.
  3. Construction documents. The sealed drawing set, coordinated with mechanical, electrical, plumbing and structural engineering.
  4. Permits. Applications, submission, review comments, resubmittal and approval.
  5. Construction. Contractor questions, inspections and closeout.

The first three stages move at the pace of decisions: how quickly the layout is approved, how quickly the brand and landlord respond, and how quickly equipment is chosen. The fourth moves at the pace of the agencies.

What sets the pace of permitting

How many approvals the site needs

A tenant fit-out inside an existing restaurant may need only a building permit and a health review. A new use in a former retail space can add a change of use or zoning step. A freestanding drive-thru can add site plan approval. A historic district adds a commission. Every added approval is another reviewer and another set of comments.

Which approvals must come first

Some approvals gate others. On a Maryland drive-thru pad, the county would not accept the building permit until site plan approval was granted. In a Pennsylvania borough, the municipal change of use approval had to close before the building review could start. In managed shopping centers, the landlord’s approval comes before the town’s. These gates run in sequence, not in parallel, so they add up.

Meeting calendars

Boards and commissions meet on fixed dates and set submission deadlines ahead of each meeting. On a historic district project in Ohio, the commission’s questions from one business meeting had to be answered by a noon deadline to stay on the next hearing. Missing a deadline can cost a full meeting cycle.

How the filing is submitted

Online portals have their own rules. One Florida county matched each uploaded file name against the sheet number printed on the drawing, and filings that did not match went back for renaming. A small village could not receive our permit set by email because the file was too large to reduce, so review could not start until another route was found. Some reviews do not begin until the fee is paid.

Comment cycles

Most reviews come back with comments. We answer them with a response letter and a revised sealed set, and the reviewer checks the response in a second cycle. Some comments can only close in the field: on one West Virginia project, a roof framing comment was left to be verified once demolition exposed the structure.

State and outside reviewers

Some approvals sit above the town. Depending on the state, health plan review, fire marshal review or even the building permit can come from a state agency. In parts of Pennsylvania, a third-party code agency hired by the municipality does the plan review. Each one is a separate queue.

Owner information

This is the delay owners can control most directly. A recurring pattern on our permit board is a filing that cannot go in until the owner’s information arrives: signatures, the general contractor’s name and license, the construction value, the menu for health review, the landlord’s authorization and the exact legal entity. One county voided an application for inactivity while those items were outstanding.

After the permit

Issuance is not quite the finish line. Some cities require the contractor to register before the first inspection can be scheduled, and on a West Virginia project the general contractor’s state license had to be in place before the permit was issued. Health approvals can be conditional, with the conditions cleared at the final inspection. At one opening inspection, the inspectors asked to see a copy of the fire marshal’s approval before signing off.

How to shorten the timeline

  • Start early. Review takes as long as the town takes; the part you control is when you start.
  • Have owner details ready before the drawings are sealed: signatures, contractor, construction value, menu and landlord authorization.
  • Settle brand and landlord comments before filing, so the town reviews the final set once.
  • Get the right survey. Drawings that start from an old plan instead of the real building draw comments.
  • Ask for a site-by-site list of approvals at the start, with the gates in order.

Questions

Common Questions

Why can't my architect give me an exact permit date?
Because the review clock belongs to the town, the health department and any other agency involved, not the architect. What we can do is tell you which approvals your site needs, what order they run in and what could slow each one, so the schedule you plan around is realistic.
Does the health department review happen at the same time as the building permit?
Often it runs in parallel, but it is a separate filing with its own reviewer. In some places it is a county office, in others a state agency, and a few jurisdictions take building, zoning, health and fire through one online package.
What is the most common reason a restaurant permit gets delayed?
A recurring one on our permit board is a filing that cannot go in because owner information is missing: signatures, the general contractor's name, the construction value, the menu or landlord authorization. Getting those ready before the drawings are finished saves real time.
Does a remodel take less time to permit than a new restaurant?
Usually fewer approvals are involved, since a remodel inside an existing restaurant often skips zoning and site plan steps. But a remodel still needs a building permit and often a health review, and old drawings that no longer match the building can cause comments.
