Permitting guide

Restaurant Build-Out Timeline: From Lease to Opening

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant build-out moves through nine linked stages: feasibility, survey, design, brand and landlord review, permits, ordering long-lead equipment, construction, final inspections and the certificate of occupancy, then opening. The total length depends on which approvals your site needs, which must come first, how fast decisions are made and how long each agency takes, so a realistic schedule is built from those gates rather than from a single number.

Opening a restaurant takes as long as its chain of dependencies. From a signed lease (or before it) to the first customer, every project passes through the same stages: feasibility, survey, design, brand and landlord review, permits, ordering long-lead equipment, construction, final inspections and the certificate of occupancy, then opening. Some stages overlap. Others cannot start until the one before them closes.

We do not publish a typical number of weeks, because the honest range is too wide to plan with. Review time belongs to each agency and changes with the season and the office. What you can plan with is the order, the gates and the decisions that move the date. This guide lays those out, stage by stage, and links to the guide that covers each one in depth.

Before the lease: feasibility

The first decision point comes before you sign. A short feasibility review answers whether the site can work: zoning, the existing utilities, the exits, the roof, and whether a hood, grease interceptor and restrooms fit. It also tells you which approvals the site will need, which is the single biggest factor in the length of everything that follows. A tenant fit-out in a former restaurant may need only a building permit and a health review. A former retail space can add a change of use. A freestanding building can add site plan approval. A historic district adds a commission.

See will this site work for the checks, and the lease checklist for what to settle with the landlord before signing.

Survey and design

Design starts from a measured survey of the real space, not an old landlord drawing (see why every project starts with a site survey). From there the architect adapts the brand’s prototype, or your own concept, into a layout with a code analysis. Once you and the brand approve the layout, the full sealed drawing set follows, coordinated with the mechanical, electrical, plumbing and structural engineering.

This stage moves at the pace of decisions. The decision points that most affect it are approving the layout, settling the equipment list, and answering questions such as keeping the existing hood or replacing it. Each late change reaches into the engineering. Our owner checklist lists what you supply at each step.

Brand and landlord review

Franchise brands review the design at set stages, and most landlords must approve tenant drawings before work starts. Some landlords also have to sign the permit application. Settle their comments before filing, so the town reviews the final design once. See how franchise brand approvals work and landlord work versus tenant work.

The landlord’s own progress can also be a gate. In a new South Carolina development, the tenant fit-out could not be submitted until the developer’s base building permit gave the site a permitted address the building department could attach our filing to.

Permits

Most restaurants need more than one approval, each with its own reviewer: building, health, fire, and sometimes zoning, site plan, historic or a utility’s grease program. Some run in parallel. Others gate each other. On a Columbus, Ohio storefront in a historic district, the building permit could not issue until the historic commission’s certificate of appropriateness was approved, and the commission met on a fixed calendar with submission deadlines ahead of each hearing.

Inside each review there is usually a comment cycle: the reviewer asks for changes, the architect answers, and the reviewer checks the response. Some offices do not start the clock until the fee is paid. See how to answer plan review comments, what actually speeds up a permit and health department plan review.

Ordering long-lead items

Kitchen equipment, walk-in coolers, rooftop units, the hood system, electrical gear and signs are made or shipped to order. Lead times vary by product and season, so ask each vendor rather than assuming. The decision point is when to order: after the layout and equipment list are approved, so you are not buying the wrong product, and early enough that items arrive when the crew is ready for them. If a utility needs to upgrade the electrical service or set a new gas meter, start that request as soon as the loads are known, because the utility works on its own schedule.

Construction

Construction starts once the required construction permits and health plan approvals are in hand and the contractor has everything the office requires. In several of our jurisdictions that means the contractor’s state license, local registration or business license must be on file before the permit is released, and in some cities the contractor pulls the permit itself. During construction the architect, within the contracted scope, answers the contractor’s questions, reviews submittals and handles any change that touches the approved drawings. See what your architect does during construction.

Inspections, the certificate of occupancy and opening

Each trade has its own inspections, then finals. The fire side and the health department usually inspect separately. The building department issues the certificate of occupancy, or its local equivalent, when the required sign-offs are in. In one South Carolina city no separate occupancy inspection was needed, because the inspections under the fit-out permits covered it. Confirm the health authority’s authorization-to-open process and inspection timing; building closeout alone does not authorize food service. In New York City, for example, a new operator may open after a waiting period without a pre-permit inspection (see NYC restaurant permits). Some cities issue the business license only after the certificate. See getting a certificate of occupancy.

Where the date usually moves

A schedule slips at the gates, not in the middle of a stage. Watch these:

  • Approvals that must come first, such as a zoning, historic or landlord step in front of the building permit.
  • Decisions after design starts, such as a new equipment package or a layout change after brand approval.
  • Owner items at filing: signatures, the legal entity, the contractor, the construction value, the menu and landlord authorization.
  • Comment cycles where a partial answer costs another round.
  • Contractor paperwork that holds an approved permit from issuing.
  • Long-lead items ordered after construction has started.

Questions

Common Questions

Why can't my architect give me an exact opening date?

Because several clocks belong to other people: the town's plan review, the health department, the brand, the landlord and the contractor. An architect can map which approvals your site needs, in what order, and what each one is waiting on, which is what a dependable schedule is built from.

Can construction start before the building permit is issued?

Generally not, although some cities have an early start route for limited work such as interior demolition. Ask the building department whether one exists and what it covers before planning around it.

When should I order kitchen equipment and rooftop units?

Once the layout and equipment list are approved by you and the brand, and before construction starts, so long-lead items arrive when the contractor needs them. Ordering before the design is settled risks buying the wrong unit; ordering after permit issuance can leave the crew waiting.

Does a remodel follow the same timeline as a new restaurant?

It follows the same stages but often with fewer approvals. A remodel inside an existing restaurant may skip zoning and site steps, but permit and health-review requirements depend on the work; finishes or like-for-like work may be exempt. Where drawings are needed, base them on a real survey of what is there.
