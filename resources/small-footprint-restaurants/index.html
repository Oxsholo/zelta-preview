Kiosks, Counters and Pickup-Only Stores: Designing Small

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A small restaurant works when every square foot has one job. The big decisions are whether to offer seats at all, how the brand's equipment and storage fit behind a short counter, and how customers and staff move through a tight plan with an accessible route intact. Small does not exempt a store from the health code, accessibility rules or the building code, although some code provisions do scale with size and occupant load.

A small restaurant is not a big restaurant with less in it. It is a different plan. In a store of a thousand square feet or less, the door, the ordering point and the pickup point sit within a few feet of each other, and the kitchen behind the counter has to hold almost everything a larger store keeps in back rooms.

This guide is about fitting a brand’s equipment, storage and seating into limited area while keeping an accessible route through it. How pickup customers, delivery drivers and kiosk users move through the room is covered in our guide to designing for mobile orders and delivery.

How small is small

The sizes in our own portfolio give a sense of the range:

  • Gumption Coffee in the Flatiron District is 737 square feet, and Gumption Coffee in Midtown is 767.
  • Our Wendy’s in the Green Acres Mall food court is 811 square feet, leaning on the mall’s shared seating.
  • Captain Cookie & The Milkman in NoHo is 875 square feet.
  • PopUp Bagels in Winter Park is 953 square feet, and PopUp Bagels in Rockledge is 1,167.
  • Dunkin’ in the Oculus at the World Trade Center is a compact kiosk with no seating at all.

What these have in common is not a size. The dining room shrank or disappeared so the counter could do more.

Decision one: seats, a few seats, or none

The seating decision comes first because it shapes the rest of the plan. Small stores we have designed fall into three groups:

  • No dining seats. Insomnia Cookies on Fordham Road and Pizza Hut in Jackson Heights are built for takeaway and pickup, with at most a chair or two for someone waiting.
  • A handful of seats. Our Dunkin’ on Broadway on the Upper West Side seats up to two. Dunkin’ in Washington Heights seats four. 375º Chicken ‘N Fries in Newark’s Gateway Center seats three at a narrow bar for concourse traffic.
  • A real but compact dining room. Dave’s Hot Chicken in Park Slope seats 16, Dave’s Hot Chicken on the Upper East Side seats 19 and Taco Bell on Westchester Square seats 26.

Seats and the occupant load, the code’s count of people a space holds, are related but not in a simple way. Under chapter 10 of the International Building Code, the occupant load of a dining area with loose tables and chairs is usually calculated from its floor area, not from the number of chairs. Fixed seating such as booths is counted by the seats themselves. So adding chairs to an existing dining area does not always change the number, but turning a waiting area into dining space, or adding booths, can. The occupant load then feeds into exits, plumbing fixtures and accessible seating.

Restrooms are where this matters most in a small store. Model codes include exceptions for very small spaces and for takeout-only counters, and many states and cities amend them. The 2021 International Building Code, in chapter 29, does not require customer restrooms in spaces built for quick transactions such as takeout and pickup when the public area is 300 square feet or less. Staff still need a toilet. Brands also have their own restroom standards, and some grant exceptions for small urban stores, as our brand approvals guide explains. Settle both before the layout is fixed, because a restroom is one of the largest fixed items in a small plan.

Decision two: fitting the front of house

In a small store the door, the line and the pickup point share a few feet of floor, so their order matters more than their size. Ordering usually sits close to the door. At Dunkin’ in Mott Haven, the compact footprint keeps ordering, the pastry case and a short queue line close together for fast takeaway.

Floor space is the constraint. Kiosks mounted on a wall or at the front counter save floor area, which is why one brand told us it prefers them there whenever possible. Seating usually goes to one side of the line or toward the back, as at Taco Bell on Westchester Square, where kiosks stand near the entrance and the seats fill the back. A window counter can add a few seats without taking floor from the line. A wall-mounted bench, like the one near the flavor wall at Wingstop on Flatbush Avenue, gives waiting customers a place to sit without adding a dining room.

Whatever goes in, the accessible route has to survive. The entrance, the path to the counter, a portion of the counter itself, any seating and any customer restroom have to work for a wheelchair user under the 2010 ADA Standards and your state’s building code. In a narrow store, that route usually decides where the kiosks, condiments and queue rails can go. Our accessibility guide covers what gets checked.

Decision three: where the back of house goes

The dining room is the easy part to shrink. The kitchen, storage and equipment are not, because the brand’s equipment list does not get shorter in a small store. That is usually where small stores are won or lost.

Common answers in our work:

  • A cellar for storage. Our Taco Bell in Lenox Hill has a 1,478-square-foot first floor with a 685-square-foot cellar for storage. Splitting storage onto another level brings its own code and labor questions, such as keeping a cellar corridor clear as an exit route.
  • Equipment on the roof. Moving refrigeration condensers or other heat-producing equipment to the roof keeps heat and noise out of a tight kitchen, if the landlord and the structure allow it. Our guide to adding rooftop units covers what that takes.
  • The counter as storage. Underbar coolers, retail shelving and grab-and-go cases do double duty. Our Dunkin’ in Rensselaer uses a stocked grab-and-go shelf by the register.

If the menu involves frying or grilling, the store usually needs a grease-rated hood, exhaust and grease handling at full scale, however small the room. In a city storefront that is often the hardest item on the list. Our guide to kitchen engineering explains what travels with the hood, and our guide to getting exhaust out of a city storefront covers the duct route.

Landlords and small spaces

Small spaces sit in malls, transit hubs and dense city blocks, and those landlords bring their own rules: approved contractors, rooftop limits, storefront and sign criteria. A small unit rarely gets a lighter version of them, so read the tenant criteria before you assume a compact store means a quick approval. Our mall and transit guide explains how that review works.

What does not shrink

  • Health review. A pickup-only store still needs food service plan review, hand sinks, a mop sink and cleanable finishes.
  • Accessibility. The entrance, the counter, any seating and any customer restroom still have to comply.
  • The building code. Exits, fire protection and the occupant load follow the same code as a large store. Some provisions scale with size, such as when one exit is enough or when restrooms can be shared, but the analysis still has to be done and shown on the drawings.

Questions

Common Questions

How small can a quick-service restaurant be?
Smaller than most owners expect. We have designed coffee shops of 737 and 767 square feet, a food court Wendy's of 811 square feet and a PopUp Bagels of 953 square feet. What sets the floor is the brand's equipment list and whether the kitchen cooks, not the dining room.
Does a takeout-only restaurant need a customer restroom?
It depends on the code your jurisdiction adopted. The 2021 International Building Code, for example, does not require customer restrooms in spaces built for quick transactions such as takeout and pickup when the public area is 300 square feet or less, but staff still need a toilet and local codes and brands can require more. Confirm the local rule before you lay out the space.
Where should ordering kiosks go in a small store?
As close to the entry as the line allows, and off the floor where possible. One brand told us its preference is kiosks mounted on a wall or at the front counter, because freestanding units take floor space a small store does not have.
