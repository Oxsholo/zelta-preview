What Drives the Cost of a Restaurant Build-Out?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant build-out's cost is set mostly by the building you start with and the systems a kitchen needs: exhaust and make-up air, grease, electrical service and gas, fire protection and the roof that carries the equipment. Site work, zoning approvals and brand or landlord requirements add to it. For a franchise, the brand's Franchise Disclosure Document, Item 7, gives the brand's own estimate of the initial investment range.

The cost of building out a restaurant depends far more on the building you start with and the systems a kitchen needs than on the square footage. The big drivers are the kitchen exhaust and the air that replaces it, grease handling, the electrical service and gas, fire protection, the roof that carries the equipment, and any site work or zoning approval the project triggers. Brand and landlord requirements set the finish level on top of that.

We do not publish cost figures, because they vary too much by site, region and year to be useful in a guide. This guide explains what moves the number and where design decisions can bring it down, so you can ask the right questions before you commit.

Where to find real numbers for a franchise

For a franchise, the most reliable starting figure is Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document. Federal rules require franchisors to give prospective franchisees this document, and Item 7 sets out the brand’s estimated initial investment, line by line.

Item 7 is a range across the brand’s whole system. Your site will land somewhere in it, or outside it, depending on the drivers below. A contractor’s bid on finished drawings is the only number specific to your restaurant.

The building you start with

The starting condition of the space is the single largest driver. A new building on a pad site carries site work, utilities and the whole structure (see building on a pad site). An inline space in a shopping center depends on what the landlord delivers. A former restaurant may come with a usable hood, grease interceptor and gas service.

Our proposals for existing spaces state that we reuse existing building systems where practical, and the survey is how we find out what is practical (see site surveys). What the landlord provides, and what you pay for, is set by the work letter, the part of the lease describing landlord work (see landlord work vs tenant work). On one project, the restaurant’s electrical service and gas were to be delivered by the landlord, which put a large cost on the landlord’s side of the lease.

Kitchen exhaust and make-up air

The hood system is one of the most expensive parts of a restaurant fit-out. The cost depends on how far the exhaust duct has to travel to reach the roof, whether it passes through floors above, and the make-up air unit, the equipment that replaces the air the hood removes.

In a one-story building with the kitchen near an outside wall, the route is short. In a ground-floor space under apartments or offices, the duct may need a fire-rated enclosure through every floor above. In a landmark building, even storefront louvers can need a separate preservation review: on one New York City project, louvers in the storefront needed an application to the city’s Landmarks Preservation Commission. See kitchen exhaust in a city storefront.

Grease

Grease handling ranges from a small unit under a sink to a large tank buried outside. The local sewer authority sets the requirement, so the same menu can need different equipment in two towns.

An outdoor interceptor adds excavation, site restoration and sometimes a civil engineer. On one recent sandwich shop, the sealed set had to show a new exterior grease interceptor before the building permit could be filed. Where an existing interceptor is large enough and the authority accepts it, reuse can save a great deal. Our kitchen engineering guide explains the options.

Power and gas

If the space’s electrical service is too small for the kitchen, a new service is often the largest single surprise in a budget. It can involve the utility company, a new meter and work outside the space, on the utility’s schedule.

The fuel choice matters too. On one project, the owner chose to open with all-electric equipment instead of a gas and electric mix, which changed the electrical load the building had to carry. Fix the equipment list early, because the engineering follows it.

Fire protection

Whether the building is sprinklered, and whether the work extends or adds sprinklers and a fire alarm, moves the budget. The occupant load, the number of people the code assumes are inside, often decides what is required.

Hood fire suppression is needed for most cooking lines regardless. Our guide to sprinklers and fire alarms covers when each applies.

The roof and structure

New rooftop units, exhaust fans and make-up air units have to sit on a roof that can carry them. If the existing framing is not strong enough, reinforcement adds structural work and roofing.

You often cannot know this until the ceiling is open. On one project, the roof framing could only be seen after demolition, so the structural engineer’s fee was set after the framing was exposed. See adding rooftop units.

Site work and approvals

Work outside the building brings in zoning, and approvals take time and consultant fees. A drive-thru, an addition or new signs can each need a board approval before the building permit.

On one New Jersey project, an addition on the front of the existing building would have encroached on the front yard setback and needed a variance, and the town allowed only one primary building sign, so the brand’s sign package needed a variance too. On another project, an addition where the old walk-in stood required planning board approval. A feasibility study finds these before you sign.

Brand and landlord requirements

The brand package sets finishes, equipment and fixtures, and landlords can add their own standards. Mall and airport landlords often have the strictest criteria (see malls and transit hubs).

On one project in a major shopping center, the landlord required all equipment to be NSF certified or equivalent, NSF being the best-known food equipment sanitation certification, or alternates had to be found.

City conditions

Dense cities add costs suburban stores never see. In New York City, our storefront jobs have needed separate plumbing and sprinkler filings alongside the main filing, and on one storefront a separate permit for the construction fence. Each filing has its own contractor, inspection and sign-off. See NYC permits.

Where design decisions save money

The best savings come from decisions made before the drawings are finished. A few that consistently matter:

  • Survey before you design. Knowing what exists avoids paying twice.
  • Put the kitchen near the shortest exhaust route.
  • Reuse what works. An existing hood, interceptor or service that meets the new load saves time and cost.
  • Settle equipment early. Late equipment changes redo the engineering.
  • Ask about approvals first. A variance found after design costs more than one found at the site visit.

Questions

Common Questions

How much does it cost to build out a restaurant?
It depends on the space and the systems more than the square footage, so no single figure is reliable. For a franchise, start with Item 7 of the brand's Franchise Disclosure Document, which lists the brand's estimated initial investment, then have the specific site checked for the cost drivers in this guide.
Is a former restaurant cheaper to build out than a retail space?
Often, if its hood, grease interceptor, gas and electrical service can be reused for your kitchen. If they are undersized or in the wrong place, the savings shrink. A survey before you sign tells you which.
What costs do owners most often miss?
Usually the ones outside the walls: a new or larger electrical service, an outdoor grease interceptor, roof structure for new rooftop units, zoning approvals for an addition or signs, and city requirements such as construction fencing.
