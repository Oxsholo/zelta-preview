Will the Town Let Me Open a Restaurant Here? Zoning Explained

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Zoning decides whether a restaurant may operate at an address at all. The district's use table says whether a restaurant is permitted, allowed only with a special permit after a hearing, or not allowed without a variance, and it may treat fast food, drive-thrus and outdoor seating as separate uses. A change of use from retail is also a separate building code review, so check both before you sign.

Zoning is the town’s rulebook for what can happen on each property, and it decides whether a restaurant can open at your address at all. Every property sits in a zoning district. Each district has a use table that lists what is permitted outright, what is allowed only with a special permit after a public hearing, and what is not allowed. If a restaurant is permitted, you go straight to the building permit. If it needs a special permit or a variance, a board decides first, and that hearing sets your schedule.

Zoning is also only half of the question when you convert a store. Changing the use is a zoning matter; changing the building’s code class is a building department matter. This guide explains both and what to ask before you sign. For the wider pre-lease check, see will this site work.

Read the use table, then read the fine print

The answer starts in the use table, but restaurants are rarely one line. Many codes split them into sit-down restaurants, fast food or takeout restaurants, drive-thrus, bars and outdoor dining, each with its own rule.

That split matters. On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, fast food was not a permitted use on the site, so the restaurant and all of its signs needed variances along with site plan approval. A drive-thru is often a separate use again, as our guide to drive-thru approval explains.

Look for conditions attached to a permitted use, too: limits on hours, seat counts, distance from homes or parking. A use can be permitted on paper and still fail a condition.

Ask the town to confirm it

The most reliable answer is the zoning office’s own. Ask early, and ask for it in writing when the decision is close or the deal depends on it.

Sometimes the answer is that zoning has no step at all. For a small takeout store in Pittsburgh, we confirmed with the zoning official that the store needed only building and health permits, not a zoning approval. Sometimes zoning comes first by rule: on a West Virginia store, the zoning certificate had to be issued before the building permit, and the zoning application needed only the property owner’s signature. Either answer changes how you plan the next months.

Three outcomes: permitted, special permit, variance

Once you know the use rule, the path falls into one of three:

  • Permitted. A zoning permit or sign-off, sometimes over the counter, and then the building permit.
  • Special permit, special exception or conditional use. The use is allowed if a board approves it after a public hearing. The board can attach conditions, such as hours or site changes.
  • Variance. Relief from a rule the project cannot meet. A use variance, for a use the district does not allow, generally needs strong reasons and is the hardest to get.

Who hears each varies. In New Jersey, the planning board reviews site plans and the zoning board of adjustment hears variances, as our New Jersey guide describes. In New York City, the Board of Standards and Appeals acts as the relief valve for properties uniquely restricted by the zoning rules.

Lots that are not one zone

Corner lots, deep lots and old commercial sites often carry more than one zoning answer. Check the map line, not just the address.

Two Queens sites show how. On one, the commercial district along the main road reached only 100 feet back from Merrick Boulevard, and the rest of the lot was zoned residential, so any development reaching into that part would need approval from the Board of Standards and Appeals. On another, a former gas station sat in a district not zoned for commercial use. The station’s commercial use had been approved by the board, so developing a restaurant there would need a new board application. Both are questions for the start of a project, not the permit stage.

Change of use and change of occupancy

Turning a store, bank or office into a restaurant usually means two separate reviews. Zoning decides whether the new use is allowed. The building department decides whether the space meets the code for a restaurant, which is generally classed as A-2, the code’s class for restaurants and other assembly uses.

Pembroke Pines, Florida, where we have designed restaurants, spells this out in its change of use guideline. The city’s zoning division determines whether a change of use applies, the tenant then needs a building permit for it, and before applying the tenant must obtain Broward County’s environmental review and transportation concurrency certificates. The guideline uses retail to restaurant as its example.

Smaller places can split it the same way. In a central Pennsylvania borough, a former bank becoming a restaurant needed the borough’s own change of use approval, separate from the building permit, which a third-party code agency reviewed. See our guide to converting retail space for the building side.

Former restaurants and lapsed uses

A space that was a restaurant is usually the easiest zoning case, but not automatically. If the old restaurant did not meet the current rules and has been closed for long enough, the right to reopen it may have lapsed.

On our Taco Bell remodel in Oxon Hill, Maryland, the county planning agency asked for proof that the restaurant had operated continuously without a long closure. Ask the town the same question for any former restaurant you are considering.

Zoning reaches past the use

Even when the restaurant is allowed, the zoning code still governs signs, additions, parking and upper floors. Each can need its own approval.

  • Signs. In Woodmere, Ohio, exterior signs on a bagel shop triggered the village’s planning and zoning review. See signage permits.
  • Upper floors. On a Brooklyn project, the second floor was zoned for offices, so a sign there could be permitted as long as the landlord approved.
  • Additions. In one Westchester County city, adding a walk-in cooler to the outside of a building needed planning board approval.
  • Parking and site changes. A restaurant often needs more parking than the retail use it replaces, and new striping or a new lane can send a project to a board.

Questions to ask the zoning office

  1. Which district is the property in, and is any part of the lot in another one?
  2. Is a restaurant permitted here, and does the code treat fast food or takeout differently?
  3. Is a drive-thru or outdoor seating allowed, and on what terms?
  4. Is the existing restaurant use, if any, still active?
  5. Does a change of use need its own approval before the building permit?
  6. Which boards, if any, will hear this project, and how often do they meet?

Questions

Common Questions

How do I find out if a restaurant is allowed at an address?

Look up the property's zoning district on the town's zoning map, then find restaurant in that district's use table. Because codes split restaurants into types and add conditions, the safest step is to ask the zoning office to confirm in writing.

What is the difference between a special permit and a variance?

A special permit, also called a special exception or conditional use, is for a use the code allows in that district if a board approves it. A variance is relief from a rule the project cannot meet, such as a use the district does not allow, and is generally harder to get.

Is change of use the same as change of occupancy?

No. Change of use is usually the zoning question of whether a restaurant may replace the old use. Change of occupancy is the building code question of whether the space meets the rules for a restaurant's code class. A retail-to-restaurant conversion often needs both.

Can a former restaurant lose its right to be a restaurant?

Yes, in some places. If a use that no longer meets the current zoning stops operating for long enough, it can be treated as abandoned. Ask the town whether the use is still active before you rely on it.
