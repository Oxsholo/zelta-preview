Permits by State guide

Opening a Restaurant in Ohio: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In Ohio, the building permit comes from a city, county or township building department certified by the state, or from the state's Division of Industrial Compliance where no certified department covers commercial work. The local health district must approve the kitchen layout and equipment in writing before that work is built, and zoning and design boards are local. Finding out who covers your address is the first job.

In Ohio, who reviews a restaurant depends on the address. The building code is statewide, but the Ohio Board of Building Standards certifies city, county and township building departments to approve plans and inspect. Where no department certified for commercial buildings covers the property, Ohio law sends the plans to the state’s superintendent of industrial compliance, in the Department of Commerce’s Division of Industrial Compliance. The kitchen is reviewed separately by the local health district, which licenses and inspects food service operations. Zoning, design boards and sign review are local.

Additional approvals depend on scope. A historic district, a new sign, a grease interceptor variance or the landlord’s own review can each add a step. We are licensed in Ohio and have taken restaurants through review in Columbus and in a Cleveland suburb, and this is how we plan Ohio jobs.

Step 1: Find the building department

The first job is confirming who reviews commercial construction at your address. Larger cities run their own certified departments. Many smaller villages and townships do not review commercial work themselves, and their projects go to the county or to the state.

We have seen both. In Columbus, the city’s Building and Zoning Services handled the permit for a fit-out in the Italian Village neighborhood. In a small village in Cuyahoga County, the building permit for a counter-service fit-out came from the Ohio Department of Commerce’s Division of Industrial Compliance, even though the village still ran its own planning and zoning review. One project, two levels of government, and the drawings had to satisfy both.

Step 2: Zoning and local boards

Zoning in Ohio is local. Cities, villages and townships each write their own code and run their own boards, and the triggers are not always obvious.

  • Signs. In the Cuyahoga County village, the interior work did not need a board, but adding exterior signage triggered planning and zoning review. The illuminated channel letter sign also had to go to the village’s architectural review board, the body that approves the look of exterior changes.
  • Historic districts. Columbus has several historic districts, each with its own commission. Our Columbus fit-out sat in Italian Village, where the Italian Village Commission issues certificates of appropriateness, its approval for exterior changes. That certificate had to come before the building permit.

Board review runs on a meeting calendar. In Italian Village, the commission asked for more detail on the roof, a guard rail and the exterior finishes before its hearing. We answered in writing with marked-up exhibits. Our guide to restaurants in historic buildings covers commission reviews in more depth.

Step 3: The building permit

The plans go to whichever department Step 1 identified. For most restaurant work they must be sealed by an architect or engineer registered in Ohio, though seal rules have exemptions; our guide on whether you need an architect explains them. Under Ohio law, construction may not proceed until the plans are approved.

How you file depends on the department. In Columbus, the building permit is filed through the city’s online portal, and the portal itself is the application. Supporting forms are uploaded as attachments rather than filed on paper.

Ask which vendor drawings, such as the walk-in cooler’s fabrication drawings, must accompany the initial application and which may be deferred. Obtain approved vendor drawings early, and obtain vendor drawings for long-lead equipment early enough that they exist at filing; defer an item only with the building official’s approval. Our guide to restaurant construction documents explains deferred submittals.

In Columbus the permit reviewer also checked the historic approval directly. The permit was not released until the certificate of appropriateness was provided, which is why we settle storefront, sign and exhaust details before anything else in a historic district.

Step 4: Grease and the city’s utility reviewers

Grease handling can involve more than one desk. In Columbus, a variance for the grease interceptor and a variance for the service port go to two different reviewers, and the city’s general grease inbox is not the place for an interceptor variance. A request sent to the wrong person simply waits. We confirm the right reviewer before sending anything. The engineering side is covered in our guide to kitchen hoods, grease and gas.

Step 5: The health district

Food service plan review in Ohio is done by the local health district. Under Ohio Department of Health rule 3701-21-03, no one may build, install, equip or extensively alter a food service operation until its layout and equipment specifications are approved in writing by the licensor, and the licensor acts on them within thirty days of receipt. Which office that is depends on the address:

  • In Columbus, it was Columbus Public Health, the city’s own department.
  • In the Cuyahoga County village, it was the Cuyahoga County Board of Health.

The health approval and the building permit are separate, and one does not stand in for the other. Kitchen construction waits for the health district’s written approval. The packet needs the menu, equipment information and food handling details from the operator, so we start it alongside the building set to keep both approvals on the same timeline. See our health department plan review guide for what goes in it.

The building and the landlord

In a shopping center, the landlord’s systems shape the drawings. At the Cuyahoga County site, the landlord recommended using the building’s existing fire alarm vendor to tie the restaurant’s devices into the new central fire alarm panel. Questions like this belong in the lease review, before the design is fixed. Our lease checklist lists the others.

Inspections and changes in the field

Whoever issued the building permit inspects the work, and field questions are normal. When a contractor wants to install something differently from the approved drawings, treat it as a change that needs approval before the work is covered.

The clean path is short. The contractor describes the alternative. The architect of record confirms it still meets code and documents it in writing. The inspector accepts it. Settling changes on paper keeps inspections moving and leaves a clear record for closeout. Our guide to what your architect does during construction covers how changes are handled.

After final inspections, the building department issues the certificate of occupancy, and the health district inspects before it licenses the restaurant.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Confirm whether a certified city, county or township department, or the state, reviews commercial work at the address.
  • Check whether the site is in a historic district or needs architectural review for signs.
  • Ask whether exterior signage alone will trigger planning and zoning review.
  • Find out which health district covers the address, and plan for its written approval before kitchen work starts.
  • Ask the landlord how a new fire alarm must tie into the building’s system.
  • Obtain vendor drawings for long-lead equipment, such as the walk-in, early enough to include them at filing if the department requires it.

Questions

Common Questions

Who issues a restaurant building permit in Ohio?

A city, county or township building department certified by the Ohio Board of Building Standards for commercial work, where one covers the property. Where none does, Ohio law sends the plans to the state's Division of Industrial Compliance. On one of our fit-outs in a small Cuyahoga County village, the permit came from the state, not the village.

Who reviews a restaurant's kitchen plans in Ohio?

The local health district, which may be a city health department or a county board of health. In Columbus it was Columbus Public Health; in a Cleveland suburb it was the Cuyahoga County Board of Health. It is a separate approval from the building permit.

Can kitchen construction start before the health district approves the plans?

No. Ohio's rule says a food service operation may not be built, equipped or extensively altered until the licensor approves its layout and equipment in writing. Plan for both the building permit and the health approval to be in hand before kitchen work starts.

Does a historic district change the order of approvals in Ohio?

It can. In Columbus, the building permit reviewer told us the permit could not be issued until the historic commission's certificate of appropriateness was in hand, so the exterior details had to be settled first.
