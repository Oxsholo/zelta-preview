Permitting guide

Restaurant Sign Permits: Zoning, Landlords and Historic Review

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant sign usually needs several separate approvals: the brand's sign standard, the landlord under the lease's sign criteria, the town under its zoning sign code and sign permit, and in a historic district, a preservation commission. Sign codes vary widely from town to town, so the allowed signage should be checked before the lease is signed and the design is fixed.

A restaurant sign usually needs more than one yes. Your brand has a sign package. Your landlord has sign criteria in the lease. Your town has a sign code that limits how many signs you can have, how big they can be and whether they can light up, and usually a sign permit to install them. In a historic district, a preservation commission may have the final word on all of it.

Each of these is a separate approval, and they do not always agree. The safest approach is to check what is allowed before the lease is final, then design the sign package around what can actually be approved.

  1. The brand sets the logo, colors, sign types and often a minimum sign package. On remodels the brand may require signs brought up to its current standard. On one of our remodels, that meant removing an old co-brand sign from the side of the canopy and adding a blade sign, a sign that projects out from the wall.
  2. The landlord applies the lease’s sign criteria covering size, placement, letter style and lighting, and usually wants the sign company’s shop drawings before approving.
  3. The town applies its zoning code to the number, area, height and lighting of signs, and normally requires a sign permit to install them.
  4. A historic commission reviews signs where the building is landmarked or in a designated district.

Local sign codes vary more than people expect

There is no single national rule for restaurant signs. Every town writes its own, and the differences are large.

New York City is a useful example of how layered the rules can be. The city’s Department of Buildings requires a sign permit for most signs, exempting only small signs under six square feet that are not illuminated. Signs must also meet the city’s Zoning Resolution, an illuminated sign that extends past the building line may need an annual illuminated sign permit, the electrical work is filed by a licensed electrician, and installation is done or supervised by a licensed sign hanger, with some exemptions.

Elsewhere, from our own projects:

  • One northern New Jersey town allowed only one primary building sign, so the proposed sign package needed a variance from the zoning board.
  • Another New Jersey town had a very limited sign code, so the allowable signage had to be confirmed early.
  • On one remodel, planning board or zoning approval was required for the drive-thru menu board, the parking lot striping and the signage, while the new sink, counter and painting inside needed no building permit at all.

Variances are possible but usually add a public hearing and time. If a site cannot carry the signs your brand requires without one, that belongs in the site decision, not in the construction schedule.

Site layout affects signage too

Signs are not just about the building face. A corner site may be allowed signs on both street frontages, but only if the local sign code permits signage on each frontage and the total stays within the town’s limits. At our Dunkin’ in East Harlem, the corner site allows signage on both street-facing sides. For drive-thrus, the menu board is tied to the queue, so its position is set with the lane and the site lighting, and brand remodel programs can add site items such as digital menu boards that need their own approvals. Our drive-thru guide covers the lane.

How landlord approval works in practice

Landlord approval is usually a review of the sign company’s shop drawings against the lease criteria. Each landlord documents it differently. One mall landlord we worked with would not fill out the sign company’s forms, but issued an approval letter on its own letterhead once the shop drawings were approved. Another landlord required a shop drawing from the sign company before it would consider the signage at all.

Get the sign company engaged early. Their shop drawings are what the landlord approves and, in many towns, what the sign permit is issued against.

Historic districts and landmarked buildings

In a historic district, a preservation commission reviews how the finished work will affect the historic building, and signage is a large part of that. In New York City, the Landmarks Preservation Commission says a permit is needed to install or replace signs and lighting on designated buildings.

Commissions often want far more than a sign drawing. For our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the commission asked for construction document level drawings of the whole storefront change. Our guide to restaurants in historic buildings covers that application and how the sign design was adapted.

Good design helps. At our Dunkin’ in the Paramount Building in Times Square, the sign is a marquee-style lit sign that matches the building’s Art Deco facade instead of standard channel letters, the individual lit letters most brands use.

Protect yourself in the lease

If a commission or zoning board will not rule on your signage until after you would normally sign a lease, raise it early. On one historic district project, the advice during lease negotiation was to include lease language letting the tenant out if the signage was not approved, with the brand’s approval or a lease exhibit showing the signage as the reference. Your attorney should draft that language. Your architect can tell you whether you need it.

From approval to installation

Once signs are approved, the sign company fabricates them. Before manufacturing, the sign or graphics vendor should field verify the dimensions on site. Where facade panels or artwork form the backdrop for the sign, sequence the trades so the backdrop is in place first.

A short checklist for owners:

  • Get the brand’s sign package and the landlord’s sign criteria together at the start.
  • Have the local sign code checked before the lease is final.
  • Ask whether the site is in a historic district or the building is landmarked.
  • Bring in the sign company early for shop drawings.
  • Build any variance or commission hearing into the schedule.

Questions

Common Questions

Is the sign permit part of my building permit?

Often it is a separate application. In many towns the sign company files it with its own shop drawings, and illuminated signs may also need an electrical permit. Some towns route signage through zoning or planning board review first.

What if the town will not allow the signs my brand requires?

The options are to redesign the package within the code or to apply for a variance, which is permission to depart from the zoning rules. A variance adds a public hearing and time, so it is better to know before you commit to the site.

Do drive-thru menu boards need approval?

Usually yes. Drive-thru menu boards and order canopies are often treated as signage or site features, and we have seen a town require planning board or zoning approval for a menu board even when the interior work needed no building permit.

Can I put a sign above the ground floor?

Sometimes. It depends on the zoning and sign rules for that floor and facade, and on the landlord. On one of our Brooklyn projects, the second floor's zoning allowed signage there, but only with the landlord's approval. Check each level before you design for it.
