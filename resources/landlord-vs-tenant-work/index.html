Landlord Work vs Tenant Work: Shell Types and the Work Letter

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Landlord work is whatever the lease makes the landlord responsible for; tenant work is everything the lease leaves to you, even when the two happen at the same time. Shell labels like cold shell, vanilla shell and white box have no fixed definition, so the work letter decides who provides the electrical service, gas, heating and cooling, restrooms and grease interceptor. Each item needs a provider, a size and a connection point written down.

Landlord work is whatever the lease makes the landlord responsible for building or delivering. Tenant work is everything the lease leaves to you. The two are defined by responsibility, not by timing: a landlord can install your rooftop units after you take the space, and a tenant can start design long before handover. The line is set in the lease, usually in an exhibit called the work letter, and for a restaurant it decides who provides the electrical service, gas, heating and cooling, restrooms and grease interceptor.

Owners ask us about this constantly. “Will the new electrical service and panel be the tenant’s scope or the landlord’s?” “Should we put installed by landlord on the plans?” This guide owns the work letter: who delivers each system and where their work stops. Whether the site can hold a restaurant at all is covered in site feasibility, and the facts to hand your attorney before signing are in our lease checklist.

Shell labels mean less than you think

Cold shell, vanilla shell and white box are useful shorthand, but no building code defines them. Two landlords can use the same label for very different spaces, so treat the label as a starting point and the work letter as the answer.

Roughly, as the terms are commonly used:

  • Cold shell or dark shell. Structure, roof and exterior walls, often an unfinished slab, with utilities stubbed to the building or the unit. No heating and cooling, no restrooms, no ceiling.
  • Warm shell or vanilla shell. Adds some basics, often a heating and cooling system, restrooms or finished perimeter walls. Exactly which ones varies.
  • White box. Closer to ready for finishes: walls taped and painted, a ceiling, lighting and sometimes restrooms.
  • Second-generation. A former restaurant delivered as is, with whatever the last tenant left (see turning a space into a restaurant).

None of these labels tells you whether the service is big enough for a kitchen. That is where restaurants get caught.

The sizes come from the test fit and the brand’s equipment list, not from the lease. If the lease says “adequate”, the table shows the number we need instead, and that becomes a question for your attorney.

The items that need a number

Electrical service. A restaurant needs far more power than most retail, and the kitchen sets the number. On one Queens project, the operator needed 400 amps and gas, and the landlord had to deliver both. A clear split names the size and where the landlord’s work stops, such as a main panel by the landlord and the restaurant’s own panel by the tenant. A vague one says “adequate service” and leaves you paying the difference.

Gas. Ask whether gas reaches the unit, who sets the meter and how much load it must carry. Check that every lease document agrees. A responsibility matrix, a landlord’s list of its own work and the landlord’s drawings are often written at different times, and they do not always say the same thing about gas. When two documents disagree, ask for written confirmation of the one that controls.

Heating and cooling. Some landlords deliver a system sized by a retail-style rule in the lease. A kitchen with a hood also needs make-up air, the outside air replacing hood exhaust, so the engineer checks whether the delivered system can carry it. On a Manhattan storefront, the landlord installed electric heating and cooling units through its own installer, so the tenant design had to work with that equipment.

Restrooms. Delivered restrooms help only if they meet the fixture count calculated under the applicable plumbing code and the accessibility requirements. On a recent Memphis restaurant, the landlord agreed to provide the electrical service, new heating and cooling, water and the restrooms.

Grease interceptor. Sometimes the landlord’s, sometimes yours. Either way, the local sewer authority sets the rules, so the size has to match your menu, not the last tenant’s.

Equipment the landlord picks for you

When the landlord supplies equipment, your design starts from it. Get the sizes and models before engineering begins.

On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, the landlord provided one 10-ton and one 15-ton rooftop unit, and Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, asked the engineers to design to those sizes. Landlords also control where equipment goes: one Bronx building manager would not allow rooftop units directly on the roof and required them on dunnage, a raised steel frame. Our guide to rooftop units and structural checks covers the roof side, and mall and transit restaurants covers landlord rules on contractors and trades.

When the landlord’s work is still being permitted

In a new or reworked building, your filing can depend on the landlord’s. Ask the landlord for its permit status and schedule before you commit to an opening date.

On our Taco Bell in Staten Island, the landlord and its architect asked us to hold our filing until the building’s temporary certificate of occupancy was secured. On a recent Memphis restaurant, the landlord’s improvements went through a separate permit from ours. For a brand-new outparcel, where the lot itself may not yet be recorded, see our pad site guide.

Turning the work letter into drawings

Once the table is agreed, the drawings follow it.

  • Every landlord item is shown as by landlord, with its size, so the plan reviewer sees a complete design.
  • Every connection point is drawn: where the landlord’s service ends and your panel begins, where the landlord’s duct stops, where the gas meter sits.
  • Anything the letter leaves vague goes back to you and your attorney as a question, with the number we need.
  • The contractor prices only tenant work, which keeps bids comparable.

Who pays

The lease decides who pays, not the code. Common arrangements are the landlord building an item outright, the landlord paying an allowance toward tenant work, or the tenant paying for everything. The cost risk sits in the gaps: a service that is too small, a system without make-up air, or an interceptor the sewer authority rejects. How the lease allocates that risk is your attorney’s call; our job is to put the right numbers in front of them.

Questions

Common Questions

What is the difference between a cold shell and a vanilla shell?
A cold or dark shell is usually bare structure with no finished walls, ceiling, heating and cooling or restrooms. A vanilla or warm shell usually adds some of those basics. The terms vary by landlord and region, so read the work letter for what is actually included.
Who pays for the electrical service in a restaurant lease?
Whoever the lease says. Some landlords bring a new service to the unit and leave the tenant the panel inside; others leave all of it to the tenant. What matters is that the lease states the size the kitchen needs, not just adequate service.
Should landlord work appear on my restaurant drawings?
Yes. We show landlord-provided items on the drawings as by landlord, with their sizes and connection points, so the reviewer sees a complete design and the contractor knows what not to price.
What if two lease exhibits disagree about who provides something?
Ask your attorney to get written confirmation of which document controls before design depends on it. We flag every item where the work letter, the responsibility matrix and the landlord's drawings do not match.
