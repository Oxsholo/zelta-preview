Restaurant Drive-Thru Design and Stacking Requirements

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A drive-thru is designed from the lot inward: how many cars the lane holds, whether there is an order point or only a pickup window, how cars enter and leave, and which way the building faces. The local zoning code usually sets the minimum stacking, the brand adds its own standards, and the town reviews the lane as a site change, so the building plan follows the lane.

A drive-thru is a site plan first and a building second. Before anyone draws a counter, the lot has to answer four questions: how many cars the lane can hold, whether the lane has an order point or only a pickup window, how cars get in and out of the property, and which way the building faces. Answer those and the floor plan follows.

This guide covers the lane and the approval questions it raises. For the full sequence of building a new restaurant on its own lot, from survey to building permit, see our pad site guide. For how a drive-thru compares with an inline or food court unit, see restaurant building types.

Two kinds of lane

A drive-thru lane is either an order lane or a pickup lane, and the difference changes the site plan. Decide which one you are building before the layout starts.

An order lane is the familiar sequence:

  • The entry. Where cars leave the parking aisle and commit to the lane.
  • The stack. The cars waiting before the order point, measured in car-length spaces.
  • The order point. The menu board, the speaker post and often a canopy over them.
  • The window or windows. Pay and pickup, sometimes combined into one.
  • The exit and bypass. How a car gets back to the street without blocking the lot.

A pickup lane serves only orders placed ahead in an app or online. There is no order point, so the menu board and speaker post can come off the drawings, and the lane can be shorter. If most of your drive-thru volume will be app orders, ask whether a full order point is needed at all. Check how the local code treats a pickup-only lane before you count on the shorter stack.

On a freestanding building the lane usually wraps the building, because that gives the most stacking on the least land. Our Dunkin’ in Hackettstown and Dunkin’ in Paramus follow that pattern.

Stacking: the local code sets the minimum

Most towns that allow drive-thrus set a minimum number of stacking spaces, and the number varies widely. Baltimore City’s zoning code, for example, requires at least four stacking spaces per bay or lane unless another part of its code requires more. Other codes also say where the cars must be, such as a set number behind the order point.

Check the total stacking requirement and the required queue behind the order point before adapting the brand’s sample layout (see our Maryland guide for a county site plan example).

Brands set their own standards on top. On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, the brand’s reviewer had us swap a site light and the menu board canopy so the menu sat at the car position the brand wanted. Moves like this are cheap on paper and expensive once concrete is poured.

The order point and menu board

The menu board is often the most reviewed object on the site. It is a sign, it may be lit or digital, it may carry a canopy, and it sits close to neighbors and the street. Many towns treat it as a sign or site item that needs zoning approval, as our signage guide explains.

Brand programs change it too. On our Taco Bell remodel in Ridge, New York, the brand’s newer image carried site requirements the older image did not, including a digital drive-thru menu board.

What the town reviews

A drive-thru is a site use, so the approval questions sit with zoning and planning rather than the building department. The direct answer: confirm the use is allowed, then expect site plan review of the lane, access and signs.

  • Is a drive-thru allowed here? Some zones permit it, some require a special approval, and some do not allow it at all. On one northern New Jersey project, the town required a zoning variance for the drive-thru itself.
  • Site items on a remodel. On one drive-thru remodel, the town said the new menu board, parking lot striping and signs needed planning or zoning approval, while the interior sink, counter and painting needed no building permit.
  • Traffic and parking. Boards look at how the queue affects the street and the parking lot. Some ask for a traffic or parking study; ask the planning office early whether yours will.

How those boards run and what comes before the building permit is covered step by step in our pad site guide and, for one state, our New Jersey guide.

Site access and parking

Planning boards look hard at how cars reach the lane. On the Paterson Taco Bell, the planning board required right-in, right-out access from the side street, meaning cars could only turn right entering and leaving.

Parking is usually the trade. A longer lane or a wider bypass often costs a space or two. On the Paterson job, the operator accepted losing a parking space as long as the brand’s architect approved it. That kind of sign-off is worth getting in writing before the site plan goes to the board.

Delivery and app orders claim part of the lot as well. On our Taco Bell in Manville, the brand asked for two or three dedicated stalls near the side entry for delivery drivers and mobile pickup, so those customers stay out of the drive-thru lane.

Turning the building to fit the lot

The lane often decides which way the building faces. On the Manville store, the building was mirrored from its first layout, moving the patio to the front and the cooler and freezer to the rear, so the sign tower faced the shopping center’s main entrance. Mirroring a prototype moves the drive-thru window, the kitchen line and every utility connection with it.

Towns care about the faces too. The ordinance for our Taco Bell in Morristown allowed building signs on the front and side only, not the rear, and the town was strict that the approved elevations be built as approved.

Separating drive-thru, counter and pickup customers

The best drive-thru stores keep each type of customer on its own path. Dave’s Hot Chicken in Sumter has a pickup shelf by the side door, apart from the counter and the lane. At our Dunkin’ and Baskin-Robbins on Jerome Avenue in the Bronx, drive-thru, counter and seated guests move through without crossing paths. At our Dunkin’ in Throggs Neck, we added a walk-up window alongside the lane so guests on foot can pick up without joining the car line.

For app orders and delivery inside the store, see pickup and delivery design.

Remodels and drive-thru additions

Plenty of drive-thru work is on existing buildings. Our Taco Bell on Page Avenue in Staten Island and Taco Bell in Hackensack are remodels of existing drive-thru stores. Our Dunkin’ in Guilderland included a drive-thru addition, and our Dunkin’ on the West Shore Expressway service road runs a drive-thru from inside a convenience store.

An addition is where zoning bites. A new lane is a site change, so plan for planning or zoning review and, in some towns, a variance. Our remodel guide covers what else a remodel can trigger.

Questions to answer before you commit to a site

  • Is a drive-thru permitted, conditionally allowed or prohibited in this zone?
  • Order lane or pickup lane, and how does the local code count stacking for each?
  • Where must the stacked cars be, and does the brand’s layout meet that?
  • Where can curb cuts go, and will the town limit turns in or out?
  • Does the lot fit the lane, or will the building need to be mirrored or rotated?
  • Is the menu board, canopy or speaker a separate sign or site approval?
  • How many parking spaces does the lane cost, and does the brand accept that?

Questions

Common Questions

How many cars does a drive-thru lane need to hold?

The local zoning code decides, and the number varies widely. Some codes also say how many of those cars must sit behind the order point. Baltimore City, for example, requires at least four stacking spaces per lane unless another part of its code requires more.

Does a pickup-only drive-thru window need a menu board?

Not necessarily. A lane that only hands out orders placed ahead has no order point, so the menu board and speaker post can come off the plan. The local code may still set a stacking minimum, so confirm how it treats a pickup-only lane.

Can I add a drive-thru to an existing restaurant?

Sometimes. A new lane is a site change, so expect zoning or planning review of the lane, striping, menu board and access, and in some towns a variance. Our Dunkin' in Guilderland, New York, included a drive-thru addition.
