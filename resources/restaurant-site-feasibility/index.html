Will This Site Work? Restaurant Feasibility Before You Sign

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A feasibility study answers one question before you commit money: can this site legally and physically hold your restaurant, and what would it take? It combines a test fit of your brand's layout, a zoning and code check, and a look at utilities, exhaust, grease and the roof. The result lists the risks, the investigations still needed and the likely upgrades, each marked confirmed or still open.

A site feasibility study tells you, before you sign or buy, whether a site can hold your restaurant and what it would take to get there. It is a short, focused piece of work: a test fit of your layout, a check of zoning and the building code, and a look at the things that drive cost in a restaurant, which are power, gas, exhaust, grease and the roof. The answer is rarely a plain yes or no. More often it is “yes, if”, followed by the approvals, investigations and upgrades still to be confirmed.

This guide owns that question: can the site work? Once the answer is yes, two other guides take over. The building facts to hand your attorney are in our lease checklist, and who delivers each system in a leased space is in landlord work vs tenant work.

Start with a test fit

A test fit is the fastest way to learn whether a space works. It is a simple floor plan that drops your brand’s kitchen, counter, seating and restrooms into the real unit or onto the real lot. For a franchise, it is also the first drawing the brand reviews, and brand teams use it to try ideas cheaply before design starts.

A good test fit answers:

  • Is the unit or lot big enough for the brand’s program?
  • Can the hood sit where its duct can reach the roof or another legal discharge point?
  • Is there room for the restrooms and an accessible route?
  • Where do deliveries, trash and mobile pickup go?
  • For a drive-thru, does the lane fit the lot (see drive-thru design)?

Check zoning and use first

Zoning decides whether a restaurant may operate on the site at all, so it comes first. The direct answer comes in one of three forms, and each has a different schedule.

  • Permitted. The use is allowed as of right, and you go to site plan review if needed and then building permits.
  • Special or conditional approval. The use is allowed only with a permit from a board, usually after a public hearing with conditions attached. In New York towns, for example, Town Law section 274-b defines a special use permit as an authorization of a use the zoning allows subject to requirements, and requires a public hearing.
  • Not permitted. The site may need rezoning, a use variance where the jurisdiction allows one, or a different use or location. In New York, Town Law section 267 defines a use variance as authorization to use land for a purpose the zoning does not allow, and for New York towns section 267-b requires showing unnecessary hardship, including that no permitted use can earn a reasonable return. Other states differ: North Carolina’s statute, for example, forbids changing permitted uses by variance.

Terms and procedures differ by state and town, so ask the zoning office which path applies and get the answer in writing where you can. On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, fast food was not a permitted use on the site, so the restaurant and its signs needed variances plus site plan approval; our pad site guide follows that path. Our New Jersey guide explains how boards there work.

Look past the use itself. Signs, drive-thrus and additions each trigger their own reviews. On a freestanding site in Bergen County, a preliminary code check found the town allowed only one primary building sign, so the brand’s standard package would need a zoning variance. Finding that during feasibility meant it was planned for, not discovered at permit time.

Some answers are worth getting in writing even when the use is permitted. On a recent fit-out in Durham, North Carolina, part of the permit plan was a written request asking the planning department to confirm that no site plan was needed.

Get the records the town already has

Existing approvals and drawings on file can answer feasibility questions quickly. Ask for them early, because records requests take time.

On a recent upstate New York restaurant, we filed a request under the state’s Freedom of Information Law and received the site’s existing site plans. Old site plans show what was approved for parking, the footprint and the curb cuts, which tells you how far a new plan departs from it.

For a former restaurant, also confirm the restaurant use is still valid. Many zoning codes end a nonconforming use, one that predates the current rules, after it has been discontinued for a period the local code sets. Our guide to turning a space into a restaurant explains why that matters.

Size up the utilities

Power, gas and water drive a large share of the cost, so we size them at feasibility stage, not after the lease. A restaurant needs far more electrical capacity and gas than most retail, and the kitchen sets the number.

The number can change with the menu. On our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the brand suggested planning for a third cooking line, and Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, pointed out that a third line needs a 600 amp service, not 400. Knowing the number lets your attorney write it into the lease and lets you compare sites fairly.

Ask the landlord or seller for the existing service size, whether gas is at the unit or only nearby, and where the water service comes in. For a vacant lot, ask the utilities whether service is available at the lot line.

Exhaust, grease and the roof

These three are where a site most often turns out harder than it looked, and each can be checked before you commit.

  • Exhaust. Can a duct reach the roof? In a city storefront under apartments, sometimes not. See urban exhaust.
  • Grease. Is there an interceptor, and will the local sewer authority accept it for your menu? Some authorities require a new exterior unit.
  • Roof. Can the structure carry new rooftop units and an exhaust fan? Where the framing is hidden, we say so and plan to confirm it once the ceiling is open. See rooftop units and structural checks.

When the site is new

A vacant lot or a developer’s new outparcel brings a different list: a land survey, usually a soils report, a civil engineer for the site plan, stormwater rules and confirmation that the lot legally exists. In a new development the lot may not yet have a recorded plat or a street address. Our pad site guide covers that path.

What you get at the end

A feasibility study ends with a short, plain list, not a thick report. Each line is marked confirmed, conditional or still open, with what would close it:

  1. The test fit, with seat count and any brand exceptions
  2. The zoning path: permitted, special approval, variance or rezoning, and who confirmed it
  3. Utility sizes needed, what exists today, and what the utility or landlord has said
  4. The exhaust route and grease solution, and whether the sewer authority has weighed in
  5. Roof and structural questions that need investigation
  6. Approvals in order, with those that need hearings flagged
  7. The building facts to hand your attorney for the lease

It does not give you a construction price or a guaranteed schedule. It tells you what has to be true for the site to work, so you can price and negotiate with the open items in view.

Questions

Common Questions

What is a test fit for a restaurant?

A test fit is a quick floor plan that places your brand's kitchen, counter, seating and restrooms in the actual space or on the actual lot. It shows whether the unit is big enough and where the hood, restrooms and entrances can go before you commit.

How long does a restaurant feasibility study take?

It depends on how fast the town, the landlord, the utilities and the brand answer. The drawing work is quick; the waiting is on records, utility answers and brand feedback. Start as soon as the site is under consideration, not after the lease is final.

Can I get zoning confirmation before I buy a building?

Often. Many zoning offices will say in writing whether a restaurant is allowed or what approval it needs, and some buyers make the purchase depend on that answer. A concept plan from the architect is usually what the office reviews.

Do I need a feasibility study for a former restaurant?

Yes, a lighter one. The use may already be in place, but the hood, grease interceptor, power and gas still have to suit your menu, and a restaurant use that has been discontinued may not carry over.
