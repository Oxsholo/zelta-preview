Turning a Retail Space into a Restaurant: Change of Use

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Turning a store, bank or office into a restaurant raises two separate questions. Zoning asks whether a restaurant is a permitted use, needs a special or conditional approval, or is prohibited, which may require rezoning, a use variance where legally available, or a different site. The building code asks whether the new use is a change of occupancy that brings stricter requirements. A former restaurant can skip some of this, but only if the use has not lapsed and the kitchen systems suit your menu.

Yes, a retail space can usually become a restaurant, but it has to clear two separate tests. Zoning decides whether a restaurant may operate there at all: as a permitted use, with a special or conditional approval, or not at all, in which case it may require rezoning, a use variance where legally available, or a different site. The building code decides whether the conversion is a change of occupancy, which can bring the space up to stricter rules for exits, restrooms, fire protection, ventilation and plumbing. The two are reviewed by different offices and answered separately.

A former restaurant, often called a second-generation space, can shorten both tests, because the use and some kitchen systems may already be in place. That only helps if the use has not lapsed and the equipment suits your menu. This guide covers the conversion first, then reuse.

Test one: zoning use

Zoning answers whether the town allows a restaurant on that property. There are three common answers, and each changes the schedule.

  • Permitted use. A restaurant is allowed as of right in that zone. You may still need site plan review for exterior changes, but no hearing on the use itself.
  • Special or conditional use. The zoning allows a restaurant only with a board’s permit, usually after a public hearing and with conditions. In New York towns, Town Law section 274-b sets out special use permits and requires a hearing.
  • Not permitted. A prohibited use may require rezoning, a use variance where legally available, or a different site. In New York, Town Law section 267 defines a use variance as permission to use land for a purpose the zoning does not allow, with its own legal test. Other states limit or forbid it: North Carolina’s statute, for example, forbids changing permitted uses by variance.

Names and procedures differ by state and town; some call it a special exception, others a conditional use permit. A zoning change of use can also have its own approval separate from the building permit. On a co-branded quick-service restaurant in a former bank in central Pennsylvania, the borough issued a municipal change of use approval before the building permit, which a third-party code reviewer handled. Our site feasibility guide explains how we find the zoning answer before you commit.

Test two: building code occupancy

The building code sorts uses into occupancy classes. Restaurants are generally Group A-2, the code’s class for restaurants and other assembly uses, though very small spaces with low occupant loads can be classified differently. Moving a space into a new class is a change of occupancy.

The definition reaches further than a class change. The International Code Council explains that under the 2021 International Building Code, a change of use within the same class can also count when it changes the purpose or level of activity and the code requires more for accessibility, structural strength, fire protection, exits, ventilation or sanitation. Which edition applies depends on what your state has adopted, and some states use their own rehabilitation codes, so the first question is which code governs your address.

A recent conversion shows what this looks like on the drawings: a 2,427 square foot chicken restaurant in a former phone store in an eastern North Carolina shopping center, designed as an A-2 space in a one-story sprinklered building.

What a conversion adds

Beyond the approvals, a retail shell usually lacks restaurant infrastructure. Plan for each of these in the budget and the schedule.

  • Grease. Without an existing interceptor you are building one, and the local sewer authority sets the size and location. Some require an exterior unit.
  • Exhaust. A new hood needs a duct route to a legal discharge point, which is not always possible in a multi-story building. See urban exhaust.
  • Power and gas. Most retail services are too small for a kitchen. Who brings in the upgrade is a lease question, covered in landlord work vs tenant work.
  • Restrooms, exits and fire protection. These follow from the occupancy and occupant load, which is why the code question comes before layout.

Taking over a former restaurant

A former restaurant can avoid the use question and some of the infrastructure, and the savings are real when the existing systems fit. Our renovation of an existing freestanding restaurant building for Dave’s Hot Chicken in Shelby, North Carolina is the best case: an interior and exterior renovation with no change of use, exits or occupancy and no footprint or site changes. The city’s building department confirmed it could be submitted under a change of tenant permit.

Inherited systems still come with conditions:

  • The grease interceptor has to be proven. In Anderson, South Carolina, the city sewer department accepted the installed grease control device but required it to be serviced and cleaned, with records, before opening.
  • Existing equipment still needs paperwork. On an outlet center project in Connecticut, the health reviewer asked for data on the existing water heater, which had to come from the landlord and operator.
  • Decide early what stays. Whether an existing exhaust fan stays or is replaced changes the mechanical drawings, the roof work and the fire review. Settle it before the set is issued and show it the same way on every sheet.
  • The use can lapse. Many zoning codes end a nonconforming use, one that predates the current rules, after it has been discontinued for a set period. On one Maryland remodel, the county planning agency asked for proof the restaurant had operated without a long closure; Prince George’s County, for example, looks for no break of more than 180 consecutive days (see our Maryland guide). If the use has lapsed, you may face the approvals a new restaurant would.

A new operator, menu or layout usually also means a new health department plan review. See health department plan review.

How to compare two sites

Ask the landlord and the town for these before you sign:

  1. The zoning status of a restaurant on this property, in writing if possible.
  2. The current approved use and occupancy, and whether the use has been interrupted.
  3. Hood, fan and duct sizes, age and inspection records.
  4. Grease interceptor size, location and last cleaning report.
  5. Electrical service size and whether gas is at the unit.
  6. Restroom count and whether the building is sprinklered.

Then have an architect walk the space with your menu and equipment list. A former restaurant whose hood is in the wrong place for your line can cost as much to fix as a retail shell, and a retail shell in a zone that permits restaurants can be the simpler path.

Questions

Common Questions

Can any retail space become a restaurant?

Only if zoning allows it. The use may be permitted outright, allowed with a special or conditional use approval after a hearing, or prohibited. A prohibited use may require rezoning, a use variance where legally available, or a different site. Ask the zoning office which applies before you sign.

What is a change of occupancy?

It is a change in how a building is used that the building code treats as significant: a change of occupancy classification, or, under the 2021 International Building Code, a change of use within the same class that brings stricter requirements for things like exits, fire protection, ventilation or sanitation.

Can I reuse the previous tenant's kitchen hood?

Sometimes. The hood, fan and duct have to suit your cooking line and meet current code, and the drawings have to say clearly what stays and what is new. Reviewers look closely at reused exhaust equipment.

What should I check before signing on a former restaurant?

Confirm the restaurant use is still valid with the town, get the hood, fan and grease interceptor sizes and condition, and find out what electrical service and gas the unit has today.
