Former Restaurant or Retail Space: Which Is Easier?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

A former restaurant, often called a second-generation space, can save time because the use, hood, grease interceptor and gas may already be there. That only helps if the equipment suits your menu, still passes inspection and the restaurant use has not lapsed. A retail conversion is a change of occupancy, so expect new kitchen infrastructure and more review.

A former restaurant is usually the faster, cheaper starting point, but not automatically. You save the most when the space is already approved for restaurant use, the kitchen hood and grease interceptor fit your menu, and the gas and electric service are big enough. You save little, and sometimes lose time, when the old equipment does not fit, does not pass, or the restaurant use has quietly lapsed.

Converting a retail space is the more predictable path, even if it costs more. You are building the kitchen infrastructure from scratch, and you are changing the building’s occupancy class. Here is how the two compare, using patterns from projects we have designed.

What “second generation” means

A second-generation space is one that has been built out for a restaurant before. A first-generation space is a new shell or a unit that has only ever held another use, such as a phone store or a bank. The label matters because the existing use, the existing kitchen systems and the existing approvals all carry over, for better or worse.

What a former restaurant can give you

The big-ticket items in any restaurant fit-out are the kitchen exhaust hood and its rooftop fan, the grease interceptor, gas service and enough electric capacity. If those are in place and suitable, they can save you weeks and a large share of the budget.

Our renovation of an existing freestanding restaurant building for Dave’s Hot Chicken in Shelby, North Carolina is the best case. The scope was an interior and exterior renovation with no change of use, exits or occupancy and no footprint or site changes, which kept the permit path simple.

Smaller inherited systems help too. On one quick-service fit-out in Queens, the new restaurant tied into the building’s existing fire alarm system instead of installing its own.

What it can cost you

Inherited equipment comes with conditions, and reviewers check them.

  • The grease interceptor has to be cleaned and proven. On a project in Anderson, South Carolina, the city sewer department required the installed grease control device to be serviced and cleaned before opening and permitting. On another job, the city let two brands share an existing interceptor, but final approval depended on a certification form from an approved grease hauler.
  • Existing equipment still needs paperwork. On an outlet center project in Connecticut, the health reviewer asked for the spec sheet of the existing water heater. Because it was existing, the data had to come from the landlord and operator, not from our drawings.
  • Decide early what stays. Whether an existing kitchen exhaust fan stays or is replaced changes the mechanical drawings, the roof work and the fire review. On that same project, the fan was a focus of the fire safety comments. Settle what is reused before the set is issued, and show it the same way on every sheet.
  • The brand may not accept what is there. On one remodel, the brand required an existing chair lift to be brought up to code in order to keep it.
  • The use can lapse. On our Taco Bell remodel in Oxon Hill, Maryland, the county planning agency asked for proof that the restaurant had operated continuously for years without a long closure. If a restaurant use has been abandoned, it may not carry over, and you could face the approvals a new restaurant would.

What a retail conversion adds

Turning a store, bank or office into a restaurant is a change of occupancy. Restaurants are generally classed as A-2, the code’s class for restaurants and other assembly uses, though very small takeout spaces can fall into other classes. Moving into that class brings the space under the rules for it, which can touch exits, restroom counts, fire protection and plumbing.

From our recent conversions:

  • Former phone store. A 2,427 square foot chicken restaurant in an eastern North Carolina shopping center, designed as an A-2 space in a one-story sprinklered building.
  • Former bank. A co-branded quick-service restaurant in central Pennsylvania needed an interior fit-out plus a change of occupancy. The borough issued its own change of use approval, separate from the building permit, which a third-party code reviewer handled.
  • New grease infrastructure. Without an existing interceptor you are building one, and the local sewer authority sets the rules. In one Pennsylvania township the authority required an exterior interceptor of a minimum size, with sewer and grease permits filed after the building permit. On another Pennsylvania job, the authority’s large minimum size could reportedly be waived in exchange for more frequent cleaning.
  • Plumbing reviewers apply grease rules strictly. On New Jersey projects, plumbing review has required kitchen floor drains to run through the grease interceptor, and food prep sinks count as grease-producing fixtures too.
  • Utilities may need upgrading. On one Queens project, the electric service and gas the kitchen needed had to be delivered by the landlord.

Former restaurant to a different brand

Even a restaurant replacing a restaurant is not a simple swap. When we converted a 1,457 square foot former sandwich shop in central Pennsylvania for a different sandwich brand, the work was a full interior fit-out to the brand’s current prototype. The food service plan review also went to the borough’s own health department rather than the state agency, because that borough is one of the few in Pennsylvania that runs its own.

How to compare two sites

Ask the landlord and the town for these before you sign:

  1. The current approved use, and whether it has been interrupted.
  2. Hood, fan and duct sizes, age and inspection records.
  3. Grease interceptor size, location and last cleaning report.
  4. Electric service size and whether gas is at the unit.
  5. Restroom count and whether the building is sprinklered.
  6. Any landlord rules on rooftop equipment, contractors or drawings.

Then have an architect walk the space with your menu and equipment list in hand. A former restaurant whose hood is in the wrong place for your line may cost as much as a retail shell.

Questions

Common Questions

Can I reuse the previous tenant's kitchen hood?
Sometimes. The hood, fan and duct have to suit your cooking line and meet current code, and the drawings have to say clearly what stays and what is new. Reviewers look closely at reused exhaust equipment.
Does a former restaurant need a new health department review?
Usually yes. A new operator, menu or layout generally means a new food service plan review, and the health department may ask for details on existing equipment such as the water heater.
What is a change of occupancy?
It is when a space moves from one building code class to another, for example a store becoming a restaurant. It brings the space under the rules for the new class, which can affect exits, restrooms, fire protection and plumbing.
What should I check before signing a lease on a second-generation space?
Confirm the restaurant use is still active with the town, get the hood, fan and grease interceptor sizes and condition, and find out what electric service and gas the unit has today.
