Putting a Restaurant in a Historic Building

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In a locally designated historic district or landmark, a preservation commission usually has to approve the exterior changes a restaurant needs, and in many cities that approval must come before the building permit is issued. The hard parts are usually the sign, the kitchen exhaust and the storefront, so design those with the commission's rules in mind from the first sketch, and have your attorney protect you in the lease.

A restaurant in a locally designated historic district, or in a building designated as a landmark, needs one more approval than most: the local preservation commission, which reviews how your exterior changes will affect the historic character of the building and the street. In many cities that approval, often called a certificate of appropriateness, has to be in hand before the building department will issue your permit. A building that is merely old, or only listed on the National Register, usually does not carry that extra review.

The commission’s focus is what the public sees, although some commissions ask for interior drawings to understand it. For a restaurant that usually comes down to three things: the storefront, the sign and the kitchen exhaust. Get those right early and a historic building can be a strong home for a brand. Leave them for later and they can stall the whole project.

Find out what kind of “historic” it is

Historic means different things legally, and the difference decides who reviews you.

  • Local historic district or landmark. This is the one that matters most. A town or city designates the district or building, appoints a commission, and requires its approval for exterior work. In New York City this is the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which says any change to the exterior of a storefront on a designated building needs its permit, as do new signs, lighting and awnings. In Jersey City it is the Historic Preservation Commission. In Columbus, Ohio, some districts have their own architectural review commission, such as the Italian Village Commission.
  • National Register of Historic Places. Listing is recognition and a planning tool. The National Park Service says listing places no federal restrictions on what a non-federal owner does with the property, unless the project involves federal funding, licensing or permits, and that state or local preservation laws may still apply. If the landlord is using historic tax credits, the landlord’s own commitments may limit your work.

Check the zoning map and the city’s preservation office before you sign. The landlord will usually know whether the building is designated, but not always what the commission actually allows.

Expect a full design, early

Commissions review the finished result, so they want to see it. For our Taco Bell in Jersey City, set in the stone base of a historic building, the city told us at the outset that the storefront alteration, two signs and an awning all needed a certificate of appropriateness, and that the commission needed construction document level drawings because it was judging how the finished work would affect a historic resource. A complete application included the commission’s form, an existing versus proposed plan set of the entire space including the interior, risers showing where the kitchen would exhaust, and sections through any new doors, window systems and awnings.

That changes the order of work. In a historic district, the storefront, sign and exhaust details have to be resolved before the building permit, because the commission’s approval gates it. In Columbus, the building permit reviewer on one of our projects told us directly that the permit could not be issued until the certificate of appropriateness was provided (our Ohio guide covers that sequence).

Commissions meet on fixed calendars and often comment before the hearing. On a recent fit-out in a Columbus historic district, the commission’s staff asked for an existing roof plan, a guard rail detail, a black finish for the exposed duct and railing, and a marked-up photo of the rear of the building before the hearing. Missing a commission deadline can cost a full meeting cycle, so commission dates deserve the same attention as permit deadlines.

The sign

The sign is where brand standards and historic guidelines often collide. The historic guidelines for our Jersey City Taco Bell did not permit internally illuminated signs, the lit box and channel letter signs most brands use. The design looked at external lighting instead, such as a slim top light or a minimalist gooseneck fixture. The projecting sign had to hang from a single arm, be as thin as possible, and be installed within the existing patchwork on the facade. An old photograph of the building, showing a large historic sign where the restaurant would be, helped set the size and style of sign the commission was prepared to consider.

Good design can turn the constraint into a feature. At our Dunkin’ in the Paramount Building in Times Square, the sign is a marquee-style lit sign that matches the building’s Art Deco facade. Brands can and do approve sign exceptions like these. Our signage guide covers the rest of the sign approvals.

The exhaust and the roof

A restaurant kitchen has to exhaust somewhere, and in a historic building every route is visible from somewhere. Commissions want to know exactly where. The Jersey City application asked for risers. In Columbus, the commission asked for the existing roof plan and specified the finish on the exposed duct and its railing. In New York City, adding exhaust louvers to a landmarked storefront meant a Landmarks application of its own.

Before you commit to a site, ask where the hood exhaust and the rooftop units can go and how much of them can be seen from the street. If the only route is a new louver in a historic facade, expect a longer review and possibly a no. Our guide to getting kitchen exhaust out of a city storefront compares the routes.

The storefront and the inside

Historic storefronts usually stay. The Jersey City design kept the building’s arched entry and tall storefront windows. New doors, window systems and awnings are drawn in section so the commission can see how they meet the old masonry.

Old buildings also hide surprises. Floors are out of level, walls are thicker than drawings show, and structure is rarely where the old plans say. A careful existing conditions survey matters even more here, because a design built on a guess can mean going back to the commission with changes. Many states also adopt the International Existing Building Code, whose chapter 12 gives building officials some flexibility for historic buildings, though not a waiver of life safety.

Accessibility still applies

A historic building does not exempt a restaurant from accessibility. The entrance, the route through the space and the restroom still have to work for a wheelchair user. Under section 202.5 of the 2010 ADA Standards, narrower alternatives for accessible routes, entrances and toilet rooms in a qualified historic building apply only where the State Historic Preservation Officer or the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation determines that full compliance would threaten or destroy the building’s historic significance. Where the qualified-historic alteration exception applies and toilet rooms are provided, provide an accessible room for each sex or one compliant unisex room (213.2, exception 2). Where physical access would threaten or destroy the historic significance and is not feasible, 28 CFR 36.405(b) calls for alternative methods of access instead. A step at a historic entrance is a design problem to solve early, not a reason to skip the ramp. See accessibility in small restaurants.

Protect yourself in the lease

The commission can say no, or yes with conditions that change your brand presence. On the Jersey City project, the advice during lease negotiation was that if the commission would not approve the signage before the building permit was filed, the lease needed language letting the tenant out if the signage was ultimately refused. Before you sign:

  • Confirm whether the building or district is locally designated.
  • Ask the preservation office what signs, lighting and storefront changes the guidelines allow.
  • Find a route for the kitchen exhaust that the commission is likely to accept.
  • Get the brand’s agreement to any sign or storefront exception.
  • Have your attorney tie the lease to the approvals you cannot do without. We can tell you which approvals those are.

Questions

Common Questions

Does a National Register listing stop me from changing the building?

Usually not on its own. The National Park Service says listing places no federal restrictions on a non-federal owner unless the project involves federal funding, licensing or permits. The rules that usually bind a restaurant tenant are local: a designated historic district or landmark with its own commission.

What does a historic commission review for a restaurant?

Mainly changes that alter how the building looks from outside: the storefront, doors and windows, signs and lighting, awnings, exhaust and rooftop equipment. Some commissions also ask for interior drawings to understand the exterior changes, and some review interior work on landmarked buildings.

Can I use my brand's standard illuminated sign on a historic building?

Often not. Many historic guidelines limit or ban internally lit signs, so brands accept a smaller, externally lit or custom sign. Find out what the district allows before you sign the lease.

Does a historic building have to be accessible?

Yes. A restaurant in a historic building still needs an accessible entrance, route and restroom. The ADA allows narrower alternatives for a specific element only where the State Historic Preservation Officer agrees that full compliance would threaten or destroy the building's historic significance. Where physical access is not feasible without that harm, federal rules call for alternative methods of access.
