Accessibility in Small Restaurants: What the ADA Requires

Zelta Design · 4 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

A small restaurant has to meet two accessibility rulebooks: the federal Americans with Disabilities Act and the accessibility chapter of the building code your state adopted. In practice the entrance, the order counter, a share of the seating, the restroom and the route from parking are what get checked, and in an existing space the scope of your work decides how much else must be brought up to date.

Accessibility in a small restaurant comes down to five places: the entrance, the order counter, the seating, the restroom and the route from the parking or sidewalk to the door. Every one of them is reviewed against two sets of rules. The first is the Americans with Disabilities Act, the federal civil rights law, and its 2010 design standards. The second is the accessibility chapter of the building code your state adopted, which is what the plan reviewer checks before issuing a permit.

The two overlap heavily but are enforced differently. The building department enforces the code at plan review and inspection. The ADA is enforced through complaints and lawsuits, often years after opening. A good design satisfies both on the drawings, so the owner never has to argue about either.

The entrance

The customer entrance has to be level or ramped. This is where small spaces, and especially older storefronts, most often run into trouble, because a single step at the door can mean a ramp, a regraded sidewalk or a lowered floor.

What we see on real jobs:

  • On a recent Bronx storefront, we added a ramp at the front entry. It was held to a 1:12 slope, the steepest the accessibility rules allow, kept to a short projection from the building, with round steel railings.
  • On another storefront, the ramp was questioned during design and confirmed as still required.
  • On a recent Florida fit-out, the front had to be level, while the rear door could keep its step because it was a service door, not a required accessible entrance.

The door matters as much as the floor. On a Manhattan storefront, Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, made at least 18 inches of clear floor space beside the latch on the pull side of the door a condition of approving the storefront design. Without it, a wheelchair user cannot open the door.

Ramps also have to work for the crew. On our Taco Bell in Manville, the brand’s main concern with the back-of-house ramp was trash cans rolling off the end, so a railing went there too.

The order counter, kiosks and condiments

The order counter needs a section no higher than 36 inches, at least 36 inches long, so a seated customer can order and pay. Brand counters are often taller, so this is a detail we draw for every store rather than assume.

Everything a customer reaches has to sit within reach range. On a Bronx Taco Bell, the brand itself flagged a three-tier condiment unit as not ADA compliant. On a Brooklyn job, the accessible section of the counter had to be marked with the access symbol, as an inlay or a sign. Self-order kiosks and mobile pickup shelves, now standard in most quick-service stores, follow the same reach rules, which affects where they are mounted.

Seating

At least five percent of the dining surfaces must be accessible: a top between 28 and 34 inches high, with knee and toe space underneath. They should be spread across the kinds of seating you offer, not tucked in one corner.

This is where modern restaurant interiors cause problems. Many current brand packages lean on high-top communal tables, counter seating along the windows and fixed booths. All of those can be used, but a room made only of them does not comply. We plan the accessible tables when the seating plan is drawn, not after the furniture is ordered.

The restroom

Small restaurants often have a single customer restroom, and it has to be fully accessible: a clear turning space, grab bars, a toilet at the right height and position, and a sink with knee space. On our Dunkin’ in Hillsborough Township, the review comments set a 34 inch maximum height for the sink, which is the accessible limit.

Brand standards add their own rules. At least one brand we design for requires a customer restroom in any store with a dining room. Health inspectors check the restroom too: on a recent Maryland opening, the inspection list required self-closing restroom doors.

Parking and the route to the door

If the restaurant has its own parking, it needs accessible spaces and an accessible route from them to the entrance. Local reviewers can be specific about where those spaces go. On a recent Maryland project, the county required the accessible space inside the restaurant’s own lot, even though other parking was across the road.

Existing spaces: how much do you have to fix?

This is the question owners ask most, and the answer depends on your scope. Under the federal rules, when you alter the main customer areas of a restaurant, you also have to make the path to them accessible, including the entrance and restrooms, up to a cost cap of 20 percent of the alteration. Many states apply their own versions of this rule, with their own thresholds.

That makes scope a design decision. On a recent bagel shop in Connecticut, the defined scope of work stayed under the state’s threshold for bringing the entire establishment into compliance, so some existing elements could remain as they were. The reverse also happens: a small added piece of work can push a project over the line.

The most expensive surprise in a second-generation space, a space that was already a restaurant, is an existing step at the door or a restroom too small to fix in place. We find both during the site survey, before the layout is drawn.

Questions

Common Questions

Does a small restaurant have to be ADA compliant?
Yes. The ADA applies to restaurants of every size, and the building code's accessibility rules apply to any space you build or alter. Small size changes how you comply, not whether you must.
Do I have to fix the whole space if I only renovate part of it?
Not always. Under the federal rules, altering the main customer areas also means making the route to them, the entrance and the restrooms accessible, up to a cost cap. Some states set their own thresholds, so the size of your scope can decide how much else is triggered.
Can my restaurant have only high-top tables?
No. A share of the dining surfaces must be at an accessible height with knee space, and they should be spread across the seating types you offer, so a room of only high-tops or communal benches will not pass.
Does the back door have to be accessible?
Usually not, if it is a service door and not one of the entrances the code requires to be accessible. The customer entrance must be level or ramped.
