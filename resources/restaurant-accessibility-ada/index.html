Restaurant ADA Requirements: Entrances, Seating and Restrooms

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant has to meet two accessibility rulebooks: the federal Americans with Disabilities Act and the accessibility chapter of the building code your state adopted. The entrance, the counters, the dining surfaces, the restrooms and the route from parking are where most requirements land. In an existing space, an alteration brings its own upgrade duties, and the federal duty to remove barriers where that is readily achievable applies whether or not you renovate.

Accessibility in a restaurant comes down to five places: the entrance, the counters, the dining surfaces, the restrooms and the route from parking or the sidewalk to the door. Each is checked against two sets of rules. The first is the Americans with Disabilities Act, the federal civil rights law, and its 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design. The second is the accessibility chapter of the building code your state adopted, which usually points to a national technical standard, ICC A117.1, and is what the plan reviewer checks before issuing a permit.

The two overlap heavily but are enforced differently. The building department enforces the code at plan review and inspection. The ADA is enforced by the Department of Justice and through private complaints and lawsuits, and a building permit is not proof of ADA compliance. Designing to both from the first layout is the best protection, though no drawing can guarantee how either is applied later.

The figures below come from the 2010 ADA Standards. Your state’s adopted code can differ in places, so the drawings follow whichever rule is stricter for each element.

The entrance

At least 60 percent of a facility’s public entrances must be accessible, under section 206.4.1 of the 2010 Standards, and an accessible route has to connect the entrance to accessible parking, public sidewalks and transit stops. For a small storefront with one customer door, that door is the accessible entrance.

Older storefronts are where this gets expensive, because a single step at the door can mean a ramp, a regraded sidewalk or a lowered floor. A ramp’s running slope can be no steeper than 1:12, one inch of rise per foot (section 405.2), with landings and handrails as the rise increases.

The door matters as much as the floor. When you approach a door from the front on the side you pull it, the Standards require 18 inches of clear floor beside the latch (Table 404.2.4.1), so a wheelchair user can reach the handle and swing the door past. Storefront frames, vestibules and planters often take that space without anyone noticing until inspection.

Ramps also have to work for the crew. On our Taco Bell in Manville, the brand’s reviewer had one concern once the ramps were code compliant: trash cans rolling off the end of the back-of-house ramp. The reviewer suggested a railing there.

Counters, kiosks and condiments

Where a restaurant has sales or service counters, at least one of each type needs an accessible portion (section 227.3). Order and pickup count as different types, so a separate pickup counter needs its own. The accessible portion depends on how a wheelchair user pulls up to it, under section 904.4:

  • Parallel approach, alongside the counter: a section at least 36 inches long and no higher than 36 inches.
  • Forward approach, facing the counter: a section at least 30 inches long and no higher than 36 inches, with knee and toe space underneath.

Brand counters are often taller, so we draw this detail for every store rather than assume the prototype covers it. On a recent West Virginia fit-out, the plan reviewer asked for an accessible counter section, and our answer was a counter 34 inches high for its entire length.

Everything a customer reaches has to sit within reach range. For an unobstructed forward reach, that means between 15 and 48 inches above the floor (section 308.2.1). Check condiment units for compliant reach and clear floor space. Self-order kiosks and mobile pickup shelves follow the same reach rules, which affects where they are mounted.

Seating

At least 5 percent of the seating and standing spaces at dining surfaces must be accessible (section 226.1). The count is by places to eat, not by tables. Accessible surfaces sit between 28 and 34 inches high (section 902.3) with knee and toe space, and they must be dispersed through the dining area (section 226.2), not tucked in one corner.

This is where current brand interiors cause trouble. Many packages lean on high-top communal tables, window counters and fixed booths. Whatever furniture is selected, provide the required accessible dining spaces (section 226). We plan the accessible places when the seating plan is drawn, before the furniture is ordered.

Restrooms

Where toilet rooms are provided, each one must be accessible (section 213.2), with narrow exceptions. One of them covers single-user toilet rooms clustered in one location. The clustered single-user-room exception applies separately to each use at each cluster; calculate and round required rooms under the Standards (section 213.2, with rounding under section 104.2). A small restaurant with one customer restroom needs that room fully accessible, with turning space, grab bars, a toilet at the right height and position, and a lavatory no higher than 34 inches (section 606.3). On our Dunkin’ in Hillsborough Township, the plan review comments set exactly that 34 inch maximum for the sink.

How many restrooms you need, and where they can go, is a separate plumbing code question, driven by your occupant load and the plumbing code your state adopted. Health inspectors check restrooms too, so the room has to satisfy all three reviews at once.

Parking and the route to the door

If the restaurant has its own parking, it needs accessible spaces in the number set by Table 208.2 of the Standards, and an accessible route from them to the entrance. Local reviewers often care where those spaces sit relative to the door, so we show them on the site plan from the first submission.

Existing spaces: what you have to fix

This is the question owners ask most, and it has three separate parts under the federal rules.

  • What you alter must comply. Any element or space you alter has to meet the Standards for that work.
  • Path of travel. When you alter an area containing a primary function, such as the dining area, the Title III rules in 28 CFR 36.403 also require the path of travel to it, and the restrooms, phones and drinking fountains serving it, to be made accessible. That extra work is considered disproportionate when it costs more than 20 percent of the alteration. Above that cap, you still spend up to it, in priority order: entrance first, then the route, then restrooms. A series of small alterations cannot be used to dodge the rule.
  • Barrier removal. Separately, the ADA’s Title III requires businesses to remove architectural barriers in existing facilities where that is readily achievable, meaning easy to do without much difficulty or expense for that business. This duty applies even when you are not renovating.

State building codes add their own triggers for existing buildings, so scope is a design decision. A small added piece of work can push a project into a larger upgrade, and the reverse is also true. We confirm the state’s rule for each address before the scope is fixed.

The most expensive surprise in a second-generation space, one that was already a restaurant, is an existing step at the door or a restroom too small to fix in place. We look for both during the site survey, before the layout is drawn. See remodeling an existing restaurant for the other code upgrades a remodel can trigger.

Questions

Common Questions

Does a small restaurant have to be ADA compliant?

Yes. The ADA applies to restaurants of every size, and the building code's accessibility rules apply to any space you build or alter. Size changes how you comply and what counts as readily achievable, not whether the law applies.

Do I have to fix the whole space if I only renovate part of it?

Not necessarily. The part you alter must comply. If it is a main customer area, the federal rules also require an accessible path to it, including the entrance and the restrooms serving it. If full path-of-travel compliance would cost more than 20 percent of the primary-function alteration, provide improvements up to that limit in the required priority order. State building codes can set their own triggers.

Can my restaurant have only high-top tables?

No. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, at least 5 percent of the seating and standing spaces at dining surfaces must be at an accessible height with knee space, spread through the dining area, so a room of only high-tops or communal benches will not comply.

Does the back door have to be accessible?

Usually not as a public entrance, if only staff use it. The 2010 Standards require at least 60 percent of public entrances to be accessible, and employee work areas must still let a person using a wheelchair approach, enter and exit them.
