How Do You Get a Certificate of Occupancy for a Restaurant?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A certificate of occupancy is the building department's approval that a finished space may be occupied for its approved use. Which inspections and sign-offs it depends on varies by jurisdiction, but it usually follows the trade finals and any fire or zoning sign-offs the office requires. It is separate from the health department's permit to operate and from a business license, so a restaurant typically needs both building closeout and permission to operate before it opens.

A certificate of occupancy, or CO, is the building department’s approval that your finished space may be occupied for its approved use. It closes out the building side of the job. What it depends on varies by place, but it usually follows the trade final inspections and whatever fire, zoning or other sign-offs the office requires.

It is not the only thing you need to open. Confirm the health authority’s authorization-to-open process and inspection timing; building closeout alone does not authorize food service. New York City, for example, lets a new operator open after a waiting period (see our New York City permit guide). Some cities issue the business license only after the certificate. So think of closeout as two tracks: building closeout, which ends in the certificate or its local equivalent, and permission to operate, which ends in the health permit and licenses. This guide covers both, and how to finish without a surprise at the end. For the architect’s role during construction, see what your architect does during construction.

CO, temporary CO and completion documents

The names vary, but the documents fall into three groups.

  • Certificate of occupancy. Approves occupancy for the approved use. New buildings need one. In New York City, existing buildings need a new or amended CO when there is a change in use, egress or type of occupancy.
  • Temporary certificate of occupancy (TCO). The model building code lets the building official issue a temporary certificate before all permitted work is complete, where the occupied portion can be used safely, and requires the official to set how long it is valid (2021 International Building Code, section 111.3). Conditions, extensions and fees are set locally, so ask your office.
  • Completion documents for smaller work. New York City issues a letter of completion for minor alterations that do not need a new or amended CO, once all permits on the job are inspected and signed off. New Jersey closes smaller alterations with a certificate of approval (see opening a restaurant in New Jersey).

Local names differ too. Prince George’s County, Maryland issues a use and occupancy permit. Fairfax County, Virginia calls its version a certificate of occupancy or Non-Residential Use Permit, required for a new structure or a change in use; the county fire marshal also inspects on a change in owner or tenant (see opening a restaurant in Virginia). Ask what your office calls its closeout document at the start of the job, not at the end.

Why a former store may need a new certificate

Under the International Building Code, which most states adopt with amendments, a restaurant is usually Group A-2, the assembly class for eating and drinking. Small ones can be classified differently. The 2021 code’s section 303.1.1 classifies an assembly building or tenant space with an occupant load under 50 as Group B, the business class. A small northern Virginia counter-service fit-out we designed, with 14 seats, was classified that way.

Either way, a former shop, bank or phone store is usually a different class or use, so converting it is a change of occupancy or use that often needs a new or amended certificate. The certificate may list the use and the occupant load, so the drawings, the posted occupancy sign and the room as built have to agree. See taking over a former restaurant versus converting retail.

Permission to operate

Building closeout does not license a restaurant to serve food. The health department usually reviews the plans first, sometimes with conditions from plan review to clear later (see health department plan review). Confirm the health authority’s authorization-to-open process and inspection timing; building closeout alone does not authorize food service. Confirm the health office’s payment and inspection deadlines at application. Business licenses, and any liquor license, follow their own processes, and in some cities the business license waits for the certificate.

What holds a certificate up

These are the usual open items at the end of a job.

  • Field changes never filed. A change made on site has to reach the building department, not only the contractor. Inspectors check against the approved set.
  • Open applications or violations. In New York City, a CO cannot be obtained while there are open applications or violations on the property, including ones older than your lease. Search the property’s record before signing.
  • Accessibility items built differently from the drawings, such as a restroom or counter. See accessibility in small restaurants.
  • Base building not finished. In new developments, the landlord’s building needs its own approvals before a tenant fit-out can proceed or close.
  • Missing paperwork at the final. In New York City, Erik Liepins, RA, our Principal Architect, advises keeping a new rooftop unit’s certificate of compliance on site, because the fire department may ask for it.

Letters for landlords and lenders

Landlords, lenders and their attorneys often want proof the work is complete. Approval of a permit is not the same as completion. The completion document comes after the work is inspected and signed off. Tell your landlord which document your office actually issues, and when, so the lease milestones match it.

A closeout checklist

  • Ask the office at the start what it issues at the end and what it needs.
  • Track each fire, health, zoning and agency sign-off as its own line.
  • File every field change with the building department.
  • Search the property for open violations and old permits early.
  • Keep approvals and equipment certificates on site for inspectors.
  • Line up the health permit, business license and landlord letters behind the certificate.

Questions

Common Questions

Do I need a new certificate of occupancy for a restaurant in a former retail space?

Often yes. A change of use or occupancy usually needs a new or amended certificate, because retail and restaurants are classified differently under the building code. A fit-out inside a former restaurant may only need its permits closed out, depending on the jurisdiction.

What is a temporary certificate of occupancy?

A time-limited approval to occupy a space before all permitted work is finished, where the occupied part can be used safely. The building official decides whether to grant one, sets how long it lasts and any conditions; whether and how it can be extended depends on local rules.

What is a letter of completion?

In New York City, it is the document that can close out minor alterations that do not need a new or amended certificate of occupancy, once all permits on the job are inspected and signed off. Other places use their own names for similar closeout documents.

Can I open once the building inspector signs off?

Not always. Confirm the health authority's authorization-to-open process and inspection timing; building closeout alone does not authorize food service. The fire side may have separate finals, and some cities issue the business license only after the certificate of occupancy.
