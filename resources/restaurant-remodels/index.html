Remodeling a Restaurant: What Triggers Code Upgrades

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant remodel is reviewed by what it changes. Like-for-like replacement and finishes may need little or no permit, while reconfigured space, new systems and new equipment usually need drawings, and exterior or site changes can bring in zoning. Altering customer areas also brings federal accessibility duties for the path to them, up to a cost limit, so define the scope item by item before design.

A restaurant remodel is reviewed by what it changes, not by what it is called. Replacing finishes or swapping equipment like for like may need little or no permit. Reconfiguring space, adding equipment or changing systems usually needs drawings and a permit. Exterior and site changes, such as a new menu board, striping or an addition, can bring in zoning or the planning board. And altering the customer areas brings accessibility duties for the route to them.

The scope is a decision you make, and it decides what the remodel triggers. More than 150 of our Dunkin’ projects are remodels, and we do the same work for Taco Bell and other brands. If you are turning a non-restaurant space into a restaurant, that is a change of use, not a remodel; see our change of use guide.

Start with a defined scope

Most brand remodels mix several kinds of work: a new counter, case or kitchen line, new finishes and lighting, a new facade and colors, new signs, equipment changes and sometimes dining room and drive-thru rework. Each part can have a different reviewer.

On one New Jersey drive-thru remodel, the town said the new menu board, parking lot striping and signs needed planning or zoning approval, while the interior sink, counter and painting needed no building permit. The visible exterior work is often what sends a remodel to a board.

Write the scope down item by item before design starts. It is the basis for every permit decision that follows.

How existing-building codes sort the work

Many states adopt the International Existing Building Code for work on existing buildings; some use their own rehabilitation code. Its idea is simple: the more you change, the more has to meet current standards. The City of Houston’s summary of the 2021 edition sets out the categories:

  • Repairs. Fixing or renewing what is there for maintenance or to correct damage.
  • Level 1 alterations. Replacing or covering existing materials, equipment or fixtures with new ones for the same purpose, with no reconfiguration of space.
  • Level 2 alterations. Reconfiguring space, adding or removing doors or windows, extending systems or adding equipment, over up to half the building area.
  • Level 3 alterations. Work covering more than half the building area.
  • Change of occupancy. A change of use the code treats as significant.
  • Additions. Making the building bigger.

Each level carries more requirements than the one before. Which edition and which method apply depends on your state and city, so the first question on any remodel is which code governs your address.

Common triggers, and what is often exempt

The direct answer: like-for-like work is the most likely to be exempt, and anything that reconfigures, extends or adds is the most likely to trigger upgrades.

Often exempt or light review (confirm locally):

  • Painting, finishes and like-for-like replacement of fixtures or equipment
  • Furniture and decor that do not change exits or seating counts much

Common triggers:

  • Accessibility. Under the federal ADA rules in 28 CFR 36.403, altering a primary function area, such as the dining room or ordering area, means making the path of travel to it accessible, including the restrooms serving it, unless the cost is disproportionate, defined as more than 20 percent of the alteration’s cost. Separately, the Justice Department’s Title III guidance describes an ongoing duty for existing businesses to remove barriers where readily achievable. State and local codes add their own rules. Our accessibility guide explains more.
  • Fire protection. Adding seats, enclosing space or changing the use can change the sprinkler and fire alarm coverage required. The thresholds vary by code and jurisdiction, so check early.
  • Mechanical work. A new hood or ductwork is reviewed like new construction. Fire officials may also ask for an air balancing report comparing design airflow with measured conditions before closing the permit, so include the balancing contractor in the plan.
  • Energy rules. Replacing lighting or rooftop equipment can bring current energy code requirements for that equipment.

Equipment changes ripple

Brand remodels often change the equipment lineup, and new equipment rarely drops into old connections. Self-order kiosks, new beverage systems and new cases each need power, water or drains in the right place. On one Tennessee remodel, new back-of-house power ran in surface-mounted conduit so the walls did not have to be opened. Our guide to kitchen engineering covers the systems behind them.

The exterior and the site

Exterior work is where remodels most often meet a board.

  • A new brand image. On our Taco Bell remodel in Ridge, New York, the brand’s newer image carried site requirements the older one did not, such as a digital drive-thru menu board, and a change of image after approval went back to the brand for review.
  • Signs. New signs, a replaced monument sign or a new sign band usually need a sign permit. At our Staten Island Dunkin’ inside a convenience store, the remodel included a new monolith sign and an updated sign band.
  • New openings. We added a walk-up window at our Throggs Neck Dunkin’ in the Bronx. A new opening is a storefront change, and in a historic district it may need preservation review.
  • Additions. In one Westchester County city, adding a walk-in cooler to the outside of a building required planning board approval, a full extra review before the building permit. Additions also have to respect setbacks, which older buildings sometimes already break.

See our signage guide and drive-thru guide for those reviews.

Staying open during the work

Many owners want to keep trading through a remodel. It can often be done, but it has to be planned into the drawings and the contract, not improvised on site.

  • Phase the work. Decide which areas close in which order, and show the phases on the drawings if the building department asks.
  • Keep exits, restrooms and fire protection working for whatever part stays open. A sprinkler or alarm shutdown usually has to be coordinated with the fire department.
  • Plan the kitchen. Decide whether the line keeps cooking, moves to temporary equipment or the store closes for the kitchen phase.
  • Separate customers from work with barriers, dust control and a safe route to the counter or drive-thru.
  • Ask the health department whether it allows food service during the work you plan.

Agree the phasing with the contractor, the brand and the landlord before construction starts. Our permit timeline guide shows where those decisions fall.

The landlord and the existing use

If you lease, the landlord reviews the remodel too and may ask for a different level of drawing detail than the town. Ask early what the landlord needs so the drawing scope is agreed in the proposal.

Check that the restaurant use itself is still valid. On one Maryland remodel, the county planning agency asked for proof the restaurant had operated without a long closure, because the use predated the current zoning. Our change of use guide explains why that matters.

Surveys first

Every remodel starts with a survey visit. We measure and photograph the store and note the equipment, electrical panels and rooftop units, because those are what remodels most often reuse or replace. See why every project starts with a site survey.

Questions

Common Questions

Does repainting and new finishes in a restaurant need a building permit?
Often not on its own, but it varies by town. On one New Jersey drive-thru remodel, the interior sink, counter and painting needed no building permit, while the new menu board, parking lot striping and signs needed planning or zoning approval.
Will remodeling my restaurant force me to make it fully accessible?
Not necessarily fully, but under the federal ADA rules, altering a main customer area means making the path to it accessible, including the restrooms serving it, unless that cost exceeds 20 percent of the alteration. State and local codes can add their own rules.
Can I replace kitchen equipment without new drawings?
A like-for-like swap sometimes can, if the new unit uses the same power, gas, drains and exhaust. New equipment often changes one of those, and then the change needs drawings and usually a permit.
Can my restaurant stay open during a remodel?
Often, if the work is phased so exits, restrooms, fire protection and the kitchen stay usable for the open part. Agree the phases with the contractor, the brand and, where required, the building department before construction starts.
