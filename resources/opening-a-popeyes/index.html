Opening a Popeyes: Design, Permits and Build-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A new Popeyes is shaped by two things: its format, which ranges from a freestanding drive-thru to a multi-level city store or a food court counter, and its frying kitchen, which drives the hood, make-up air, grease and fire suppression design. Settle both early, because they decide which approvals the store needs and how long the engineering takes.

A new Popeyes comes down to two decisions: what kind of building it goes in, and how its frying kitchen will be ventilated, protected and drained. The format decides which approvals the store needs. A freestanding drive-thru goes through zoning and site plan review, a city storefront goes through the building owner and the building department, and a mall or outlet unit goes through the landlord first. The kitchen decides most of the engineering, because a fryer line needs a hood, fire suppression, make-up air and grease control that a lighter menu does not.

This guide covers what is particular to the brand’s design, permits and build-out. For the brand’s review steps, which follow the same broad pattern as other franchisors, see how franchise brand approvals work. For investment figures, read Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document, the estimated initial investment table required by the FTC Franchise Rule; the FTC’s guide to buying a franchise explains how to read it.

The formats

Popeyes builds across a wide range of sites, and the same menu has to work in all of them.

  • Freestanding with a drive-thru. Our Meriden, Connecticut store has a brick tower entrance and twin drive-thru order canopies in the lot. Our Paramus, New Jersey store is a ground-up building on a highway with the brand’s white and brick exterior, teal awnings and a pole sign.
  • Corner lots. Our Staten Island store on Page Avenue sits on its own corner lot, wrapped in brick and floor-to-ceiling glass under a teal steel canopy, with sixteen seats and a separate nook that keeps mobile orders away from the ordering line.
  • City storefronts. Our Greenpoint, Brooklyn store runs a long dining room back from the windows. In the East Village, our 14th Street store seats eight and adds a second pickup counter in a back hallway.
  • More than one floor. Narrow Manhattan lots push dining upstairs. Our Greenwich Village store spans a cellar, two floors and a penthouse, with a diamond-shaped window rising two stories at the entrance. Our store near Union Square splits ordering and dining across two levels with seating for 37. Our Times Square store sits under the district’s billboards.
  • Food courts and outlets. Our Bridgewater, New Jersey food court unit puts a row of self-order kiosks beside the counter in front of shared seating. See our guide to malls and transit hubs for how landlord review works there.

The brand’s look

Our recent freestanding Popeyes stores carry the brand’s exterior of brick lower walls, teal trim and accents, wood siding and orange details. Our Albany, New York store shows it on a freestanding building: brick below, teal trim over the windows, a band of vertical wood siding around the corner and an orange entry door. Inside, the brand leans on its New Orleans story, with Louisiana graphics, wood-front counters and self-order kiosks. Brand packages change, so confirm which version is approved for your location before design starts.

On a freestanding store, that look has to clear the town as well as the brand. Brick, canopies and signs on several faces can all run into local design rules and sign limits. We check the sign code and any design standards before the brand locks the elevations, so the exterior the brand approves is one the town can approve too. Our signage guide covers how those approvals work.

The frying kitchen

Popeyes is a frying and breading operation, and that shapes the back of the house.

  • The hood. Every fryer sits under a commercial exhaust hood with its own fire suppression system. The hood connects by duct to an exhaust fan, usually on the roof. In a multi-story city building, that duct may have to run up through floors above you, which is a question for the building owner before you sign a lease.
  • Make-up air. The hood removes a lot of air, and it has to be replaced. The International Mechanical Code (2021 edition, section 508.1) calls for make-up air roughly equal to the exhaust from all sources in the building. That is a balance for the whole store, not always a dedicated unit for the hood, so the engineer sizes the rooftop units, the exhaust fan and any make-up air unit together.
  • Phased approvals. In some jurisdictions, part of the design, such as the mechanical drawings, can go in as a deferred submittal, a portion approved after the main permit, so demolition can start sooner. Ask the building department early whether it allows this, and plan the engineering schedule around its answer.
  • Grease. Frying produces grease that must be kept out of the sewer. Freestanding stores often use an in-ground interceptor outside the building, and in older buildings it may have to go in a cellar. Local sewer authorities often set their own rules.
  • The breading and batter line. Health reviewers check this equipment like any other, so include the spec sheets for the batter station’s chiller and dispenser with the rest of the package.

Our guides to kitchen engineering and health department plan review go into each of these in more depth.

Pickup and the dining room

Like most quick-service brands, Popeyes now designs for customers who never sit down. Kiosks, a mobile pickup shelf away from the line, and a drive-thru that does not block parking all help. On the Page Avenue store, the separate pickup nook keeps app orders out of the ordering line. In Bridgewater, the kiosks take the order load off the counter in a busy food court. A small dining room still has to meet accessibility rules for seating, the counter and the restroom, which our accessibility guide explains.

The permit path

Popeyes stores go through the same local steps as any restaurant: zoning, any planning board review for a new or changed site, the building permit, health review and inspections. What changes from store to store is the order and how many of those apply. A drive-thru on a new pad may need site plan approval and sign variances before a building permit can be filed. A second-generation storefront, one that was already a restaurant, may not need a new zoning approval, but confirm that with the zoning office rather than assuming it, and expect questions about reusing the old hood and grease trap. Our guide to second-generation spaces covers that choice, and our permit timeline guide explains what sets the pace.

Questions

Common Questions

What makes a Popeyes kitchen different from a coffee or sandwich shop?

Frying. A fryer line needs a commercial exhaust hood with its own fire suppression, make-up air to replace what the hood removes, and grease control. Those systems need roof, duct and sewer answers that a coffee shop rarely does.

Can a Popeyes work on more than one floor?

Yes. Our Greenwich Village Popeyes spans a cellar, two floors and a penthouse, and our store near Union Square orders on one level and seats guests on another. Each added level brings its own exit, accessibility and fire questions.

Can I reuse the hood and grease trap in a former restaurant?

Sometimes. The existing hood, suppression system, exhaust fan and grease interceptor have to suit a fryer line and meet the code in force today. Have them inspected and checked against the brand's equipment list before you count on reusing them.

What should be in the health department package for a Popeyes?

The menu, the equipment schedule and the manufacturer's spec sheet for every piece of equipment, including the breading and batter station. A complete package the first time is the simplest way to avoid a round of comments.
