Permits by State guide

Opening a Restaurant in Tennessee: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In Tennessee, building plans for a restaurant are reviewed by the local code office if the State Fire Marshal's Office has approved that city or county as an exempt jurisdiction, and otherwise by the state when the building meets its submittal triggers. The 2021 building and fire codes apply statewide. Kitchen plans go to the Tennessee Department of Health or, in Davidson, Hamilton, Knox, Madison and Shelby counties, the county health department. Grease programs and contractor licensing add their own steps depending on the city and the scope.

In Tennessee, who reviews your restaurant’s building plans depends on whether your city or county is an exempt jurisdiction, meaning one the state lets review its own plans. The State Fire Marshal’s Office keeps the list. In those places the local code office reviews and inspects. Elsewhere, the state reviews buildings that meet its submittal triggers. Kitchen plans follow a separate map: the state health department in most counties, a county health department in five. Grease programs, landlords and brands can add steps, so the full list of approvals depends on the address and the scope.

Our work there runs from the two-story Taco Bell Cantina in Nashville, built around a live music stage, to a tenant fit-out in Memphis and a two-restaurant building in Clarksville. The Memphis and Clarksville details below are local examples, not a statewide sequence.

Step 1: Find the right offices

Start with the parcel, because three different maps decide who reviews what.

Building and fire. The State Fire Marshal’s Office enforces the statewide building and fire codes. Since April 17, 2025 those are the 2021 International Building Code and 2021 International Fire Code, with state amendments. An exempt jurisdiction reviews and inspects most buildings itself, and must keep its adopted codes within seven years of the latest edition. Memphis, Shelby County, Clarksville, and Nashville and Davidson County are on the exempt list, though some small cities inside those counties are carved out. Outside exempt areas, the state’s plans submittal rules decide whether the state must review, by occupancy and size. For assembly spaces, the trigger is a calculated occupant load of 300 or more. Ask the local office what it reviews too.

Health. The Tennessee Department of Health permits food service establishments in most of the state. Davidson, Hamilton, Knox, Madison and Shelby counties run their own food programs, so a Memphis kitchen goes to the Shelby County Health Department, not the state.

Grease. Some cities run their own fats, oils and grease programs. Memphis’s FOG program monitors more than 3,000 food establishments, separate from both the building permit and the health review.

Step 2: Who can seal the drawings

For a sit-down restaurant, the drawings need a Tennessee registrant. The state board’s seal exemptions clarification says only an architect or engineer registered in Tennessee may prepare design documents for assembly occupancies, the code’s class for restaurants. Small takeout spaces can fall in other code classes, where the board lists some exemptions. Our guide to whether you need an architect covers those.

Step 3: The building permit, with Memphis as an example

The filing depends heavily on the city. In Memphis, applications go through Develop901, the online portal for the Memphis and Shelby County code office. It serves Memphis, Arlington, Germantown, Lakeland, Millington and unincorporated Shelby County. Projects in Bartlett and Collierville cannot be filed there.

On our Memphis fit-out, we filed as a commercial alteration. Review did not start until the permit fee was paid, so the owner should be ready to pay the day the application goes in. The reviewer also asked for items only the contractor could supply. When plumbing comments came back, we revised the set and Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, released it for resubmission through the same portal. Our guide to answering plan review comments covers rounds like that.

Step 4: Line up the contractor early

In Tennessee the general contractor is often part of the filing, so choose one before the drawings are final. Use this checklist:

  • State license. Tennessee requires a contractor’s license before bidding or pricing projects of $25,000 or more. Confirm the license and its monetary limit cover your job.
  • Portal account. In Clarksville we saw the contractor create its own account on the city portal and pull the permit itself. Ask whether your city works that way.
  • Local registration and paperwork. In Memphis we saw the reviewer ask for a contract valuation letter on the contractor’s letterhead, matching the application, and the contractor’s local registration.
  • Trade data. Grease applications may need operating figures and a contractor signature (Step 6).

Our guide to who does what on a restaurant project explains how these roles split.

Step 5: Health review

Tennessee law requires food service plans to be approved before construction, remodeling or conversion begins (Tenn. Code Ann. 68-14-718). The plans must show the layout, equipment and finishes.

The application can ask for more than drawings. On our Memphis fit-out, we sent the Shelby County Health Department the sealed construction set and the proposed menu, with the square footage and seat counts. The department then told us plans and menu had to be delivered on paper at its office, so we arranged printed sets. Ask each health office how it accepts plans before you assume a portal will do. File health review alongside the building application. Our health department plan review guide covers the full packet.

Step 6: Grease programs and interceptors

Grease rules are local, so ask the city early what it needs.

In Memphis, the grease program had its own application. We filled it in as far as the drawings allowed, but it also asked for operating data: daily wastewater flow, prep hours, staff count, expected grease per day, construction dates and a contractor signature.

In Clarksville, the city let two restaurants share the building’s existing grease interceptor, the tank that keeps fats out of the sewer. Final approval depended on an approved grease hauler completing a certification form for the city. If you plan to reuse an interceptor, book the hauler early. Our guide to kitchen hoods, grease and gas covers sizing and placement.

Landlords and brands

In a shopping center, what the landlord provides decides what your drawings cover. On our Memphis fit-out, the landlord agreed to provide the electrical service, new heating and cooling, water and the restrooms, and the space was split off from a larger unit during early design. Get that scope in writing before design starts.

Brands review their own pieces too. In Clarksville, the brand’s store planning team asked us to confirm the curbing and drive-thru layout matched what it had approved, and to send any change back for review.

Inspections and opening

The office that issued the permit inspects each trade and issues the certificate of occupancy after final inspections pass. The health department inspects the finished kitchen before issuing the food service permit, and a grease program may need its own sign-off. Ask each office at the start what it needs to see at the end.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Check the exempt list to see whether your city or county reviews plans itself, and whether your county runs its own health department.
  • Choose a licensed general contractor early, and ask the city whether the contractor must pull the permit.
  • Ask the health office how it accepts plans, and file alongside the building application.
  • Ask whether the city runs a grease program, and gather the operating data it will want.
  • In a shopping center, get the landlord’s scope in writing.

Questions

Common Questions

Who reviews restaurant building plans in Tennessee?
It depends on the address. Cities and counties that the State Fire Marshal's Office has approved as exempt jurisdictions review plans and inspect through their own code offices. Elsewhere, the state reviews buildings that meet its submittal triggers, such as assembly spaces with a calculated occupant load of 300 or more, so confirm the route for your address.
Who reviews restaurant kitchen plans in Tennessee?
State law requires food service plans to be approved before construction starts. The Tennessee Department of Health handles most of the state, while Davidson, Hamilton, Knox, Madison and Shelby counties run their own food programs.
Does the general contractor need to be lined up before the permit in Tennessee?
Often, yes. A state contractor license is needed for projects of $25,000 or more, and some cities ask the contractor to pull the permit or supply documents during review. Choosing the contractor before the drawings are final keeps the permit from waiting on that decision.
Can two restaurants share a grease interceptor in Tennessee?
Sometimes. The local sewer or grease program decides. In Clarksville we saw the city allow two restaurants in one building to share an existing interceptor, with final approval tied to a certification form from an approved grease hauler.
