Opening a Buffalo Wild Wings GO: Design, Permits and Build-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Buffalo Wild Wings GO is the brand's takeout and delivery format, usually an inline storefront with little or no seating and a full cooking line under a commercial hood. Opening one means finding a space that can take the hood, exhaust, power and gas, adapting the brand's versioned prototype to it, and running building, health and fire reviews side by side.

Buffalo Wild Wings GO is the takeout and delivery version of Buffalo Wild Wings: a small storefront built around ordering, cooking and handing off food, with little or no seating. To open one you need a space that can take a full cooking line under a commercial hood, the brand’s approval of a layout drawn from its prototype, and building, health and fire approvals from the authorities where the store sits.

We have worked on GO stores since 2023, in New York City and upstate New York and on sites in Pennsylvania, Virginia, West Virginia and Texas. This guide covers design, permits and build-out. For investment figures, read Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document, the estimated initial investment table required by the FTC Franchise Rule; the FTC’s guide to buying a franchise explains how to read it.

What the format is

A GO store is a pickup and delivery machine. The layout puts the order point near the door, the kitchen behind it, and a clear handoff for drivers and mobile orders. Our published stores show the range:

  • East Village, Manhattan. One of the first GO locations in Manhattan, in a narrow inline storefront on First Avenue. A wall of numbered cubbies past the register separates mobile and delivery pickup from the ordering line, so people collecting orders never cross the people waiting to order.
  • Park Avenue South, Manhattan. Another narrow space, running from the order counter past a sauce bar and drinks cooler to a small seating area at the back.
  • Schenectady, New York. Arranged for takeout and delivery, with limited customer seating.

The common thread is that the room is planned around flow, not seats. Our guide to pickup and delivery design covers that layout logic in more depth.

Choosing the space

Our GO projects have been in two kinds of space: city storefronts and inline units in shopping centers. In West Virginia, a Dunkin’ we designed in a Charles Town shopping center sits right beside a Buffalo Wild Wings GO.

Because the menu is cooked to order, the space needs more infrastructure than its size suggests. Before the lease, check:

  • A hood and exhaust route. The cooking line needs a commercial hood with fire suppression, a duct to the roof and make-up air. In a city storefront the duct route can decide whether the site works.
  • Electric service. A small store can still need a new panel. Expect the plan reviewer to ask for a service and feeder load calculation showing the existing service can carry the new equipment.
  • Gas. Natural gas is not always at the unit. Some shopping center sites run on propane from a tank behind the building, and the drawings then have to state the fuel type and show the gas piping sized for it.
  • Grease. Expect the local sewer authority to have a view on the interceptor. See kitchen engineering for how that varies.
  • Water pressure. Reviewers can ask for a calculation showing enough pressure at the fixtures.
  • Roof structure. New rooftop units and exhaust fans need the roof checked. Where the structure is hidden behind finishes, plan to verify it once demolition opens the ceiling (see rooftop units and structure).

Our lease checklist lists the building facts to confirm before signing.

The prototype and brand approval

The brand issues its GO prototype in numbered versions, and each location is drawn to the version approved for it. A newer release does not automatically replace the one a project started on, and switching midway means redrawing. Brand review then runs in stages, from test fit to final release (see how franchise brand approvals work).

The prototype assumes an ideal box. Real spaces have columns, soffits, old walls and a previous tenant’s leftovers, so every site starts with a survey, and the early plans are checked against the field before the set goes out. Small corrections caught at that stage are what keep plan review short. Our survey guide explains the visit.

What plan reviewers ask about

A small store does not mean a short review. The infrastructure items above are the usual subjects, along with two more:

  • The counter. Reviewers ask to see the accessible portion of the transaction counter, so include a section through it. The 2010 ADA Standards (section 904.4) set its length and height, with different rules for parallel and forward approaches.
  • Who handles life safety. Reviewers may ask how the fire and food safety approvals are being handled, especially where another agency reviews them.

Most of these are answered by putting the right information on the right sheet the first time. Our guide to answering plan review comments covers the response process.

Three reviews at once

Building, health and fire are usually separate reviews, and filing them together keeps one from waiting on another. In West Virginia, the county reviews the building permit, the county health department reviews the food establishment, and the State Fire Marshal’s plans review division reviews fire and life safety plans, including hood suppression. Our West Virginia guide covers that sequence. In Northern Virginia, the county building permit and the health department’s food establishment application are separate applications through the county’s systems.

The operator’s information is often what holds the health review: menu, equipment schedule and food handling details. Gather those while the drawings are being finished.

Seating, occupant load and exits

One advantage of a pickup format is code simplicity, but only if the numbers work. Under the International Building Code (2021 edition, sections 1004 and 1006), the occupant load, the number of people the code assumes are inside, comes from the floor area of each part of the store divided by the code’s factor for that use. Seats are only part of it. A space can have a single exit only when that load and the travel distance to the door both stay within the code’s limits, and local amendments can change those limits. Adding seats raises the load and can change exits, restrooms and accessibility requirements, so decide on seating before the layout is fixed.

Questions

Common Questions

How big is a Buffalo Wild Wings GO?

It is a small-footprint format built for pickup and delivery, so it fits inline storefronts that would be too small for a full dining restaurant. The brand sets its own size range; the test fit tells you whether a specific unit works.

Does a Buffalo Wild Wings GO need seating?

Not necessarily. One of our Manhattan stores has a small seating area at the back, and others have little or none. Fewer seats can keep the occupant load low, but the number of exits depends on the calculated load of the whole store and the travel distance to the door, not on seating alone.

Can a Buffalo Wild Wings GO go in a former retail space?

Often, but a retail space usually lacks a hood, grease interceptor and the electric and gas service a cooking line needs. Those are added as part of the fit-out, so check the building's capacity before signing the lease.

Which approvals does a Buffalo Wild Wings GO need?

Usually a building permit, a health department plan review and a fire review, plus the brand's own approval. In some states, such as West Virginia, the fire review is done at the state level and runs alongside the others.
