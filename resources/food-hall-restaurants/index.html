Opening a Restaurant Stall in a Food Hall or Food Court

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A food hall or food court stall is a small tenant fit-out inside a building someone else runs. The operator usually provides the shared seating, restrooms and some of the kitchen infrastructure, and reviews your design before the town does; you still need your own building permit work and, in most places, your own health permit. Read the operator's criteria before you draw anything.

A food hall or food court stall is a restaurant built inside a building someone else runs. The operator, usually the landlord or a food hall company, provides the shared seating, the restrooms and often part of the kitchen infrastructure. You design a small counter and kitchen within the operator’s rules, the operator reviews it, and then the town and the health department review it.

That makes a stall cheaper and smaller than a standalone restaurant, but not simpler. The rules come from three places at once: the operator’s criteria, the building code for the whole hall, and the health department’s view of how many food businesses share one roof.

Food hall or food court

Both put several food businesses around shared seating, and both are designed and permitted the same way. A food court is usually a mall’s common dining area with chain counters around it. A food hall is usually run by an operator who curates the vendors and often builds more of the shared kitchen infrastructure.

For you, the differences show up in the lease and the criteria package: who provides what, and how much freedom you have in the storefront. Our closest published work is in mall food courts and transit concourses: a Wendy’s in the Green Acres Mall food court, a Popeyes in a Bridgewater mall food court, a 375º Chicken ‘N Fries on the concourse of Newark’s Gateway Center and two Dunkin’ shops in the Oculus at the World Trade Center.

What the operator provides, and what you build

The lease and the operator’s criteria divide the work, and that split drives your drawings. Ask about each of these before design starts:

  • Seating. Usually the operator’s, in the common area. That is what lets a stall be small.
  • Restrooms. Usually shared and provided by the operator.
  • Grease. Some halls run a shared grease line to a central interceptor and ask each stall to add its own. At one New Jersey mall we worked in, every food tenant needed its own secondary grease interceptor in addition to the center’s system. Our grease trap and interceptor guide explains the two types.
  • Exhaust. Whether there is a shaft or roof route for a hood, and how much cooking it can serve. A stall without a route for a grease hood may be limited to cooking that does not need one.
  • Utilities. Electrical capacity, gas, water and how each is metered.
  • Equipment and finishes. Operators can set standards above the code. In the Oculus, the center required all kitchen equipment to be NSF certified, a food equipment safety standard, or an approved equivalent, and the property’s chief engineer required union electrical labor.

Our mall and transit guide covers landlord review in more depth, and our landlord and tenant work guide explains how a work letter becomes a scope split.

Permits: one building, many stalls

Expect the operator’s review first, then your own permits. At large managed centers, your construction drawings and sign drawings go to the operator, and only after its approval can they go to the building department. On an outlet center we worked on, each contractor then filed its own trade permit under the approved project.

Health permits vary more. Many health departments treat each stall as its own food establishment with its own plan review and permit; others permit a central operator. Some states have written rules for it. North Carolina changed its law in 2015 to allow more than one food establishment permit at the same location, as long as each establishment meets the rules, and its guidance for shared-use kitchens makes the primary operator responsible for shared items such as toilets, garbage and pest control. Ask the local health department how it permits the hall, and what it expects from you versus the operator.

Ownership can change the path too. On a two-brand campus location in Pennsylvania, the university owned the building, so the state ran the building code review instead of the regional code office, and the tenant still applied for its own permits. Our Pennsylvania guide has the details.

Seats and restrooms you share

Shared seating is counted for the whole hall, not stall by stall, so the operator’s code analysis has to cover your customers. Restrooms work the same way. New York State’s building code bulletin, based on the 2015 International Building Code, explains that required toilet facilities can be shared as long as they are no more than one story away and within a 500 foot path of travel, reduced to 300 feet in malls, and that patron restrooms can also serve employees.

What to check:

  • That the hall’s occupant load and restroom count already include seats for your stall.
  • Whether your staff can use the shared restrooms, or need their own.
  • Whether the health department wants a hand sink, mop sink or staff restroom inside your stall regardless.

Small spaces also have code exceptions of their own, as our small-footprint guide explains.

Designing a stall

A stall sells from the counter, so the first few feet do most of the work. Patterns from our food court and concourse stores:

  • Make the counter the storefront. The Green Acres Wendy’s fits into 811 square feet with wood cladding, a geometric backsplash and suspended menu boards, laid out for food court orders and mobile pickup.
  • Use the frontage for ordering. At the Bridgewater Popeyes, an orange “Order here” wall holds a row of self-order kiosks beside the service counter, facing the shared seating.
  • Be visible from both directions. The Oculus Dunkin’ at the World Trade Center is a compact kiosk with no seating and a curved glass front, so commuters see the donut case as they step off the escalator. The Oculus concourse Dunkin’ runs its counter the length of the store with a pickup counter for mobile orders.
  • Keep pickup out of the line. Delivery and app orders need a shelf or counter of their own, even in a small stall. Our pickup and delivery guide covers where it goes.

Questions for the operator

  1. What does the criteria package require, and who reviews my drawings?
  2. Is there a grease line, an exhaust route and enough power and gas for my menu?
  3. Do I need my own interceptor, hood or make-up air?
  4. Do the hall’s seats and restrooms already count my customers?
  5. How does the health department permit vendors here?
  6. What are the rules for signs, finishes, equipment and contractors?

Questions

Common Questions

Does each food hall vendor need its own health permit?

Usually, though it depends on the health department. Many permit each stall as its own food establishment, and some states have written rules for several permits at one location. Ask the local health department how it permits the hall before you sign.

Does a food hall stall need its own restroom?

Usually not, if the hall provides shared restrooms that meet the code for the whole building. Model codes allow shared facilities within a set travel distance, which is shorter in malls. Confirm the hall's restroom count covers your seats.

Who provides the grease interceptor and exhaust in a food hall?

The lease decides. Some operators build shared grease lines and exhaust shafts and ask each stall to connect, sometimes with its own secondary interceptor; others leave it all to the tenant. Get it in writing before design.

How small can a food hall stall be?

Very small if the hall supplies the seating. Our Wendy's in a mall food court is 811 square feet, and our Oculus Dunkin' at the World Trade Center is a compact kiosk with no seating.
