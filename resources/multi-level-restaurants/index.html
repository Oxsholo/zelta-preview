Restaurants on More Than One Floor: What Changes

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Putting a restaurant on more than one level lets a narrow building hold far more seating or storage than its footprint, but every added level brings exits, stairs, an accessibility decision and fire protection into the code analysis. The operational question matters as much as the code one: which equipment and supplies have to stay on the service floor, and what can safely move up or down.

A restaurant on two or more levels is a design answer to an expensive, narrow ground floor. Stack the dining room above or below the counter, or push storage into a cellar, and a small building can hold far more than its footprint suggests. The cost is complexity: every added level brings exits, stairs, an accessibility decision and fire protection into the code analysis, and the kitchen team has to live with stairs every shift.

This guide covers what changes when a restaurant leaves a single floor, drawn from multi-level stores we have designed.

Why go vertical

In dense cities, the ground floor is the most valuable space in the building, and the floors above or below cost less. Ordering usually stays at street level, where customers come in. Dining and storage can move.

The patterns we see:

  • Order downstairs, dine upstairs. Our Taco Bell Cantina in Midtown puts the order counter at street level and the dining room upstairs, wrapping the corner behind full-height windows over Sixth Avenue.
  • Several levels in one narrow building. Our Popeyes in Greenwich Village occupies a cellar, first floor, second floor and penthouse, with an open stair leading to upstairs seating lit by a skylight.
  • Split across two levels. Our Popeyes near Union Square fills a two-level storefront, with seating for 37 spread across both floors and kiosks at the entry.
  • A mezzanine over a double-height room. Our Taco Bell Cantina on Fordham Road in the Bronx has a double-height entry holding the stair, with high tables lining the mezzanine rail above the storefront glass.
  • A cellar for storage only. Our Taco Bell in Lenox Hill pairs a 1,478-square-foot first floor with a 685-square-foot cellar storage area.

Multi-level stores are not only a New York format. Our Taco Bell Cantina in Nashville is a two-story store built around live music.

Exits: each level counts on its own

Each level needs its own way out. Under chapter 10 of the International Building Code, which most states adopt with amendments, the number of exits a level needs depends on its occupant load, the code’s count of people it holds, and on how far people travel to reach an exit. A dining room upstairs with a single stair may work at one seat count and fail at a higher one, so the seat count and the stairs have to be decided together.

Two things owners underestimate:

  • A circulation stair is not always an exit stair. A feature stair that customers use to reach the dining room may or may not count as a required exit. Required exit stairs have code-set widths, enclosure and discharge, and an open stair like the one at our Greenwich Village Popeyes has to be reconciled with the exit design for each level. Sometimes a second, enclosed stair does the exit work.
  • Basement corridors can be exits too. On one Manhattan storefront, drain lines ran low through a basement hallway that served as a means of egress, the code’s term for the exit path. The building code generally requires at least 80 inches of headroom along exit paths, so the lines had to be raised to keep the corridor a legal exit.

In New York City, a larger upstairs dining room can also cross the occupant load that triggers a Place of Assembly certificate, as our NYC permits guide explains.

Accessibility between levels

This is an item that often reshapes a multi-level plan, and it needs a current code review, not an assumption. The 2010 ADA Standards require an accessible route to all dining areas in a restaurant, including raised and sunken areas (section 206.2.5). The exceptions are narrow:

  • Private buildings with fewer than three stories, or less than 3,000 square feet per story, need not connect stories with an accessible route, but the exception does not apply to shopping centers, malls or transit stations (section 206.2.3).
  • In such a building, a mezzanine dining area can go without an accessible route only if it holds less than 25 percent of the total seating and dining area and the accessible area has the same decor and services.
  • In alterations, existing raised or sunken dining areas can be exempt where the same services and decor are available in an accessible space.

State building codes and local rules can require more than the ADA does, and alteration scope matters. Our accessibility guide explains how the scope of work drives the rest. Existing lifts are not automatically usable either: an old chair lift may need to be brought up to current code before it can count. Lifts and elevators take floor space on both levels, so confirm the approach before the layout is fixed.

What goes on which floor

The kitchen team works the service floor, and anything they reach for every few minutes has to be there. Brands and operators are specific about this, and they do not all agree:

  • On one Manhattan Taco Bell, the brand asked that all equipment stay on the first floor and that the basement not be used for any restaurant purpose.
  • On another Manhattan store, when the walk-in cooler moved downstairs, the client team asked for the soda syrup boxes and a two-door reach-in cooler upstairs, so daily items stayed on the service floor.
  • On a Queens store, the brand asked us to plan space for the used fryer oil barrel and said it should not go in the basement. On a Lower Manhattan store, the client team wanted the oil rendering barrel and large trash cans shown in the basement.
  • In older buildings the basement may be where some systems have to go. On our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the grease trap had to go in the building’s cellar.

Ask the brand and the operator early, and in writing, what may live below or above the service floor. A plan that sends daily supplies downstairs looks efficient on paper and costs labor every shift.

Fire protection and construction

More levels usually means more fire protection: sprinklers, alarm coverage on each floor and fire-rated separations around stairs and shafts, depending on the building and the code. The code summary sheet in our drawing sets records the number of stories, whether there is a mezzanine and whether the building is sprinklered, because those answers drive the rest of the analysis.

A mezzanine is worth defining carefully. Chapter 5 of the International Building Code generally limits a mezzanine to one third of the floor area of the room it sits in. Within that limit it is treated as part of the story below. Beyond it, it becomes a separate story with its own requirements.

Kitchen exhaust is the other vertical problem. A hood on the ground floor of a multi-story building has to get its duct to the roof or to an approved discharge point, often through floors that belong to someone else. Plan that route before the lease is signed; our guide to getting kitchen exhaust out of a city storefront compares the options.

Zoning and signs on upper floors

Different floors of the same building can sit under different rules. On one Brooklyn store, the second floor was zoned for offices, and we confirmed that signage could be permitted on that floor with the landlord’s approval. Check the zoning, sign rules and lease terms for each level you plan to use and each facade you plan to sign.

Before you commit to a multi-level space

  • How many seats do you need on each level, and do the stairs and exits support that occupant load?
  • Which accessibility rule applies, and how will customers who cannot use stairs reach the dining areas?
  • What must stay on the service floor, per the brand and the operator?
  • Is any basement corridor an exit route that must stay clear?
  • Where does the kitchen exhaust leave the building?
  • Do upper floors carry different zoning, sign rules or landlord terms?

Questions

Common Questions

Does a second-floor dining room need an elevator?

It depends. The 2010 ADA Standards require an accessible route to all dining areas, with narrow exceptions: some small private buildings need no route between stories, and a mezzanine holding less than 25 percent of the seating can be exempt if the accessible area offers the same decor and services. Shopping centers and malls do not get the story exception. Settle it with a current code review at the test fit, because a lift or elevator takes real floor space.

Can I use the basement of my storefront for restaurant storage?

Usually, but brands and operators differ on what can go down there. One brand asked us to keep all equipment on the first floor and not use the basement at all, while another client team wanted the oil barrel and trash cans shown in the basement. Any basement corridor that serves as an exit must also stay clear.

What is a mezzanine in building code terms?

A mezzanine is an intermediate level open to the room below. Under the International Building Code it is generally limited to one third of the floor area of that room, and within its limits it is treated as part of the story below rather than as a separate story. Outside those limits it becomes a story with its own requirements, so the code analysis has to say clearly whether there is one.
