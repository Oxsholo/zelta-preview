As-Built Drawings and Site Surveys for Restaurants

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Restaurant drawings can only be as accurate as what is known about the space. An existing conditions survey measures and records the real dimensions, structure and building systems before design starts, so the layout, equipment and permit set rest on verified facts rather than on a prototype or an old landlord drawing. What the survey cannot see is recorded as unknown, not assumed.

An existing conditions survey is a measured record of a space as it stands today: its walls, columns, ceiling heights, openings, structure and building systems, drawn as existing conditions drawings before any new design is laid over them. It is different from record drawings, the landlord’s or a previous tenant’s drawings of what was designed or built at some point in the past. Record drawings are useful evidence. The survey is what confirms them.

For an interior fit-out, our design starts with that survey, before any layout is drawn. A kitchen is a tight puzzle of equipment, hood, grease, gas and power, and it has to fit a real room with real columns, real ceiling heights and real building systems. If the base drawing is wrong, every decision drawn on top of it is wrong too. On ground-up projects, the land survey and site information play the same role.

What an existing conditions survey records

For an interior fit-out, the survey captures what is physically there:

  • The shell: wall locations, column positions, ceiling and structure heights, floor level changes, storefront openings and doors.
  • Building systems: the rooftop units, electrical service and panel, water and gas service, sprinkler heads and mains, fire alarm devices, existing hoods and grease interceptors.
  • Access and exits: entrances, exit paths, stairs, lifts and restroom locations.
  • Context: neighboring tenants, shared walls, rear service doors and roof access.

For a freestanding or ground-up project, a land surveyor licensed in that state also prepares a boundary and topographic survey of the lot. Brands often require it. For our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, a freestanding drive-thru, the brand asked for both a survey and a soils report.

How we gather it: measurements, photos and 3D scans

No single source explains every condition, so we use several together:

  • Field measurements give the dimensions we rely on.
  • Photos show detail: equipment nameplates, ceiling conditions, the state of finishes.
  • A 3D scan, such as a Matterport capture, lets the design team walk the space again from anywhere. On recent projects we have converted the scan into a measured drawing, a step called scan to CAD. You can see what a capture looks like on the 3D tour of our Taco Bell Cantina in Nashville.
  • Landlord and base building drawings show what the building was designed to be, and often what services the landlord will provide.

Before relying on any of them, we check that the survey, photos and scan all describe the same address, suite and floor, and that they are current. A scan taken before the last tenant moved out may not show what is there today.

Why a scan does not replace a tape measure

Scans are good evidence, but they are not a final answer. A measurement taken in a scan depends on where the endpoints are placed and on which surfaces are being measured: a finished wall face, a raw opening or a diagonal. We define what is being measured, measure it from more than one viewpoint, and compare it with the field survey.

When two sources disagree, we do not average them or pick the convenient one. We record both measurements, note what decision depends on the difference, and confirm the dimension in the field. In a narrow kitchen line, a few inches can decide whether a piece of equipment fits.

What a survey cannot see

A walk-through survey with tape, camera and scanner is non-destructive: nothing is opened up. That limits what it can confirm. It cannot verify:

  • Services hidden inside walls, above hard ceilings or behind column enclosures.
  • The size and condition of structural members under the roof deck or floor, which often need a structural engineer and sometimes an opening.
  • Whether an existing duct, flue or shaft is rated and suitable for a new use.
  • Underground utilities, buried grease tanks and slab conditions.
  • Whether existing equipment actually works, which needs a contractor or service technician.

Those areas are recorded as unknowns, not assumed empty or adequate, and the existing building is drawn separately from the proposed design. A brand prototype never fills in a gap in the existing conditions. Where an unknown could change the design, we ask for a targeted look, such as lifting ceiling tiles or a contractor’s inspection, before the layout depends on it.

What the survey decides

The survey is where many of a project’s biggest decisions get made:

  • Keep or replace equipment. An existing rooftop unit or walk-in in good condition may be worth keeping; one at the end of its life is not. Our guide to adding rooftop units covers what the roof itself has to carry.
  • Tie into existing systems. An existing fire alarm or sprinkler system may be modified and extended rather than replaced, if the survey shows what is there.
  • Building limits. An existing flue or shaft may already serve other tenants, and building management may not allow another connection. Get the landlord’s position in writing before the exhaust design depends on it.
  • Code triggers. Existing lifts, stairs and restrooms may need upgrades once they are part of the new work. Record them so the code analysis can decide.
  • Zoning limits. At one freestanding site in New Jersey, the existing building already sat closer to the road than the zoning setback allowed, so any addition on the front would have needed a variance.
  • Landlord work versus tenant work. What the landlord will deliver, such as power, heating and cooling or restrooms, changes the scope of the drawings. Our guide to landlord and tenant work covers that split.

Turning unknowns into specific questions

When something cannot be confirmed from the survey, we ask a precise field question. That means marking the room, opening and endpoints on the plan, asking for the specific dimension needed, and asking for a photo that shows both ends of the measurement. When the answer comes back, we record who confirmed it and when, then update every drawing it affects.

A vague question (“can you check the back wall?”) gets a vague answer. A marked-up question gets a number we can build from.

What owners can do to help

You can make the survey stage faster and more complete:

  • Arrange access to the space, the roof and any shared mechanical or electrical rooms.
  • Send the lease exhibits, landlord work letter and any base building or previous tenant drawings.
  • Tell us what the previous tenant left behind, especially hoods, grease interceptors and walk-in coolers.
  • Allow ceiling tiles to be lifted where needed, so concealed conditions are seen before design rather than during construction.

Questions

Common Questions

Is a Matterport scan enough on its own?

No. A 3D scan is excellent for orientation and context, but we use it alongside field measurements, photos and any landlord drawings. Where two sources disagree, we record both and confirm the dimension in the field rather than picking one.

Do I need a land survey for a restaurant fit-out?

Usually not for an interior fit-out in an existing building. Ground-up drive-thrus and projects with site work normally need a boundary and topographic survey prepared by a land surveyor licensed in that state, and brands often ask for a soils report too.

Can we skip the survey if the landlord has drawings?

Landlord drawings are useful, but they show what was designed, not always what was built or what the last tenant changed. We treat them as one source and verify them against the space.
