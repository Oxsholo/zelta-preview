Permits by State guide

Opening a Restaurant in NYC: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In New York City, the Department of Buildings approves the construction drawings and issues the work permit, filed online by a New York licensed architect or engineer with the owner's authorization. Other agencies join depending on the building and the scope: asbestos clearance before the permit, Landmarks review in designated buildings, fire department requirements, separate sign permits and the Health Department permit to operate. Approval of the drawings, the work permit and permission to serve food are three different steps.

In New York City, the Department of Buildings (DOB) approves your restaurant’s construction drawings and issues the work permit. It is the central filing, but it is not the only agency. The Department of Environmental Protection controls asbestos clearance, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews work in designated buildings, the Fire Department covers fire protection systems, and the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene issues the permit that lets you serve food. Which of these apply depends on the building and the scope of work.

It also helps to separate three moments that owners often blur together: DOB approving the drawings, DOB issuing the work permit, and the city allowing you to open. Each one has its own prerequisites. We have designed and permitted restaurants across all five boroughs, from a kiosk in a transit hub to multi-level storefronts in Manhattan, and most of the planning comes down to knowing which agency owns which step.

Start with the building, not the menu

Three questions about the building decide how many agencies you will deal with:

  • Is it landmarked or in a historic district? If so, the Landmarks Preservation Commission is part of your schedule.
  • Who owns it? A private landlord, a public agency or a transit authority each brings its own review on top of the city’s.
  • When was it built, and what was there before? Older buildings need an asbestos assessment before a permit. A former restaurant usually has a head start on exhaust, grease and gas. A former retail store usually does not.

This is also when we survey the space, because a duct route or a cellar stair that does not match the drawings becomes a problem at inspection, not at review. Kitchen exhaust is the biggest question in most city storefronts; our guide to getting kitchen exhaust out of a city storefront covers the route.

The DOB filing and the work permit

The drawings are filed in DOB NOW, the department’s online system, by a New York licensed architect or engineer who is responsible for the code analysis: occupancy, exits, accessibility, plumbing fixtures and the energy code.

Two items have to be in hand before the filing can move:

  1. The owner’s confirmation. The DOB NOW statements and signatures guide says every job filing needs the owner to confirm, in DOB NOW, that the applicant is authorized to file. A tenant cannot supply it. Ask your landlord for it before the drawings are finished.
  2. An asbestos assessment. The DOB asbestos requirements call for a certified asbestos investigator to assess the work area in buildings built before April 1, 1987. Where the work is not an asbestos project, the investigator files an ACP-5 form with DEP. Where it is, abatement filings come first. Newer buildings can use an exemption certification, but known asbestos still has to be abated.

Either one can hold up finished drawings, so we list both on the first day of a New York City job.

After filing, the drawings are either reviewed by a DOB plan examiner or, for eligible jobs, professionally certified by the architect or engineer, which skips plan examination but leaves the job open to audit. Plan examiners issue objections, comments the drawings must answer before approval. Approval is not the permit: the work permit is issued once approval and the remaining prerequisites are in place.

Some rules change the design. Local Laws 92 and 94 of 2019, explained in DOB Bulletin 2019-010, require a sustainable roofing zone, solar panels or a green roof, on new buildings, qualifying additions, and alterations replacing an entire existing roof deck or roof assembly, subject to the law’s exclusions. Replacing only the roof covering does not necessarily trigger it, and most interior fit-outs do not touch the roof at all. We check the roof scope at the start.

Landmarks and historic districts

If the building is designated, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews the work as well as DOB. Its permit types page lists interior work that needs a DOB permit, and interior work that affects the exterior, such as HVAC louvers and vents, among the work that needs a Landmarks permit.

That reaches further than most owners expect. On a Flatiron storefront, adding exhaust louvers to the storefront meant a Landmarks application, because the commission reviews the louver’s style and its effect on the building. On a Sixth Avenue job, the interior work itself went to Landmarks. Design the storefront, signs and any wall or window openings with that review in mind. Our historic buildings guide covers the design side.

Fire department requirements

The Fire Department (FDNY) can be involved wherever the restaurant has a kitchen hood fire suppression system, a fire alarm, sprinklers or rooftop mechanical equipment. Some of this is reviewed with the building filing and some is inspected after you open.

One habit we pass on: when the mechanical work is signed off, keep the certificate of compliance for the rooftop HVAC unit on site. On a Queens job, that was the document we told the operator FDNY would ask for if inspectors stopped in.

Signs are separate permits

Your sign is not covered by the building permit. The DOB sign permit page lists up to three filings for one sign: a sign permit, an alteration permit when the sign hangs from a bracket or structure instead of sitting flat on the wall, and an electrical permit when it is lit. A lit sign that projects past the building line may also need an annual illuminated sign permit. Signs under six square feet that are not lit are exempt.

Timing matters too. On our Brooklyn drive-thru, the sign contractor could file early, but the sign permit would not be issued until the building permit was. Our signage guide covers what the zoning and the landlord allow.

Transit and public property

Spaces owned by a public agency follow that agency’s process as well. In Port Authority property in Lower Manhattan, the agency’s tenant review asked for the brand’s full menu with the permit submission and accepted only digitally signed and sealed drawings. Locations like our Dunkin’ in the Oculus at the World Trade Center show why these spaces are worth it: the foot traffic is enormous. The paperwork is simply longer, and our mall and transit guide covers it.

Opening: the Health Department and assembly spaces

The Health Department issues the food service establishment permit. The city’s business portal says a new operator may open 22 days after applying even without a pre-permit inspection, or sooner by scheduling a pre-operating inspection, and that the department will also inspect after you open. Hand sinks, finishes and equipment need to be right on the drawings so they are right on the day of inspection.

On the building side, the work permits are signed off after final inspections, and a change of use or occupancy can mean a new or amended certificate of occupancy. Our certificate of occupancy guide covers that closeout.

Larger dining rooms add one more step. The Place of Assembly certificate is required where 75 or more members of the public gather indoors, including to eat or drink. Stacked layouts make this a design question first: our Taco Bell Cantina in Midtown puts ordering at street level with dining upstairs, and our Popeyes in Greenwich Village spans a cellar, two floors and a penthouse. Every added level brings exits, stairs, accessibility and fire separation into the code analysis.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Confirm whether the building is landmarked or in a historic district.
  • Ask the landlord who will confirm the DOB NOW filing for the owner, and how quickly.
  • Find out when the building was built and order the asbestos assessment.
  • Find out where kitchen exhaust can go: roof, rear yard or facade.
  • Check what signs the building and zoning allow.
  • If the landlord is a public agency, get its tenant construction requirements in writing.
  • Estimate the occupant load early if the dining room is large.

Questions

Common Questions

Can I file my own restaurant plans with the NYC Department of Buildings?

No. Construction drawings for a commercial restaurant fit-out are filed by a New York licensed architect or professional engineer. The property owner must also confirm the filing in DOB NOW before it moves forward.

Do I need Landmarks approval for a restaurant in a historic building?

If the building is a designated landmark or sits in a historic district, usually yes. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says interior work that needs a Department of Buildings permit, and interior work that shows on the outside, such as louvers and vents, needs a Landmarks permit too.

Do restaurant signs need their own permit in New York City?

Usually. Most signs need a Department of Buildings sign permit, a sign hung from a bracket or structure needs a separate alteration permit, and a lit sign needs an electrical permit. Signs under six square feet that are not lit are exempt.

When can a new NYC restaurant start serving food?

After the construction work is signed off, the Health Department's authorization-to-operate rules are a separate gate. The city's business portal says a new operator may open 22 days after applying, or sooner by scheduling a pre-operating inspection. Larger dining rooms may also need a Place of Assembly certificate.
