Opening a Restaurant in NYC: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

A New York City restaurant needs a Department of Buildings approval and work permit for the construction, filed online by a licensed architect or engineer, plus separate approvals that depend on the space: asbestos clearance, Landmarks review in historic buildings, fire department sign-offs, sign permits and the Health Department permit to operate. Most delays come from items that were not lined up before filing, not from the plan review itself.

To open a restaurant in New York City you need an approved construction filing and work permit from the Department of Buildings (DOB), filed through the city’s online system by a New York licensed architect or engineer. Around that core filing sit several other approvals that depend on the building: an asbestos clearance before work starts, Landmarks review if the building is historic, fire department requirements for kitchen and mechanical equipment, separate sign permits, and the Health Department permit you need before you can serve food.

The plan review is rarely what holds a project up. What holds it up is a missing piece that should have been collected before filing: the landlord’s authorization, an asbestos report, a Landmarks submission nobody planned for. We have worked through these approvals on storefronts across all five boroughs, from a kiosk in a transit hub to multi-level storefronts in Manhattan, and the pattern is consistent.

Start with the building, not the menu

Before drawing anything, find out what the building already is. Three questions decide how complicated your approvals will be:

  • Is it landmarked or in a historic district? If so, the Landmarks Preservation Commission is part of your schedule.
  • Who owns it? A private landlord, a public agency or a transit authority each brings its own review on top of, or in place of, the city’s.
  • What was there before? A space that was already a restaurant usually has a head start on exhaust, grease and gas. A former retail store usually does not.

This is also when we survey the space. Our projects start with a survey of existing conditions, because a duct route or a cellar stair that does not match the drawings becomes a problem at inspection, not at review.

The DOB filing

The construction drawings are filed on DOB NOW, the Department of Buildings’ online filing system. A licensed architect or engineer prepares and seals them and is responsible for the code analysis: occupancy, exits, accessibility, plumbing fixtures and the energy code.

Two things must be in hand before we file:

  1. The owner’s authorization. The property owner signs off on the filing electronically. On a recent Manhattan storefront, the construction documents were finished and sent to both the client and the brand’s corporate team, and the filing still waited on the landlord’s DOB NOW authorization email. Ask your landlord for it early.
  2. An asbestos clearance. The city will not issue a work permit until a certified asbestos investigator has reported on the area being disturbed, usually on a form known as the ACP-5. On that same project the ACP-5 report was the other item standing between finished drawings and a filing.

After filing, the job is either reviewed by a DOB plan examiner or, for qualifying jobs, professionally certified by the architect and subject to audit. Plan examiners issue objections, comments the drawings must answer before approval. Some are simple. Some are real design changes. On a freestanding drive-thru we designed in Brooklyn, DOB objected under the city’s sustainable roof laws (Local Laws 92 and 94 of 2019), which require new roofs to include a solar or green roof zone. We had already sought an exception and been denied, so the roof design had to change. Rules like this apply to new buildings and full roof replacements, not most interior fit-outs, which is exactly why they surprise people.

Landmarks and historic districts

Many Manhattan storefronts sit in landmarked buildings or historic districts. If yours does, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews the work before DOB issues the permit.

What triggers Landmarks review is broader than most owners expect. On a Flatiron storefront, adding exhaust louvers to the storefront meant a Landmarks application, because the commission reviews the louver’s style and its effect on the building. On a Sixth Avenue space, even the interior work had to go to Landmarks for review. Plan for it at the start, and design the storefront, signs and any wall or window openings with that review in mind.

Fire department requirements

The Fire Department (FDNY) is involved wherever your restaurant has a kitchen hood fire suppression system, a fire alarm, sprinklers or rooftop mechanical equipment. Some of this is reviewed with the building filing and some is inspected after you open.

One practical habit we pass on to every owner: when the mechanical work is signed off, print the certificate of compliance for the rooftop or outdoor HVAC equipment and keep it on site. It is the document FDNY inspectors ask for when they stop in.

Signs are a separate permit

Your storefront sign is not covered by the building permit. Depending on size, location and lighting, a sign may need its own DOB sign permit. On a recent Upper East Side storefront, an illuminated blade sign (one that projects from the facade) needed a sign permit, a separate permit for the bracket that holds it, and an electrical permit. On another job, the sign contractor could file for the sign permit early, but the sign permit would not be issued until the building permit was. Build that into your opening date, because a restaurant without its sign is not really open.

Transit and public property

Spaces owned by a public agency follow that agency’s process. In a Port Authority property in Lower Manhattan, the agency’s own tenant construction review asked for a complete brand menu with the permit submission, and for the architect and engineer of record to digitally sign and seal the final documents. On MTA property, the authority required all of its own forms to be updated and resubmitted before the certificate of occupancy. Locations like our Dunkin’ in the Oculus at the World Trade Center show why these spaces are worth it: the foot traffic is enormous. The paperwork is simply longer.

Health Department and opening

The Department of Health and Mental Hygiene issues the food service permit and inspects before you open. The kitchen design, hand sinks, finishes and equipment all need to be right on the drawings so they are right on the day of inspection. Once the construction work is signed off, the remaining items are the final inspections and, where the use or occupancy changes, an updated certificate of occupancy.

Multi-level and larger dining rooms

Stacked restaurants are common in Manhattan because ground floors are narrow and expensive. Our Taco Bell Cantina in Midtown puts ordering at street level with the dining room upstairs, and our Popeyes in Greenwich Village spans a cellar, two floors and a penthouse. Every added level brings exits, stairs, accessibility and fire separation into the code analysis, and larger dining rooms can cross the occupant load that requires a separate Place of Assembly certificate. These are design questions first and permit questions second.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Confirm whether the building is landmarked or in a historic district.
  • Ask the landlord who will provide the owner’s authorization for the DOB filing, and how quickly.
  • Order an asbestos survey of the work area.
  • Find out where kitchen exhaust can go: roof, rear yard or facade.
  • Check what signs the building and zoning allow.
  • If the landlord is a public agency, get its tenant construction requirements in writing.

Questions

Common Questions

Can I file my own restaurant plans with the NYC Department of Buildings?
No. Construction drawings for a commercial restaurant fit-out are filed by a New York licensed architect or professional engineer, who seals the plans and takes responsibility for code compliance. The property owner also has to authorize the filing.
Do I need Landmarks approval for a restaurant in a historic building?
If the building is a designated landmark or sits in a historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews the work before the building permit is issued. Storefront changes, louvers and signs get the closest review, and even interior work in a designated building goes to Landmarks for sign-off.
Do restaurant signs need their own permit in New York City?
Often, yes. An illuminated sign, especially one that projects from the facade, can need a sign permit, a permit for the mounting bracket and an electrical permit. The sign permit is usually not issued until the building permit is.
What is a DOB objection?
An objection is a plan examiner's comment that the drawings do not yet meet code or are missing something. Each one has to be answered, usually with revised drawings or added documentation, before the job is approved.
