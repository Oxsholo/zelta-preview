Drive-thru, Inline, End-cap or Food Court: What Changes?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

The building type decides who approves your restaurant before the building department sees it. A freestanding drive-thru answers to zoning and site plan review, an inline or end-cap unit answers to the landlord and the shell it sits in, and a food court or transit unit answers to the property owner's own review first.

The building type is the first real decision in a restaurant project, and it changes more than the floor plan. It decides which approvals come before the building permit, who else has a say in your drawings, and where the kitchen’s grease, exhaust and utilities can go.

In short: a freestanding drive-thru is a site project with zoning and planning review. An inline unit is a tenant project inside someone else’s building. An end-cap is an inline unit with a second face. A food court, outlet or transit unit is a tenant project with a landlord that acts like a second building department. Below is what changes in each, drawn from the projects we have designed.

Freestanding with a drive-thru

A freestanding building on its own pad gives you the most control and the most reviews. You are no longer just fitting out a room. You are changing a site, and the town looks at the whole lot: the lane, the queue, parking, curb cuts, landscaping and signs.

Patterns we see again and again on these jobs:

  • The drive-thru itself can need a variance. On one northern New Jersey project, the town required a zoning variance for the drive-thru before anything else could move.
  • Small site items trigger the planning board. On a drive-thru remodel, the town told us the new menu board, the parking lot striping and the signs needed planning or zoning approval, while the interior sink, counter and painting needed no building permit at all.
  • Additions hit setbacks. On a freestanding remodel in Bergen County, the building already sat closer to the road than the front setback allowed, so any addition on the front would have needed a variance. On another job, adding a walk-in cooler to the outside of the building sent the project to the planning board.
  • Sign counts are limited. The same Bergen County town allowed one primary building sign, so the brand’s standard sign package needed a zoning board variance.
  • Grease often goes outside. On freestanding jobs the grease trap is often an exterior unit in the lot, and some sewer authorities set their own minimum size and location.
  • Brand programs change the site. On our Taco Bell remodel in Ridge, New York, the brand’s newer image carried site requirements the older one did not, such as a digital drive-thru menu board.

The design payoff is the ability to separate customers. At Dave’s Hot Chicken in Sumter, South Carolina, the layout keeps dine-in, walk-up pickup and drive-thru orders apart so the three groups never cross. Dunkin’ in Hackettstown and Taco Bell in Stirling are both new freestanding buildings with drive-thru lanes.

Inline: a unit in a strip center or city block

An inline unit shares walls with neighbors on both sides and usually has one storefront. Site plan review often drops away, but the landlord and the existing building take its place.

What changes:

  • You depend on the shell. On a recent South Carolina job, our tenant submission was held until the landlord’s own base building approval came through. On another in North Carolina, the landlord’s plan approval was still outstanding while the tenant set sat ready to file.
  • The space may need to be cut to size. On an inline chicken restaurant in Tennessee, the space was demised, meaning split off from a larger unit, to about 2,500 square feet during early design.
  • Utilities may be the landlord’s to deliver. On one Queens project, the operator’s electric service and gas had to be brought in by the landlord before the kitchen could work.
  • The landlord picks some trades. One Staten Island landlord asked the tenant to use the landlord’s sprinkler contractor and amend the landlord’s own sprinkler drawing.
  • Older buildings constrain the kitchen. At our Taco Bell in a historic building in Jersey City, the grease trap had to go in the building’s cellar, and building management was wary of tying another flue into the existing stack.

Inline units reward tight planning. At Taco Bell in North Arlington, 1,548 square feet holds counter service, a self-order kiosk, a mobile and delivery shelf and a late-night pickup window as separate stations.

End-cap: inline with a second face

An end-cap is the unit at the end of a row. It is still a tenant space, so the landlord issues are the same as inline. The difference is the extra exterior wall.

That second side usually means more glass, more daylight, a second sign face and sometimes room for a patio or a pickup window. Our Taco Bell at the end of a Staten Island strip plaza uses the position for high ceilings and seating for 20. Corner storefronts in cities work the same way: Dunkin’ at Lexington Avenue and East 103rd Street carries signs on both street sides.

Check two things before you count on that second face: the landlord’s sign criteria and the town’s sign code. Either one can limit how many signs you get.

Food court, outlet and transit units

These are tenant spaces where the landlord runs a full design review of its own. At one New Jersey mall, the construction drawings and sign shop drawings went to the landlord first. Only after the landlord approved them and sent the town a letter saying so could permits be filed. The landlord’s sign approval came back as a letter on its own letterhead rather than on the sign vendor’s form.

Other differences:

  • Shared infrastructure. On one co-branded job, the city allowed two brands side by side to share one existing grease interceptor, with final approval tied to a certification form from an approved grease hauler.
  • Shared seating. Food court units lean on common seating, so the unit can be small. Our Wendy’s in the Green Acres Mall food court is 811 square feet, and Popeyes in a Bridgewater mall food court puts self-order kiosks in front of shared seating.
  • Transit traffic. Dunkin’ in the Oculus at the World Trade Center is a compact kiosk with no seating, built for commuters, and 375º Chicken ‘N Fries at Newark’s Gateway Center seats three.
  • Different authorities. On an outlet center on tribal land in Connecticut, the tribal nation’s own land use commission and health office reviewed the project instead of a town and state.

What to ask before you sign

  • Is the site zoned for your use, and does a drive-thru need extra approval?
  • Who approves first: the town, the landlord, or both?
  • Does the shell have the electric service, gas, grease capacity and roof space your kitchen needs, and who pays to bring them in?
  • What do the landlord’s sign criteria and the local sign code allow on each face?

Questions

Common Questions

Does a drive-thru always need zoning approval?
Not always, but often. Many towns treat a drive-thru lane, its menu board and its striping as site changes, so they go to the planning or zoning board even when the interior work needs only a building permit.
Is an end-cap worth paying more for?
For many quick-service brands, yes. The second open side usually means more glass, a second sign face and sometimes room for a patio or pickup window, but the landlord's sign criteria and the local sign code still decide how much of that you can use.
Why does a food court unit take landlord approval before permits?
Mall and transit landlords run their own design review. At one New Jersey mall, our construction drawings and sign shop drawings went to the landlord first, and only after that approval could they be filed with the town.
Which building type is fastest to open?
There is no fixed answer. An inline unit in a ready shell skips site plan review, but it can sit waiting on the landlord's own building approvals. A freestanding pad has more reviews but fewer people in between.
