Permit Comments or Rejection? How to Respond and Get Approved

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Plan review comments are the reviewer's list of what must change or be explained before a permit can be issued. They are not a denial. Answer every comment by number in a response letter that says exactly what changed and where, send a revised sealed set that agrees with itself, and include the owner items the reviewer asked for. A complete response gives the reviewer what is needed to close each comment, though only the reviewer decides when the file is approved.

Plan review comments are the reviewer’s written list of what has to change, be added or be explained before your permit can be issued. In New York City they are called objections; elsewhere they may be comments, corrections or a review letter. Receiving them does not mean the permit was denied. It means the filing is in review and the reviewer needs more.

How quickly the next round closes depends largely on the response. Answer every comment by its number, saying exactly what changed and where. Send drawings that agree with themselves. Include the owner items the reviewer asked for. Those three things give the reviewer what is needed to close each comment. The approval decision still belongs to the reviewer.

Comments, intake rejection and denial

These are three different situations, and each has a different fix.

  • Comments, corrections or objections. The filing was accepted and reviewed. You answer with a response letter and revised drawings. In New York City, the Department of Buildings sends a notice of objections when a plan review or audit fails, and objections are resolved with the department’s plan examination or audit staff.
  • Intake rejection. The office would not accept the filing, usually because something required for review is missing or misnamed: a form, a signature, a fee or a file in the wrong format. The fix is administrative, and review has not started.
  • Denial. A formal decision against the application, such as a zoning use that is not allowed. Redrawing does not fix it. The routes are a different design, a variance or another approval, or an appeal, each with its own process.

The rest of this guide is about the first and most common situation.

Sort every comment before changing anything

The first step is sorting, not drafting. Read the whole letter and put each comment in one of three groups:

  • Changed. The drawings will be revised to answer it.
  • Waiting on someone. It needs information from the owner, an engineer, the landlord or a vendor.
  • No change, with a reason. The drawings already comply, and the response will show where.

Sorting shows who owes what and catches comments that interact. Assign structural comments to the structural engineer while other disciplines answer their own items.

Follow each change through the whole set

A comment is rarely about one line on one sheet. If a reviewer asks to move a hand sink, the sink moves on the floor plan, the elevations, the equipment schedule and the plumbing and electrical drawings behind it. Each change has to reach every related view, tag and schedule, and any affected engineering has to go back to the engineer responsible.

Skipping this is how one comment becomes a second round. The reviewer checks the revised sheet, finds it no longer matches the sheet next to it, and writes a new comment.

Make sure the set agrees with itself

Some of the most avoidable comments come from drawings that contradict each other, such as notes on one sheet keeping an existing exhaust fan while another sheet wires a new one. Before a revised set goes out, we check it for references that lead nowhere, callouts with no matching detail and scope that drifted between sheets. A reviewer should never be the first person to find a reference that goes nowhere.

Write the response letter

The response letter is what the reviewer reads first. Ours list each comment in the reviewer’s numbering, grouped by discipline, with a direct answer under each:

  1. Repeat the comment in the reviewer’s words.
  2. Answer it directly, naming the sheet and detail, not just “revised”. An illustrative answer: “Exit sign added at the rear door. See sheet A-1.0, keynote 12.”
  3. Say when no change was made and why, citing the code section and the sheet that shows compliance.
  4. Say when a comment will close later, and how. If a condition can only be confirmed once demolition opens a ceiling, say so, and say who will confirm it and what will be submitted.

Then send a revised, sealed set with the changes clouded and a new revision number and date. Check the office’s submission rules first. Some portals require specific file names, and some want a short revision narrative or an updated construction value with each resubmittal.

When the comment is right, and when it is not

If the drawings already comply, say so, cite the code section and point to the sheet. Keep it short and factual.

When a comment identifies an applicable requirement, revise the drawings and cite the changed sheet in the response. Our New York City permit guide covers one example, the city’s roof rules.

The same approach works for questions raised during construction. When a contractor proposes a different installation method that also meets code, the architect can review it and approve it in writing, which gives the inspector a clear record.

Owner items close comments too

Reviewers often ask for things the architect cannot supply. In Memphis, the building review asked for a contract valuation letter on the general contractor’s letterhead matching the construction value on the application. In Jefferson County, West Virginia, the contractor’s state license and certificate of insurance are part of what the office needs before issuance. Items like these are often why an approved set sits unissued.

We list owner items in the same response plan as the drawing comments, with who owes each one, so they move together. The owner checklist shows what to have ready.

Ask for a conversation

A phone call can save a round. If a comment is unclear, ask the reviewer what would satisfy it before redrawing. Some offices also offer meetings or faster review tracks; see what actually speeds up a restaurant permit.

After approval

Approval covers the drawings as approved. Changing them later usually means a revision filing and another review, and some towns are strict that approved exterior elevations be built exactly as drawn. Settle brand and landlord comments before filing, so the reviewer sees the final design once. See the restaurant build-out timeline for how comment cycles fit the whole schedule.

Questions

Common Questions

Is a plan review comment the same as a permit rejection?

No. Comments ask for changes or information on a filing that is under review. An intake rejection means the office would not accept the filing as complete. A denial is a formal decision against the application, which usually has its own appeal or variance route.

What is a response letter?

A letter from the architect that lists each reviewer comment by number and answers it: what changed, on which sheet and detail, or why no change is needed. It goes in with the revised drawings.

Can I disagree with a plan reviewer's comment?

Yes, if the drawings already comply. Answer with the code section and the sheet that shows compliance, politely and briefly. If the comment is correct, fix it; arguing a correct comment usually costs another review cycle.

Why do comments sometimes ask the owner for documents?

Reviewers often need items only the owner or contractor can supply, such as a contract value letter, the contractor's license or registration, insurance, or landlord authorization. The permit will not issue until those arrive, even if the drawings are approved.
