Opening a Jimmy John's: Design, Permits and Build-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A Jimmy John's can open as a city storefront, a unit in a strip center, a store shared with another brand or a freestanding drive-thru. Each format triggers different approvals before the building permit, then the same core work: a layout from the brand's current package, brand approval, a code analysis of the specific space, coordinated engineering and a food service plan review.

To open a Jimmy John’s you need a space that suits one of the brand’s formats, a layout drawn from its current design package and approved by the brand, and the local approvals that format triggers. A city storefront mostly answers to the building department, the landlord and the health department. A conversion or a drive-thru adds zoning, and sometimes a site plan, before the building permit can be filed.

We design Jimmy John’s restaurants as stand-alone stores, as co-branded stores with Dunkin’, and in buildings shared with other brands we also design. This guide covers what each format asks of the site and how the approvals line up. For investment figures, see Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document, which sets out the brand’s own estimate; our guide to what drives build-out cost explains why your site may differ.

The formats we design

The site decides the format, and each format shifts the approvals.

City storefront. Our Jimmy John’s in the Financial District occupies a ground-floor bay of a Financial District building with traditional dark wood storefront millwork, with the brand’s red roundel and lettering above wood-framed glass doors. A storefront like this trades site plan review for building questions: the existing structure, exhaust routing and the landlord’s approval.

A building shared with other brands. Our Jimmy John’s in Albany sits in a new mixed-use building, with the order counter under a red and black menu wall and a short row of seating by the glass storefront. It shares the building with a Buffalo Wild Wings GO we also designed (see opening a Buffalo Wild Wings GO).

Co-branded with Dunkin’. Covered in its own section below.

Freestanding drive-thru. A new building on a pad, with its own lot and lane. This is the format with the most approvals before the building permit.

Co-branding with Dunkin’

A co-branded store gives each brand its own presence up front while the two share one back of house.

Our Dunkin’ and Jimmy John’s near Herald Square puts both brands under one blue-paneled facade with stacked signage, keeps separate order points, and places self-order kiosks by the entrance to spread out the rush. A few blocks away, our Dunkin’ and Jimmy John’s on West 34th Street combines the two concepts in one store with seating for six.

To the building department a co-branded store is usually one restaurant. On one recent co-branded Dunkin’ and Jimmy John’s, the two brands went on one building permit application, not two. Our guide to co-branded restaurants covers shared kitchens, grease and counters in detail.

Drive-thru pads

A Jimmy John’s drive-thru is a land use project before it is a building project. Expect a site plan, prepared by a civil engineer, and a review by the town or county before the building permit is accepted.

On one drive-thru pad we designed, the county’s site plan determination required site plan approval before it would accept the building permit. The county’s queuing rules called for more drive-thru stacking spaces behind the order board than the brand’s corporate site layout showed, so the layout was redrawn to meet the local ordinance. Local code controls the site, whatever the prototype shows. Our drive-thru design guide and pad site guide cover the lane, the queue and the site plan.

Converting another building

A conversion can be the quickest way into a good location, but the change in use brings its own approval. Turning a non-restaurant building into a restaurant is a change of use, and many towns review it before the building permit.

On one conversion of a former bank into a shared Jimmy John’s and Buffalo Wild Wings GO, the borough issued its own change of use approval, and the building review could not start until that closed. The brands’ corporate revisions also had to be finished before the set was final. See turning a retail space into a restaurant.

What the brand and the code each decide

The brand package sets the layout, finishes, equipment and signs; the building and the local rules decide everything the package cannot. Our job is to fit one to the other and carry it through the brand’s approvals (see how franchise brand approvals work).

Items that changed from site to site on our Jimmy John’s work:

  • Grease. On one recent store, the sealed set had to show a new exterior grease interceptor before the building permit could be filed. Elsewhere, an existing interceptor may serve. The local sewer authority decides (see kitchen engineering).
  • Energy code paperwork. The same set needed energy code compliance documentation, typically prepared with the Department of Energy’s COMcheck tool, before filing.
  • Who reviews the drawings. In some states the reviewer depends on the municipality: its own staff, a regional code office, a private third-party agency or the state. On one recent shopping center store, identifying the right reviewing agency was the first permitting task. Our Pennsylvania guide shows how varied this can be.
  • The address. In a shopping center, the plaza’s lead address and the unit’s own address can differ. We confirm the unit address against county address records before sealed sets go out, so every application matches.

Health review and opening

Every Jimmy John’s needs a food service plan review from the local or state health authority, separate from the building permit. Start it alongside the building drawings, not after.

The health packet needs as much from the operator as from the architect: menus, the equipment schedule and spec sheets, and on one recent store a certified food manager. Time the submission to the equipment delivery, because inspections cannot happen until the equipment is in (see health department plan review). Once the building permit is issued, the contractor’s trade permits and inspections lead to the final sign-offs and the certificate of occupancy (see getting a certificate of occupancy).

Questions

Common Questions

Can a Jimmy John's share a space with another brand?

Yes. We have designed Jimmy John's stores sharing a storefront with Dunkin' in Midtown Manhattan, and a Jimmy John's in Albany that shares its building with a Buffalo Wild Wings GO. A shared store is usually one restaurant to the building department, often on one permit application.

Does a Jimmy John's drive-thru need zoning approval?

Usually. On a drive-thru pad we designed, the county required site plan approval before it would accept the building permit, and its queuing rules called for a different drive-thru layout than the brand's corporate site plan showed.

How much does it cost to open a Jimmy John's?

The brand's Franchise Disclosure Document, Item 7, gives its own estimate of the initial investment. The build-out part of that depends on the space: the hood, grease, power and any site work drive it more than the square footage.
