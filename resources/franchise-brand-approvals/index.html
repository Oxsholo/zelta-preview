How Franchise Brand Design Approvals Work

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Before a franchise location is built, the brand usually reviews its design in stages: a layout or test fit, then a design development set, then a formal release. The exact steps differ by brand. Brand approval is separate from the city permit; a location needs both, and a change after either approval usually has to go back through review.

A franchise brand approves a new location’s design before it can be built, usually in stages. A common pattern starts with a layout, often called a test fit, that shows the brand’s kitchen and dining program working in your specific space. It moves to a design development set, the drawings that fix finishes, signs and equipment. It ends with a formal release; at Taco Bell, for example, that release is called the Notice to Proceed (NTP). Each stage checks the design against the brand’s prototype, the standard design every location starts from. The names, order and number of stages differ from brand to brand, so ask your brand contact for its current process at the start.

Brand approval runs alongside the city permit, not instead of it. The brand cares about its standards. The building department cares about the code. A location needs both, and disagreement can add redesign and review. We work through these reviews for more than 40 brands; the stages below are the pattern we see most often.

Stage 1: the test fit

The test fit is a floor plan that puts the brand’s program into your actual space: counter, kitchen line, drive-thru window, dining, restrooms. It answers the first question every brand asks, which is whether this site can work at all.

This is also the stage for requests. On one location, the franchise team asked us to fit in a pickup window as long as it did not disturb the layout. Brands also set rules here that shape the plan, such as requiring a customer restroom wherever there is a dining room. Getting the test fit right is cheap. Changing it after construction documents are underway is not.

Stage 2: design development

The design development set, or DD set, turns the approved layout into a design: finishes, ceilings, lighting, signs, equipment and the exterior. Many brands want to see it before the construction documents are finished.

This is where brand standards get specific. Things we have seen brands check at this stage:

  • Finishes. On our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the brand had to approve every finish before the construction set was finalized, and all finishes had to pass the brand’s cleaning-chemical testing before they could be ordered.
  • Signs. Brands treat their logo and lettering as a fixed unit, called a lockup. On a Queens location, the brand said the logo and letters could be separated only case by case, where they sit on different planes of the building or far apart on the same one.
  • Existing conditions. On a Manhattan location, the brand said the existing chair lift could stay only if it was brought up to code.

Stage 3: the release to proceed

The final brand approval releases the project, but what it releases differs. Some brands release before permits are filed, others alongside them, and what the brand needs to see varies too, from a design development set to full construction documents. Ask your brand contact early what the release requires and plan around their answer.

We build the construction documents on the prototype version the brand approved for your location. A newer prototype release does not automatically replace one already in progress, and switching midway means redrawing.

When the brand and the town disagree

Disagreement between the brand and the reviewing authority can require redesign. The brand wants its full exterior image. The town, a historic commission or the landlord wants something else.

  • Historic districts. Our Jersey City Taco Bell sits in the stone base of a historic building, and its storefront, signs and awning needed a certificate of appropriateness from the city’s historic preservation commission. Our guide to restaurants in historic buildings covers how that review works and how to protect yourself in the lease.
  • Planning boards. On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, the planning board required a right-in, right-out driveway from the side street, which the site layout then had to absorb.
  • Sign codes. On one of our New Jersey projects, the town allowed only one primary building sign, so the brand’s signage needed a zoning variance. On another, the town expected the approved elevations to be followed closely.

The fix is sequencing. We check the local sign code, historic status and site plan rules before the brand’s image is locked, so the drawing the brand approves is one the town can also approve. Our signage guide covers the sign side in detail.

Changes after approval

Any change after the brand approves should be treated as a resubmission. On our Taco Bell in Ridge, New York, using a different exterior image than the one already approved meant uploading it for review as a revision to the NTP. Remodels work the same way: on one Taco Bell remodel, the brand required new signs to its current standard, the old co-brand sign removed from the canopy, and a proper blade sign added. Any change that touches the permit drawings also goes back to the building department as a revision.

Exceptions are allowed

Prototypes are written for an ideal site. Real sites, especially in cities, are not ideal, and brands know it. A small urban store may get an exception for a piece of equipment or part of the program that does not fit. Exceptions should be documented in writing and checked against the local code, which may still require what the brand has waived.

Before the design starts

Two documents are worth having before any of this. First, the brand’s current design package and the version approved for your location. Second, if you are new to the brand, its Franchise Disclosure Document: Item 7, the estimated initial investment table required by the FTC Franchise Rule, lists the expected costs to open, including build-out, as ranges. The FTC’s guide to buying a franchise explains how to read it. Brand-specific guides, such as opening a Taco Bell and opening a Dunkin’, cover how each brand’s package shapes the store.

Questions

Common Questions

Does franchisor approval replace the building permit?

No. The brand checks that the design meets its standards; the building department checks that it meets the code. A location needs both, and neither one overrides the other.

Can a franchise brand grant exceptions to its prototype?

Sometimes. Brands do approve exceptions for difficult sites, especially small urban stores. Get any exception in writing and make sure it still meets the local code, which may require what the brand has waived.

What happens if I change the design after the brand approves it?

Expect to resubmit. On our Taco Bell in Ridge, New York, switching to a different exterior image after approval meant uploading the new design for a revised approval.

Should brand review or permit drawings come first?

Neither waits for the other entirely. The brand should approve the layout before detailed drawings start, but local zoning, sign and historic rules should be checked before the brand locks the exterior. Plan both reviews on one calendar.
