How Franchise Brand Design Approvals Work

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

Before a franchise location is built, the brand reviews its design in stages: a layout or test fit, then a design development set, then the documents that release the project to proceed. Brand approval is separate from the city permit. A location needs both, and a change after either approval usually has to go back through review.

A franchise brand approves a new location’s design in stages before it can be built. It usually starts with a layout, often called a test fit, that shows the brand’s kitchen and dining program working in your specific space. It moves to a design development set, the drawings that fix finishes, signs and equipment. It ends with a formal release, which at Taco Bell is called the Notice to Proceed (NTP). Each stage checks the design against the brand’s current prototype, the standard design every location starts from.

Brand approval runs alongside the city permit, not instead of it. The brand cares about its standards. The building department cares about the code. A location needs both, and the most expensive surprises happen where the two disagree. We work through these reviews for more than 40 brands, and the stages below are what we see again and again.

Stage 1: the test fit

The test fit is a floor plan that puts the brand’s program into your actual space: counter, kitchen line, drive-thru window, dining, restrooms. It answers the first question every brand asks, which is whether this site can work at all.

On a recent chicken restaurant in South Carolina, the brand’s corporate operations team approved the test fit first, with a small seating change a few days later, before any detailed drawing started. This is also the stage for requests. On one location, the franchise team asked us to fit in a pickup window as long as it did not disturb the layout. On another, the team told us the restroom is typically wanted away from the seating area.

Getting the test fit right is cheap. Changing it after construction documents are underway is not.

Stage 2: design development

The design development set, or DD set, turns the approved layout into a design: finishes, ceilings, lighting, signs, equipment and the exterior. Many brands want to see it before the construction documents are finished. On a location in Queens, the brand asked for the DD set to be submitted for review before we completed the construction set for its NTP. On that South Carolina chicken restaurant, the DD set went to corporate for review before construction documents were issued.

This is where brand standards get specific. Things we have seen brands check at this stage:

  • Finishes. On our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the brand had to approve every finish before the construction set was finalized, and all finishes had to pass the brand’s cleaning-chemical testing before they could be ordered.
  • Signs. Brands treat their logo and lettering as a fixed unit, called a lockup. On our Taco Bell in Kingsbridge, the Bronx, the brand’s reviewer asked for the letters to be centered on the bell logo exactly as the lockup shows. On a Queens location, the brand said the logo and letters could be separated only case by case, where they sit on different planes of the building or far apart on the same one, and that any change to the lockup had to be reviewed with brand leadership before the NTP review.
  • Site items. On a New Jersey location, the brand’s reviewer needed a landscape plan as part of the NTP process. On a Brooklyn location, the reviewer wanted a site lighting plan and a drawing of the trash enclosure, though not as a condition of the NTP.
  • Existing conditions. On a Manhattan location, the brand said the existing chair lift could stay only if it was brought up to code.

Stage 3: the release to proceed

The final brand approval releases the project to permitting and construction. On a recent Taco Bell, the franchise team told us a full set of construction documents was not needed for that review. Timing varies by brand and by how complete the submission is, so ask your brand contact early and plan around their answer.

We build the construction documents on the prototype version the brand approved for your location. A newer prototype release does not automatically replace one already in progress, and switching midway means redrawing.

When the brand and the town disagree

This is where franchise projects lose the most time. The brand wants its full exterior image. The town, a historic commission or the landlord wants something else.

  • Historic districts. Our Jersey City Taco Bell sits in the stone base of a historic building. The city’s historic preservation commission required a Certificate of Appropriateness for the storefront alteration, two signs and an awning, with a full set of existing and proposed plans, kitchen exhaust risers and sections of the new doors, windows and awning. Because the commission might not rule on the signs before the building permit, the advice was to cover that risk in the lease, using the brand-approved signage as the reference. The finished store keeps the building’s arched entry and tall windows.
  • Planning boards. On our Taco Bell in Paterson, New Jersey, the planning board required a right-in, right-out driveway from the side street. When a site change cost a parking space, the franchisee was fine with it as long as the brand’s corporate architect approved first.
  • Strict towns. On one Taco Bell, the reviewing town insisted the approved elevations be followed exactly, and its ordinance allowed signs on the front and side only, not the rear.

The fix is sequencing. We check the local sign code, historic status and site plan rules before the brand’s image is locked, so the drawing the brand approves is one the town can approve too.

Changes after approval

Any change after the brand approves should be treated as a resubmission. On our Taco Bell in Ridge, New York, using a different exterior image than the one already approved meant uploading it for review as a revision to the NTP. Remodels work the same way: on a Taco Bell remodel, the brand required new signs to the current standard and image, the old co-brand sign removed from the canopy, and a proper blade sign added.

Exceptions are allowed

Prototypes are written for an ideal site. Real sites, especially in cities, are not ideal, and brands know it. On a location in Queens, the brand approved a project exception: no customer restroom and no self-serve beverage station. Exceptions like this should be documented in writing and checked against the local code, which may still require what the brand has waived.

Questions

Common Questions

Does franchisor approval replace the building permit?
No. The brand checks that the design meets its standards; the building department checks that it meets the code. A location needs both, and neither one overrides the other.
Can a franchise brand grant exceptions to its prototype?
Yes. Brands do approve project exceptions for difficult sites, such as a small urban store without a customer restroom or a self-serve beverage station. Get the exception in writing and make sure it still meets the local code.
What happens if I change the design after the brand approves it?
Expect to resubmit. On one Taco Bell we designed, switching to a different exterior image after approval meant uploading the new design for a revised approval before work could continue.
Does the brand need a full construction set to approve a location?
Usually not for the main approval. Brands often review a design development set first, and on a recent Taco Bell the brand's release to proceed did not require a full set of construction documents.
