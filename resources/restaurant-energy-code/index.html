Energy Code Compliance in a Restaurant Fit-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In a restaurant fit-out, the energy code applies to the new work: storefront glass and insulation you add, lighting, heating and cooling, water heating and kitchen exhaust. The unaltered parts of the building generally do not have to be upgraded. Compliance is shown on the permit drawings, usually with a compliance report or energy analysis, and some cities inspect it during construction.

The energy code applies to a restaurant fit-out wherever you do new work. If you add insulation or replace the storefront, install new lighting, put in new heating and cooling, add a water heater or build a new kitchen exhaust system, that work has to meet the energy code in force where you build. The parts of the building you do not touch generally do not have to be brought up to date.

Compliance is shown on paper before the permit, usually as a compliance report or an energy analysis in the drawing set. Some cities also inspect it during construction and check the finished work against what was approved.

Which energy code applies

Each state, and some cities, adopt their own energy code, and the rules follow the address. Most states base theirs on the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), which for commercial buildings also allows compliance through ASHRAE Standard 90.1, an engineering standard. The U.S. Department of Energy tracks which edition each state uses on its state portal.

Some places write their own. New York City has its own energy conservation code, based on the IECC with local amendments. Requirements also change with climate: insulation and glass rules are stricter in colder climate zones.

What the code covers in a fit-out

The rule for existing buildings is simple to state. New York City’s code, like the IECC it is based on, requires alterations to conform to the code for new construction without requiring the unaltered portions of the building to comply, and the work cannot leave the building less compliant than before.

In a typical restaurant fit-out, that brings in:

  • The envelope you change. New storefront glass, new insulation or new exterior walls. On one Manhattan storefront, Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, approved the storefront only with insulated, tempered glazing.
  • Lighting. Limits on lighting power for the space and automatic controls, so lights are not left on when no one needs them.
  • Heating, cooling and ventilation. Equipment efficiency, controls and duct insulation for any new or replaced units.
  • Water heating. Efficiency and pipe insulation for new water heaters.
  • The entrance. Codes based on the IECC can require a vestibule at public entrances in colder climates. New York City’s version exempts doors that open directly from a space under 3,000 square feet in buildings under 75 feet tall, which covers many small restaurants.

Remodels count too. Replacing lighting or rooftop equipment can bring in current requirements for that equipment, as our remodel guide explains.

Kitchen exhaust and make-up air

The kitchen hood is the biggest energy user in most restaurants, because every cubic foot it exhausts has to be replaced with outside air that is often heated or cooled. Energy codes address this directly. New York City’s code, for example, limits replacement air introduced straight into the hood cavity to 10 percent of the hood’s exhaust, and where total hood exhaust exceeds 5,000 cubic feet per minute it requires listed hoods with maximum exhaust rates plus one of several measures: transfer air, demand-controlled exhaust that slows the fans when cooking is light, or heat recovery.

Whatever the size of the hood, the make-up air still has to be designed. On a Brooklyn store, the brand’s standard hood had no built-in make-up air, so the building’s air handling units had to be sized to supply it. Our kitchen engineering guide covers the hood in more depth.

The same code also sets minimum efficiency for some commercial kitchen equipment, such as fryers. Brands usually specify the equipment, so check that the brand’s list meets the local code before it is ordered.

How compliance is shown

Most building departments want proof on paper before they issue a permit. The usual tools:

  • A compliance report. COMcheck, the Department of Energy’s free software, checks a commercial building, addition or alteration against the IECC or ASHRAE 90.1. The report is signed and filed with the drawings.
  • Notes and schedules on the drawings. Glass and insulation values on the architectural sheets, and equipment efficiencies on the mechanical, plumbing and electrical sheets.

Some reviewers will not take the set without it. Before filing a sandwich shop in south central Pennsylvania, our sealed set had to include an energy code compliance report along with an exterior grease interceptor.

Inspections and sign-off

Some cities check energy code work in the field, not just on paper. New York City requires energy code progress inspections by an inspector from an approved agency, and at sign-off the inspector certifies that the installed work matches the approved energy analysis. If it does not, the analysis has to be redone to match what was built.

That makes substitutions risky. A different rooftop unit, light fixture or water heater swapped in during construction can break the approved analysis. In New York City we also tell owners to print the certificate of compliance for new rooftop or outdoor HVAC equipment and keep it on site, because it is the document fire inspectors ask for.

Choices that make compliance easier

The easiest energy code project is one where the big decisions are made early.

  • Reuse what works. On one Queens Taco Bell, we kept the existing rooftop unit and added a supplemental system where it was needed. Unaltered equipment generally does not have to be brought up to current code.
  • Pick the system to fit the building. On a Brooklyn Taco Bell the heating and cooling was a variable refrigerant flow system, an efficient split system with small indoor units. Freestanding prototypes often call for gas-fired rooftop units instead.
  • Size water heating to the use. On a Yonkers store, the owner asked for two tankless water heaters, one serving only the three-compartment sink, with a switch so either could cover if the other failed. An Orlando area bagel shop also used tankless heaters.
  • Settle the fuel. Gas or all-electric changes the equipment, the electrical service and the energy calculations. Decide before the engineers start.

Questions

Common Questions

Does a restaurant tenant fit-out have to meet the energy code?

Yes, for the work you do. Codes based on the International Energy Conservation Code require alterations to meet new-construction rules for the altered parts, without forcing the untouched parts of the building to comply. New lighting, HVAC, water heaters and storefront glass are all covered.

What is COMcheck?

COMcheck is free compliance software from the U.S. Department of Energy that shows whether a commercial building, addition or alteration meets the IECC or ASHRAE Standard 90.1. Many building departments accept its report as the energy code submission.

Does the energy code cover the kitchen hood?

It can. Energy codes limit how much unconditioned replacement air goes straight into the hood, and very large hood systems must add measures such as demand-controlled exhaust or heat recovery. Your mechanical engineer checks which rules apply.

Who prepares the energy code paperwork?

The architect and the engineers. The architect covers the envelope, and the engineers cover lighting, HVAC and water heating; the results go on the drawings or in a report filed with the permit.
