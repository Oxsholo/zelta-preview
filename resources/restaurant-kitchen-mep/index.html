Restaurant Hood, Grease Trap and Gas Requirements

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Three systems drive the engineering in a restaurant kitchen: the exhaust hood with its fire suppression and make-up air, the grease interceptor that keeps fats out of the sewer, and the gas and electric service that feeds the equipment. Each depends on the building, the landlord and the local sewer authority or utility as well as the drawings, so each needs answers before the layout is final.

A restaurant kitchen is engineered around three things: the hood that pulls smoke and grease off the cooking line, the grease interceptor that keeps fats out of the sewer, and the gas and electric service that feeds the equipment. Together they fall under MEP, short for mechanical, electrical and plumbing, and they are where restaurant fit-outs often meet late surprises.

None of them is decided by the drawings alone. The roof, the landlord, the sewer authority and the utility all get a say, and the earlier those answers come in, the less the layout moves. This guide is the overview. Our guides on getting exhaust out of a city storefront and adding rooftop units go deeper on the duct route and the roof.

The hood and the exhaust

Cooking that produces grease or smoke normally sits under a grease-rated commercial hood. The International Mechanical Code, which most states adopt with their own amendments, calls this a Type I hood and also sets the rules for its duct, exhaust fan, make-up air and fire suppression. The same chapter allows some exceptions, such as certain electric equipment tested to produce very little grease, so the equipment list decides the answer, and the local official confirms it.

Brands specify their hoods and fans in the prototype, and on freestanding stores we generally follow it as drawn. On our Taco Bell in Morristown and Taco Bell in Paterson, the exhaust hood and fans followed the brand’s prototype, with the fans on the roof.

City storefronts are harder, because the duct has to find a legal way out of a building with other floors above. On one Queens storefront, the kitchen exhaust went out through louvers in the front. Our urban exhaust guide compares the routes and what each one needs.

Three pieces travel with the hood:

  • Make-up air. Air the hood pulls out has to be replaced, or doors resist opening and the room is pulled into negative pressure. Make-up air is that replacement outdoor air. It can come from a dedicated unit or from the building’s heating and cooling equipment, sized together with the hood.
  • Fire suppression. The hood carries its own suppression system, often called an Ansul system after its best-known brand. Its shop drawings usually come from the hood or suppression vendor, and on our Taco Bell in Jersey City the contractor’s suppression vendor provided them for the permit submittal. The system ties into the fuel supply so cooking stops when it discharges.
  • Balancing. Fire officials may want proof the system works as designed. On our Dunkin’ in Hillsborough Township, the fire official required an air balancing report showing the design values and the existing conditions.

The exhaust fan, make-up air unit and heating and cooling units usually end up on the roof. We fix their locations on the roof plan before the engineers start, so the design begins from the final layout. Structural checks, curbs, dunnage and landlord roof rules are covered in our rooftop units guide.

Grease

A grease trap and a grease interceptor do the same job at different scales. A grease trap usually means a small unit under or beside a sink. A grease interceptor usually means a larger tank, often buried outside the building. Both catch fats, oils and grease before they reach the sewer. Chapter 10 of the International Plumbing Code covers them, and many sewer authorities add their own rules on type, size, location and cleaning.

The building decides as much as the code. On our Morristown Taco Bell, the grease interceptor was an exterior unit. In Jersey City, where the restaurant sits in the stone base of a historic building, the grease trap had to go in the building’s cellar.

Who decides the size and type varies:

  • The town reviewing our Morristown Taco Bell asked for grease trap calculations before the permit could be submitted.
  • Some sewer authorities require the interceptor outside the building, set a minimum size or accept only certain kinds of unit. Ask the local authority before the plumbing design is drawn, and get any exception in writing.

Grease rules also show up at the end of the job. In one South Carolina city, the sewer department required the installed grease unit to be serviced and cleaned before the store could open. Build that step into the opening schedule.

Gas and electric

Utility service is a lease question before it is a design question. A restaurant needs more power and gas than most retail, and the lease or work letter should say who delivers it, how much and to where. Our guide to landlord work and tenant work covers that split. A new freestanding store often needs a new electrical service and gas service from the utility, which has its own application and lead time.

Fuel choice shapes the rest of the design. On our New Jersey Taco Bells, we used gas-fired rooftop units because the brand’s prototype called for them. Some owners and buildings now ask for all-electric kitchens, which changes the electrical service size, the water heater and sometimes the hood equipment. Where a site has no natural gas, equipment may need to be ordered for a different fuel, so confirm the gas supply before equipment is ordered.

Older buildings add their own limits: the size of the existing service, the meter room, and whether the building can give one tenant more capacity. The electrical engineer confirms what the code and the utility will allow before the layout depends on it.

Who designs what

Licensed MEP engineers design and seal the mechanical, electrical and plumbing drawings in most jurisdictions. Our job is coordination: making the engineering agree with the layout, the brand prototype and the landlord’s rules. A change is followed through the whole set. Move a sink on the plan, and the plumbing and electrical work behind it moves too. Our guide to construction documents explains how the set is checked.

Landlords sometimes fix part of the answer. On the Paterson Taco Bell, the landlord provided the rooftop units, and the engineers designed to those sizes. Other landlords ask tenants to use the landlord’s own sprinkler contractor.

In a second-generation space, one that was already a restaurant, reuse can save money. An existing rooftop unit in good condition can sometimes stay, with a supplemental unit added where the new kitchen needs more. Anything reused is checked against the new equipment and the current code first.

Questions

Common Questions

What is an Ansul system?

Ansul is a common brand name for the fire suppression system built into a commercial kitchen hood. When it detects a fire it sprays a wet chemical onto the cooking line and shuts off the fuel to the equipment under the hood. Its shop drawings usually come from the hood or suppression vendor.

Do I need a grease interceptor for a small restaurant?

Usually, if the kitchen sends grease into the drains from cooking, pot washing or a dishwasher. The plumbing code your state adopted and the local sewer authority decide the type, size and location, and some authorities set their own minimums or require the unit outside the building.

Can I reuse the hood and rooftop units from the last restaurant?

Sometimes. An existing rooftop unit can stay when it is in good condition and suits the new load, sometimes with a supplemental unit added. Everything reused has to be checked against your brand's equipment and the current code, so plan on a survey before you count on it.

Who designs the MEP for a restaurant?

Licensed engineers usually design and seal the mechanical, electrical and plumbing drawings, which together are called MEP. The architect coordinates them with the layout, the brand standards and the landlord's rules so the whole set agrees.
