Hoods, Grease and Gas: Kitchen Engineering in a Restaurant

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

Three systems drive the engineering in a restaurant kitchen: the exhaust hood with its fire suppression and make-up air, the grease interceptor that keeps fats out of the sewer, and the gas and electric service that feeds the equipment. Each depends on the building, the landlord and the local sewer or utility, not just the drawings, so they need answers before the layout is final.

A restaurant kitchen is engineered around three things: the hood that pulls smoke and grease out of the cooking line, the grease interceptor that keeps fats out of the sewer, and the gas and electric service that feeds the equipment. These sit under MEP, short for mechanical, electrical and plumbing, and they cause more late surprises in a restaurant fit-out than anything in the dining room.

The reason is that none of them is decided by the drawings alone. The roof, the landlord, the sewer authority and the utility all get a say. The earlier those answers come in, the less the layout moves.

The hood and the exhaust

Any cooking that produces grease sits under a commercial exhaust hood. The hood connects to a duct that runs to an exhaust fan, usually on the roof. Brands specify their hoods and fans in the prototype, and on freestanding stores we generally follow it as drawn. On our Taco Bell in Morristown and Taco Bell in Paterson, the exhaust hood and fans followed the brand’s prototype, with the fans on the roof.

Urban storefronts are harder. When there is no clear path to the roof, the exhaust has to go somewhere else. On one Queens storefront, the kitchen exhaust had to discharge through the front louvers.

Three pieces travel with the hood:

  • Make-up air. Every cubic foot the hood pulls out has to be replaced, or doors whistle and the room goes negative. On a Brooklyn job, the brand’s standard hood had no built-in make-up air, so the building’s air handling units had to be sized to supply it.
  • Fire suppression. The hood carries its own suppression system, often called an Ansul system after its best-known brand. Its shop drawings usually come from the hood or suppression vendor. On our Taco Bell in Jersey City, the contractor’s suppression vendor could provide the shop drawings for the permit submittal. The system also ties into the gas line: on one New York job, a plumbing amendment was filed just to add a shutoff valve for the suppression system.
  • Balancing. Fire officials may want proof the system works as designed. On our Dunkin’ in Hillsborough Township, the fire subcode official required an air balancing report showing the design values and the existing conditions.

The roof needs planning too. Landlords often set rules for rooftop equipment. At one Bronx building, the landlord would not allow rooftop units or walk-in cooler condensers to sit directly on the roof, so everything went on dunnage, a raised steel frame. We fix rooftop equipment locations on the roof plan before the engineers start, so their design starts from the final equipment layout.

Grease

A grease interceptor, sometimes called a grease trap, catches fats and oils before they reach the sewer. Small under-sink units serve some spaces. Larger in-ground tanks outside the building serve most freestanding stores. On our Morristown Taco Bell, the grease trap was an exterior unit. In Jersey City, where the restaurant sits in an older building, the grease trap had to go in the building’s cellar.

Who decides the size and type varies more than owners expect:

  • The town reviewing our Morristown Taco Bell asked for grease trap calculations before the permit could be submitted.
  • A plumbing reviewer in East Windsor, New Jersey required every kitchen floor drain to run through the grease trap.
  • A Pennsylvania sewer authority required the interceptor outside the building with a minimum size, and another appeared willing to waive its minimum size if the interceptor were cleaned more often.
  • A town near Pittsburgh would not accept concrete grease traps and required a manufactured unit.

Grease rules also show up at the end of the job. In one Pennsylvania town, the sewer connection and grease permits are filed after the building permit. On a two-brand building in Tennessee, the two restaurants were allowed to share the existing interceptor, but final approval depended on an approved grease hauler certifying it. In South Carolina, the city required the installed unit to be serviced and cleaned before opening.

Gas and electric

Utility service is a lease question before it is a design question. A restaurant needs more power and gas than most retail, and the lease should say who delivers it and how much. On one Queens site, the project terms spelled out that the landlord had to deliver the electrical and gas service the store needed. New freestanding stores often need a new service entirely: our Morristown Taco Bell needed a new 600 amp electrical service.

Fuel choice shapes the rest of the design. On our New Jersey Taco Bells, we used gas-fired rooftop units because the brand’s prototype called for them. Some owners and buildings now ask for all-electric kitchens, which changes the electrical service size and the water heater. On one Queens storefront the water heater had to be electric. On a store in Yonkers, the plan was to open on propane, which meant ordering the right kits for the gas equipment.

Meters can be a trap in older buildings. On a Jersey City fit-out, we advised that two electric meters for a single tenant would not be allowed under the electrical code without special permission from the building inspector.

Who designs what

Licensed MEP engineers design and seal the mechanical, electrical and plumbing drawings. Some jurisdictions want those sheets sealed by the engineers rather than the architect. Our job is coordination: making the engineering agree with the layout, the brand prototype and the landlord’s rules. Our drawing standard is that a change is followed through the whole set. Move a sink on the plan, and the plumbing and electrical work behind it moves too.

Landlords sometimes fix part of the answer. On the Paterson Taco Bell, the landlord provided the rooftop units, and the engineers designed to those sizes. Other landlords ask tenants to use the landlord’s own sprinkler contractor.

In a second-generation space, one that was already a restaurant, reuse can save money. On one Queens Taco Bell, we kept the existing rooftop unit and added a supplemental system where it was needed. Anything reused is checked against the new equipment first.

Questions

Common Questions

What is an Ansul system?
Ansul is a common brand name for the fire suppression system built into a commercial kitchen hood. It sprays a wet chemical onto the cooking line and shuts off the fuel if a fire starts, and its shop drawings usually come from the hood or suppression vendor.
Do I need a grease interceptor for a small restaurant?
Almost always, if you cook or wash dishes. The plumbing code and the local sewer authority decide the type and size, and some authorities set their own minimums or require the unit outside the building.
Can I reuse the hood and rooftop units from the last restaurant?
Sometimes. Existing rooftop units can often stay with supplemental equipment added, but everything reused has to be checked against your brand's equipment and the current code. Plan on a survey before you count on it.
Who designs the MEP for a restaurant?
Licensed MEP engineers design and seal the mechanical, electrical and plumbing drawings, and the architect coordinates them with the layout, the brand standards and the landlord's rules so the set agrees.
