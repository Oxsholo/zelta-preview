How to Choose a Restaurant Architect: Questions to Ask

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Choose a restaurant architect who holds an active license in your state, has designed your format and ideally your brand, is clear about who provides and coordinates the engineering, starts from a real survey, has a plan for permits and plan review comments, and defines construction support in writing. Then compare proposals scope for scope, because two fees only compare fairly when they cover the same work.

Check seven things before you hire a restaurant architect: an active license in the state where your restaurant is, experience with your format and ideally your brand, a clear answer on who provides and coordinates the engineering, a real survey of the space, a plan for permits and plan review comments, construction support defined in writing, and the ability to keep up if you grow. Then compare proposals scope for scope, not fee for fee.

None of this requires taking anyone’s word for it. Each point can be checked with a question, a public record or a line in the proposal. This guide lists the questions, what a good answer sounds like, and the evidence to ask for.

1. Is the architect licensed where your restaurant is?

This is the legal requirement. Architects are licensed state by state, and sealed drawings for a building permit must come from a professional licensed in that state. Some states also require the firm itself to be registered, and state law sets which projects need an architect at all (see do I need an architect to open a restaurant).

Check the license on your state architecture board’s public lookup and confirm it is active. NCARB, the national council of state architecture boards, links to each state’s board from its licensing requirements tool. If you plan to open in several states, ask which states the firm is licensed in today and what it does when a project lands somewhere new. Getting a new license takes time, so the answer should be a plan.

2. Have they designed your format, and your brand?

Confirm an active license for the project state, any required firm authorization, relevant competence and responsibility for the drawings being sealed. Experience makes the design efficient. A firm that does restaurants regularly already knows how a hood, make-up air (outdoor air replacing exhausted air), grease control, occupant load and the health review shape a layout. Ask for recent projects of the same format: a freestanding drive-thru, an inline storefront, a food court counter or a multi-level city store. Each has a different approval path (see drive-thru, inline, end-cap or food court).

For a franchise, brand experience matters as much. The prototype, equipment package and approval stages change with each version, and an architect who has worked through them recently will not learn them on your project (see how franchise brand approvals work). Ask which brands the firm has worked with lately and look at its published projects.

3. Who provides and coordinates the engineering?

Ask whether the proposal includes mechanical, electrical, plumbing and structural engineering, and who is responsible for making those sheets agree with the architecture. When the architect delivers one coordinated set, comments come back answered together. When engineers are hired separately, the contracts should say who coordinates them; otherwise the gap can fall to you. Also ask whether the engineers are licensed in your state, and which sheets they will seal. See who does what on a restaurant project.

4. Does the work start with a real survey?

Good restaurant design starts from measured facts, not from a prototype or an old landlord drawing. Ask how the firm documents the existing space: a site visit, measurements, photos, a 3D scan, and a record of the existing electrical service, gas, plumbing, heating and cooling and roof equipment. See why every project starts with a site survey.

5. How do they handle permits and plan review comments?

Plan review comments are a normal part of permitting, so ask how the firm handles them:

  • Have you filed with this building department, or one like it?
  • Who files: you, the contractor or an expediter?
  • How do you track the building, health, fire and zoning reviews, which often run separately?
  • How do you answer comments, and how many resubmittals does the fee cover?

A good answer is specific: comments answered by number in a response letter with a revised sealed set, and each review tracked to closure. See how to answer plan review comments.

6. What do they do during construction?

The permit is not the finish line. Ask whether the firm answers the contractor’s questions, reviews shop drawings and handles changes that affect the approved permit, and whether that is in the fee or billed as additional services. Field changes made without the architect can hold up inspections (see what your architect does during construction).

7. Can they keep up if you grow?

If you plan more than one location, ask how the firm carries a prototype from site to site while still surveying and permitting each one separately. A consistent drawing standard and one team that knows your brand saves time on every store after the first. See opening many locations with one architect.

Evidence to ask for

A short request list turns claims into documents you can compare:

QuestionEvidence to request
LicenseLicense number and state; firm registration if required
Format and brand experienceRecent published projects of your format and brand
EngineeringNamed disciplines in the proposal; who seals each
SurveyA sample existing conditions plan
PermitsWhich offices they have filed with; how resubmittals are priced
Construction supportThe proposal line that includes or excludes it
ReferencesTwo recent clients of a similar format you can call

Comparing proposals fairly

Two proposals only compare when they cover the same work. Line them up on the phases included, the engineering included and sealed, the exclusions, the caps on revisions, resubmittals and site visits, and what triggers additional services. A lower fee that leaves out the engineering or construction support moves that work to another contract. Our guide to what’s in a restaurant architect’s proposal includes a side-by-side worksheet.

Questions

Common Questions

Should I hire a restaurant specialist or a general architect?

Where an architect is required, check the architect's state license and any required firm authorization. Beyond that, a firm that designs restaurants regularly already knows the hood, grease, occupant load and health review issues, and often the brand's prototype and approval steps.

How do I check an architect's license?

Use the license lookup on your state architecture board's website. Confirm the license is active in the state where the restaurant is, and ask whether the firm itself must be registered there.

Is the cheapest proposal the best value?

Only if it covers the same scope. Check whether the survey, the engineering, plan review resubmittals and construction support are included, and what is excluded, before comparing fees.

What should I ask references?

Ask for recent projects of the same format, such as a drive-thru, an inline storefront or a food court unit, ideally in your state. Ask the reference how comments were handled, how quickly questions were answered during construction, and whether the scope matched the proposal.
