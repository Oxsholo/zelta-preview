How Much Does a Restaurant Architect Cost?

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant architect's fee depends mostly on project type and scope. In our own proposals across 324 projects, a remodel of an existing restaurant ran about three quarters of an inline fit-out, a ground-up drive-thru about one and a third times and a multi-level urban restaurant about 1.7 times. One RSMeans model for a new 5,000-square-foot restaurant uses a 7 percent architectural-fee allowance based on 2019 national data; it is not a current quote or a fit-out benchmark.

What a restaurant architect costs depends far more on the kind of project than on the size of the room. A counter or kiosk fit-out often has a smaller scope, though not always a smaller fee. An inline fit-out in a shopping center, the most common restaurant project, sits in the middle. A ground-up drive-thru with site work or a restaurant on more than one floor often has a larger scope. Everything else moves the number up or down from there: how much engineering is included, whether the structure needs work, how many approvals the design must pass, and what happens during construction.

We do not publish our own fee amounts, because a fair number depends on the site. We do publish how our fees compare across project types. This guide explains how project type and scope change a fee, what the industry percentage says, how fees are structured and how to compare two proposals.

The industry figure, and why it rarely fits a fit-out

The most widely cited benchmark is a percentage of construction cost. RSMeans, the construction cost data publisher, models a new 5,000 square foot, single-story restaurant building and carries architectural fees at 7 percent of construction cost, including the contractor’s overhead and profit, using 2019 national average data.

That is one industry model for a whole new building, not a current quote and not Zelta Design’s pricing. It is a useful sanity check on a ground-up project. It is a poor guide for a tenant fit-out, where construction cost swings with equipment, landlord work and existing conditions that have little to do with the design effort. That is why small restaurant fit-outs are usually quoted as a fixed fee for a defined scope instead of a percentage.

How project type changes the fee

This comparison comes from our own proposals across 324 projects from 2020 to 2026. For each type we divided the middle fee by the middle fee for an inline fit-out, the project most owners compare against. Only types with at least three proposals are shown, from smallest to largest.

  • Second-generation remodel. Reworking an existing restaurant, or taking over a former one, came in at about three quarters of an inline fit-out across 146 proposals. A full brand conversion of a drive-thru building can reach about twice an inline fit-out. Reusing the hood, grease line and restrooms keeps it low, if the survey proves they can be reused. Our guide to second-generation restaurant space explains the checks.
  • Remodel program, per store. When one owner remodels several stores to one brand standard, the fee per store came in at about 85 percent of an inline fit-out across 131 proposals, not lower than a one-off remodel. Each store still gets its own survey and sealed set. Our guide to multi-unit rollouts covers how that works.
  • Change of use conversion. Turning a bank or other non-restaurant space into a restaurant came in at about 90 percent of an inline fit-out across 5 proposals. Most date from 2020 to 2023, while the inline fit-outs are mostly recent, so the gap is probably smaller today. The building code class usually changes, so exits, plumbing fixture counts, accessibility and fire protection get a fresh look.
  • Inline fit-out. A full kitchen and dining room in a shopping center or storefront suite: survey, architecture, and mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineering. This is the baseline, drawn from 15 proposals that sat close together.
  • Counter or kiosk fit-out. Food court, kiosk, mall and airport units came in at about the same fee as an inline fit-out across 8 proposals, from about half to about three times, despite the smaller room. Landlord design review in malls, airports and transit hubs adds work, covered in our guide to small-footprint restaurants.
  • Ground-up drive-thru with site work. A whole building on a pad site with a drive-thru lane came in at about one and a third times an inline fit-out across 15 proposals, up to about three times, before the civil engineer, whom our proposals leave to others. Most date from 2020 to 2023. See building a restaurant on a pad site.
  • Multi-level urban restaurant. Stairs, more exits, exhaust routed up through the building and more structural work. Across 4 proposals these came in at roughly one and a half to two and a half times an inline fit-out, about 1.7 times in the middle.

Two projects of the same type can still differ widely once the site is known, so treat these as proportions, not prices.

What moves a fee up or down

Within any project type, these change the number most.

  • Survey and scan. A site visit and 3D scan of the space cost money up front and save far more later. A proposal without one is cheaper and riskier. See why every project starts with a survey.
  • Engineering disciplines included. Structural and mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineering can be in the fee or left for you to hire. That single difference often explains most of a gap between two quotes.
  • Structural work. New rooftop units, a heavier exhaust fan or a cut in the roof can need framing checks and sealed structural drawings. When the framing is hidden, we price that part after the ceiling is opened rather than guess. More in our guide to rooftop units and structure.
  • Site work. Parking, a drive-thru lane, grading or a grease interceptor outside the building add a site plan and usually a civil engineer.
  • Brand review rounds. Each round of comments from the franchisor’s design team is more drawing time. See how franchise brand approvals work.
  • Number of jurisdictions. Building, health, fire, zoning and sometimes a landlord or historic board each review the drawings. More reviewers, more submissions.
  • Resubmittals. Answering plan review comments is real work. Proposals differ on whether it is included and how many rounds.
  • Construction-phase services. Answering the contractor’s questions, reviewing submittals and site visits are often billed separately. See what your architect does during construction.

Fixed fee, hourly or percentage

There are three common ways to set the fee.

A fixed fee covers a defined scope for one agreed price, paid in installments at milestones. Ours are set up this way, usually with installments at signing, the start of construction documents and permit approval. It is the most common structure for restaurant fit-outs because both sides know the cost in advance.

Hourly billing suits work whose size cannot be known in advance, such as answering field questions during construction or investigating conditions found during demolition. In our proposals, work outside the written scope is billed hourly at an attached rate schedule, and only once you approve it.

A percentage of construction cost is more common on larger new buildings. Its weakness on a fit-out is that the fee rises when equipment or finishes get more expensive, even if the design work does not.

On multi-store programs, a fixed fee per site is common, sometimes with a separate fee for adapting the prototype once.

What is usually excluded

Most restaurant architecture proposals leave out some work that still has to be done by someone: civil engineering and site plans, zoning board applications, fire sprinkler and fire alarm design, hood fire suppression design, kitchen equipment selection and sign permits. Our guide to what’s in a restaurant architect’s proposal walks through the exclusions and assumptions in detail, and who does what on a restaurant project explains who picks each one up.

How to compare two proposals

Two fees only compare fairly when they cover the same work. Put the proposals side by side and check:

  1. Survey. Is there a site visit and scan, or is the design based on landlord drawings?
  2. Engineering. Which disciplines are included, and are the drawings sealed?
  3. Approvals. Does the fee include brand review rounds, plan review resubmittals and health department submissions? How many?
  4. Construction. Are contractor questions, submittal reviews and site visits included, capped or hourly?
  5. Exclusions. List every item each proposal excludes, then price what you would have to buy separately.
  6. Assumptions. What would trigger extra fees, such as a new prototype version mid-design?
  7. Payment schedule. How much is due up front, and what milestones release the rest?

Once both proposals cover the same scope, the lower total is a real saving. Until then, it is often just a shorter list. Our guide on how to choose a restaurant architect covers the questions to ask beyond the paperwork.

Questions

Common Questions

Is an architect's fee a percentage of construction cost?

Sometimes, mostly on larger new buildings. One RSMeans model for a new 5,000-square-foot restaurant uses a 7 percent architectural-fee allowance based on 2019 national data; it is not a current quote or a fit-out benchmark. Small restaurant fit-outs are more often quoted as one fixed fee for a defined scope.

Why do two architects quote such different fees for the same restaurant?

Usually because they are quoting different work. One may include the survey, structural, mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineering and plan review responses, while the other leaves them out for you to buy separately. Compare the scope line by line before comparing the totals.

Does a franchise prototype make the architect cheaper?

It helps, because the layout, finishes and equipment are already designed. Each site still needs its own survey, code review, engineering and sealed permit set, so the saving is real but not total.

What costs extra beyond the architect's fee?

Commonly civil engineering and site plans, zoning board applications, fire sprinkler and fire alarm design, sign permits, and services during construction if the proposal bills them separately. The exclusions list in the proposal tells you which.
