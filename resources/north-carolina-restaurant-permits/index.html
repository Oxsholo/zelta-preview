Permits by State guide

Opening a Restaurant in North Carolina: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In North Carolina, the city or county inspections department reviews the building under the statewide North Carolina State Building Code, and fire safety is reviewed locally. Kitchen plans go to the state's Plan Review Unit if the restaurant is a chain or franchise with ten or more locations, and to the county health department if it is independent. Zoning and other approvals depend on the site and the scope of work.

In North Carolina, three different reviewers look at a restaurant, and the second one is decided by a rule many owners do not expect. The building is reviewed by the city or county inspections department. The kitchen is reviewed either by the state or by the county health department, and which one depends on whether the restaurant is a chain, not on where it is. Fire safety is reviewed locally, often by the fire marshal. Zoning, driveway, landlord and sewer approvals are added on top, depending on the site and the scope of work.

We are licensed in North Carolina, and our restaurants there include our Dave’s Hot Chicken in Shelby, our Dave’s Hot Chicken in Greenville and our Dunkin’ and Baskin-Robbins in Statesville. The sequence below is how we plan a North Carolina job.

Who reviews what

Each reviewer has its own application, fee and schedule, so the first job is to find out which offices apply to your address.

  • Building. The city or county inspections department reviews the drawings against the North Carolina State Building Code. According to the Office of State Fire Marshal, the 2018 edition is still in effect while the start date of the 2024 edition has been delayed, so confirm which edition your office is enforcing.
  • Health. Chain and franchise restaurants go to the state; independent restaurants go to the county. The next section explains the test.
  • Fire. Fire plans are reviewed locally, and the route varies by office. On our Shelby restaurant, fire plan review meant contacting the fire marshal directly rather than relying on the building submission.
  • Zoning and site. The city or county planning department, plus state transportation approval when a driveway on a state road changes.

Step 1: Zoning

North Carolina cities zone the land inside their limits and, in some places, a band just outside them. Counties zone the rest where they have adopted zoning. In a few places, such as Durham, the city and county run joint planning and inspections departments.

For a restaurant in an existing shopping center unit, zoning is often a staff check that the use is allowed. On a recent chicken fit-out in an eastern North Carolina shopping center, the building application and the zoning application were filed on the same day. When a planner tells you no site plan is needed, ask for that in writing, because a verbal answer does not help when a building reviewer later asks the same question.

A new freestanding building or a drive-thru is different. Site plan review, driveway access and sometimes a state transportation permit all come into play. Our building types guide explains how a drive-thru pad changes who reviews you.

Step 2: The building permit

Plan on a North Carolina licensed architect for most restaurant work. G.S. 83A-13 separates architectural-license exemptions from the professional-seal exemption. Subsection (c) lists when no architectural license is needed, including a commercial building valued at no more than $200,000, one of no more than 3,000 square feet, and an alteration that does not change the structural system, the exit pattern or the loads on the structure. Subsection (c1) separately exempts a commercial project from the architect’s seal when it is both under $200,000 in total value and under 3,000 square feet. Check the subsection that applies to your scope. The engineering law is separate, so the kitchen’s mechanical, electrical and plumbing work can still need an engineer’s seal. Our guide on whether you need an architect walks through the exemptions.

Three things happen in order, and they are easy to blur together:

  1. Contractor setup. Under G.S. 87-1, a licensed general contractor is required on projects costing $40,000 or more. Some offices also want the contractor registered in their own system first. In Durham, the general contractor needed a city contractor ID for the online permitting system before it could file, so ask for that account as soon as the contractor is chosen.
  2. Application submission. In Durham, the building application had to be filed by the general contractor and its subcontractors, and it asked for the contractor’s name and license, owner and landlord information, the suite number, the construction value and the owner’s signature.
  3. Permit issuance. Filing is not approval. The permit is issued only after plan review is approved, fees are paid and any other conditions the office sets are met. Some offices also wait for zoning or health sign-off, so ask what has to be in hand before the permit is released.

None of the contractor and owner items come from the architect. We prepare the drawings and the application as far as they go, then list exactly what is still owed and who owes it. In a multi-tenant center, the landlord’s approval of the tenant plans can be one of those items.

Step 3: Health plan review, state or county

The test is set by the state, not left to each county. Under G.S. 130A-248 and the state’s 2022 position statement, a restaurant counts as a chain or franchise if it operates under the same name and concept with common ownership or management as ten or more food establishments, or under a franchise agreement with the same franchisor as ten or more. Those plans go to the NC DHHS Plan Review Unit. Independent restaurants go to the county health department. Out-of-state chains count their locations elsewhere too.

Two consequences matter for multi-unit owners. Once the state approves a chain’s plans, the statute says no county health department may require further plan approval; a county can suggest changes but cannot impose them without the state’s written approval. And sending plans to the wrong office delays approval, which is why the state wrote the definition down. The county still inspects and permits the finished restaurant.

The health application needs the operator as much as the architect. On our North Carolina jobs, it has asked for:

  • The operator’s signature
  • Planned construction dates
  • The seat count, and whether the job is new or a remodel
  • The menu, with equipment specification sheets
  • The plan review fee

File it as soon as the drawings allow, not after the building permit comes back. Our health department plan review guide covers the full packet.

Former restaurants and conversions

Reusing a restaurant building can keep the permit path simple. Our Shelby project was an interior and exterior renovation of an existing freestanding restaurant, with no change of use, exits or occupancy and no change to the footprint or site. The city accepted it as a change of tenant building permit.

Converting retail space is a bigger job. A chicken restaurant we designed in an eastern North Carolina shopping center replaced a phone store, so the space moved into A-2, the code’s class for restaurants. A conversion like that usually means new kitchen exhaust, plumbing and grease infrastructure, and a closer look at exits and restrooms. Our guide to second-generation restaurant space compares the two paths.

Inspections and opening

The inspections department inspects each trade and issues the certificate of occupancy after the final inspections pass. The health department inspects the finished kitchen and issues the food service permit before you can open, and the fire marshal may do its own final walk through. Ask each office at the start what it needs to see at the end.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Confirm whether the property is under city or county zoning, and whether the departments are combined.
  • Count your concept’s locations to know whether health review goes to the state or the county.
  • Choose the general contractor early, and make sure it can set up any local permitting account.
  • Ask the fire marshal how it wants to receive the fire plans.
  • Get the menu, equipment list, seat count and construction dates ready for the health application.
  • In a shopping center, ask what the landlord must approve before the tenant plans can be filed.

Questions

Common Questions

Who reviews a restaurant's kitchen plans in North Carolina, the state or the county?

It depends on the restaurant, not the county. The state's Plan Review Unit reviews chain and franchise restaurants tied to ten or more locations under the same name or franchisor. Independent restaurants go to the county health department. Once the state approves a chain's plans, the county cannot require further plan approval.

Do I need an architect for a restaurant in North Carolina?

For most restaurant work, yes. G.S. 83A-13 separates architectural-license exemptions from the professional-seal exemption. Check the applicable subsection and the separate engineering requirements for your scope, and confirm with the building department what it accepts.

Do I need a contractor before I can file for a restaurant permit in North Carolina?

Usually, yes. A licensed general contractor is required for projects costing $40,000 or more, and building applications generally name the contractor and its license. Some cities also make the contractor set up its own permitting account before it can file.

Who issues a restaurant building permit in North Carolina?

The city or county inspections department that covers the property. Some cities and counties combine their departments, so one office may handle both. They all enforce the same North Carolina State Building Code.
