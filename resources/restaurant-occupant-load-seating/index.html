How Many Seats Can a Restaurant Have? Occupant Load Explained

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Occupant load is the number of people the building code assumes a space can hold, worked out room by room from floor area and how each room is used. For a restaurant it feeds the number of exits, how the doors swing and latch, the restroom count and sometimes sprinklers. Several of those tests change at 50 people under the model code, but each has its own scope, so the line is worth planning on purpose.

Occupant load is the number of people the building code assumes can be in your restaurant at once. It is not a guess about your busiest lunch and not the number of chairs you plan. It is calculated from the floor area of each room, divided by a factor the code sets for how that room is used, and then added up.

That number feeds a surprising amount of the design: how many exits you need, which way the doors swing, whether they need panic bars, how many restrooms you need and, in some buildings, whether you need sprinklers or an alarm. The seat count you end up with is a result of all of that, so it pays to understand the number before the layout is drawn.

The figures below come from the 2021 International Building Code’s means of egress chapter, the exits chapter. Most states adopt the IBC with amendments, some use other editions, and New York City writes its own code, so we confirm the adopted version for every address.

How the number is worked out

The code assigns each kind of space a load factor, the floor area allowed per person, in Table 1004.5. The calculation goes room by room.

  • Dining with loose tables and chairs counts at 15 square feet of net floor area per person. Net area is the occupied floor, leaving out unoccupied accessory areas such as corridors, stairs, toilet rooms, mechanical rooms and closets.
  • Booths and benches count by length under section 1004.6: one person per 24 inches of booth seat and one per 18 inches of bench without dividing arms.
  • Standing and waiting areas, like the queue in front of the counter, count at 5 square feet net per person, much denser than dining.
  • Commercial kitchens count at 200 square feet of gross floor area per person, because few people work in a large space.
  • Offices and storage have their own, looser factors.

Add the rooms together and you have the occupant load. The calculated figure is the minimum the code assumes. Putting fewer chairs in a dining room does not lower it, because the factor applies to the floor area. If you want a lower number, you usually need less dining or waiting area. Some codes let a building official approve a higher occupant load where the exits are sized for it, but not a lower one simply because you plan to seat fewer people.

Why 50 is the number to watch

Several separate tests change at 50 people, each with its own scope. For a quick-service restaurant, this is often the most important decision in the code analysis.

  • Classification. Restaurants are normally A-2, the code’s class for places that serve food and drink. Under section 303.1.1 of the IBC’s occupancy chapter, a building or tenant space used for assembly with fewer than 50 occupants is classed as business, Group B. That is a test of the whole tenant space.
  • Number of exits. Table 1006.2.1 lets a room or space in an assembly occupancy have a single exit when it holds 49 or fewer people and the common path of egress travel, the distance walked before two routes split, stays within 75 feet. Above that, the space needs at least two exits, separated so one fire is unlikely to block both.
  • Door swing. Under section 1010.1.2.1, side-hinged doors serving a room or area of 50 or more people must swing in the direction people leave.
  • Panic hardware. Under section 1010.2.9, swinging doors serving an assembly room of 50 or more people cannot have a latch or lock other than panic hardware, the push bar that opens with body pressure.
  • Posting. Section 1004.9 requires assembly rooms to post their occupant load in a conspicuous place near the main exit. That posted figure is the operating limit fire officials enforce.

Small stores often stay just under 50 by design: one exit, a standard door and simpler requirements. Others need the seats and accept the second exit. Both are legitimate. The mistake is drifting over the line late, when a patio, a counter bar or a bigger waiting area adds people the original plan did not count.

What else the occupant load drives

  • Restrooms. The plumbing code’s fixture count starts from the occupant load, staff included. See how many restrooms and sinks a restaurant needs.
  • Sprinklers and alarms. For A-2 spaces, the 2021 IBC requires sprinklers at an occupant load of 100 or more, among other triggers, and a manual fire alarm at 300 or more. See sprinklers, fire alarms and the fire marshal.
  • Parking and zoning. Many zoning codes set required parking by seats or by floor area, so adding seats can mean adding spaces, or seeking relief.
  • Health permit. Health departments often ask for the seat count on the plan review application, sometimes with interior and patio seats listed separately.

Patios

Section 1004.7 has the building official assign an occupant load to yards, patios and similar outdoor areas based on how they will be used. Where the patio serves people beyond the building’s own occupants and its exit path runs through the building, the building’s exits are sized for the building and patio together. A patio added late can push the inside over a threshold.

Exits are a path, not just a door

An exit only counts if the route to it works. Reviewers check the whole path, not just the door count. A common catch is a storage room whose only way out runs through the kitchen, which the fire codes restrict (see restaurant fire protection). The main entrance also has to be accessible (see restaurant ADA requirements).

Multi-level restaurants

Narrow city storefronts often put the dining room on another floor. Each floor then needs its own occupant load and its own exits, and dining on a floor other than the exit level can trigger sprinklers on its own. Our Popeyes near Union Square spreads seating for 37 across two levels, and our Popeyes in Greenwich Village puts seating upstairs with ordering at street level. See restaurants on more than one floor.

Choosing a seat count

The right seat count is a business decision made inside code limits, and brands have their own targets. Our published stores show the range, from seating for 26 at our Taco Bell on Westchester Square to 54 guests at our Taco Bell remodel on Page Avenue in Staten Island, a freestanding drive-thru.

Run the occupant load on the first test fit, before the brand approves the layout, so the seat count, exits and restrooms are decided together rather than fixed one at a time.

Questions

Common Questions

Is occupant load the same as my seat count?

No. Occupant load is calculated from floor area and use, so it covers dining, waiting and standing areas, the kitchen and staff. Your seats have to fit inside it, and putting fewer chairs in a dining room does not by itself lower the calculated number.

Why does 50 people matter so much for a restaurant?

Under the 2021 International Building Code, several separate tests change at 50: a tenant space under 50 is classed as business rather than A-2, a room of up to 49 may have one exit if travel distance allows, and rooms of 50 or more need outswinging doors and, in assembly spaces, panic hardware on latched doors.

Do patio seats count toward occupant load?

They can. The model code has the building official assign an occupant load to patios, and if people other than the building's occupants use the patio and its exit path runs through the building, the building's exits are sized for both. Patios can also affect restrooms, parking and the health permit.

Do I have to post the occupant load?

Yes, in most places. The model building code requires assembly rooms to post their occupant load in a conspicuous place near the main exit, and that posted number becomes the limit fire officials enforce.
