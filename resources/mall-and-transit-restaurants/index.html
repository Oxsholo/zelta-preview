Opening a Restaurant in a Mall or Transit Hub: Landlord Rules

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

In a mall, outlet center or transit hub, the landlord reviews and approves your drawings before the town will accept a permit application, and the landlord's rules often decide your equipment, rooftop units, signage and even which trades can work on site. Read the landlord's criteria and the lease's division of work before design starts, not after.

A restaurant in a mall, outlet center or transit hub answers to two authorities: the town (or the agency that runs the property) and the landlord. The landlord’s review usually comes first. At large managed centers your construction drawings and sign drawings go to the landlord for approval, and only after that approval can they go to the building department. Plan for two reviews in a row, not one.

The landlord’s rules also reach further than most owners expect. They can decide where your rooftop units sit, who installs your sprinklers, which kitchen equipment is acceptable and what union labor is required on site. The cheapest time to learn those rules is before the first layout.

What the landlord reviews, and when

Managed centers publish tenant criteria: a package of rules covering storefronts, signs, mechanical equipment, utilities and construction conduct. In our experience the review works like this:

  1. Landlord drawing review. Construction drawings and sign shop drawings (the sign maker’s fabrication drawings) go to the landlord’s tenant coordinator. At one New Jersey regional mall, nothing could go to the township until that office signed off.
  2. Authorization to file. Once approved, the landlord sends a letter to the town confirming the tenant may apply for permits. For signs, the same landlord issued its approval on company letterhead after reviewing the sign shop drawings.
  3. Town review. Building, and separately the health department, review the approved set.
  4. Pre-construction meeting. Before work starts, that mall required the general contractor to meet the landlord’s on-site operations manager.

If the landlord’s comments change the plan, the town set changes too. That is why we run landlord review to completion before filing.

The lease decides who does what

Every center splits the work between landlord and tenant differently, and the split drives the drawings. Patterns we have seen on real projects:

  • Utilities. On one Queens project the landlord had to deliver a large electrical service and gas to the space. Elsewhere, a new electrical subpanel was tenant scope, not landlord scope.
  • Rooftop units. One landlord supplied the rooftop units in fixed sizes, so our engineers designed the system to those sizes. Another required the units on raised steel supports rather than directly on the roof. A third picked the exact wall location and mounting height for a supplemental unit.
  • Sprinklers. A Staten Island landlord asked the tenant to use the landlord’s own sprinkler contractor and amend the landlord’s existing sprinkler drawing.
  • Storefront structure. One landlord had no structural drawings for the storefront and made producing them the tenant’s obligation.
  • Signs. Responsibility for shared tenant panel signs can sit with either party, and we have seen it stay unresolved deep into a project.

None of this is code. It is the lease and the criteria package, and it needs to be settled in writing before engineering starts.

Kitchen rules stricter than code

Landlords protect their shared systems. At one New Jersey mall, every food tenant needed its own secondary grease interceptor, a tank that traps kitchen grease, in addition to whatever the center already had. Exhaust routing, roof penetrations and hood fans get similar scrutiny because they touch the landlord’s roof and neighbors.

Transit hubs: another layer again

Transit properties add their own operator and, sometimes, a public agency. Our two Dunkin’ shops in the Oculus at the World Trade Center (the World Trade Center store and the concourse store) show how many extra rules a hub can bring:

  • The center required all kitchen equipment to be NSF certified, a food equipment safety standard, or an approved equivalent. Anything that was not had to be swapped for an alternate.
  • The property’s chief engineer required union electrical labor for the work.
  • The center’s sustainability checklist applied by size. At the time, spaces under 1,000 square feet were exempt; one of the two units fell under that line and the other sat just over it.
  • On another nearby restaurant under the same center operator, the operator required approval through its decor vendor before anything could be fabricated, and the public agency reviewing the permit wanted the brand’s full menu included with the submission.

Transit kiosks are also small and built for people walking past, which shapes the layout as much as any rule. The Oculus stores use full glass fronts and long counters so commuters can see the case and order without slowing down.

Outlets, pads and new centers

Not every center works the same way.

  • Outlet centers on special land. At an outlet center on tribal land in Connecticut, the approvals came from the tribal nation’s own land use commission and health department, not a town. Each contractor then filed its own trade permit.
  • Pad sites at a mall. A drive-thru pad on a Maryland mall property needed site plan approval before the county would accept the building permit. The landlord reviewed the site plan, then the brand, and the county warned that needing more than five parking spaces would trigger a parking study of the entire mall.
  • New shopping centers. In a center still under construction, the tenant filing can wait on the landlord’s own base building approval. On one South Carolina project our submission sat on hold while the landlord’s base building approval was still pending.

Food courts and concourses

Food court units share seating, often share grease and exhaust infrastructure, so the landlord’s criteria tend to be detailed. Our published work includes a Wendy’s in the Green Acres Mall food court, a Popeyes in a Bridgewater mall food court and a 375º Chicken ‘N Fries on the concourse of Newark’s Gateway Center, each laid out for quick counter traffic rather than long sit-down meals.

How to keep the landlord off your critical path

  • Get the full tenant criteria package and the lease exhibit that splits landlord and tenant work before schematic design.
  • Ask for the landlord’s base building drawings and existing conditions. Our survey step always includes the landlord drawings.
  • Confirm utility capacity, rooftop space and grease requirements in writing.
  • Submit sign shop drawings early. Sign approval often runs on its own track.
  • Ask whether the property has its own permitting agency, labor rules or equipment standards.

Questions

Common Questions

Do I need landlord approval before applying for a building permit in a mall?
Usually yes. At large managed centers the landlord reviews the construction drawings and sign drawings first, then authorizes you to apply to the town. Filing before that approval tends to mean filing twice.
Who pays for utilities and rooftop units in a mall restaurant space?
The lease decides. Some landlords deliver gas, electrical service or the rooftop units themselves; others leave all of it to the tenant. We confirm the split in writing before engineering starts, because it changes the drawings.
Are transit hub restaurants permitted differently from street storefronts?
Often. A transit property can have its own operator rules, its own submission requirements and its own labor or equipment standards on top of the building code. Some even sit under a public agency rather than the local building department.
Can a mall require a grease interceptor even if the code does not?
Yes. Landlords can set requirements stricter than code. At one New Jersey mall we worked in, every food tenant needed its own secondary grease interceptor in addition to the center's system.
