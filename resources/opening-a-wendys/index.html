Opening a Wendy's: Design, Permits and Build-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Opening a Wendy's starts with the building type: a freestanding store with a drive-thru, an inline store or a mall food court unit. Each needs a different set of approvals before the building permit, then the same core work: a layout drawn from the brand's design package, a code analysis of the specific building and coordinated kitchen engineering.

To open a Wendy’s you need a site that suits one of the brand’s formats, a layout drawn from its design package and approved by the brand, and the local approvals that format triggers: zoning and site plan review for a freestanding drive-thru, landlord review for a mall unit, and a building permit and health department review for all of them. The building type decides the order and the timeline more than anything else.

We have designed Wendy’s restaurants since 2020 across New York and New Jersey, including stores in Passaic and Jersey City, New Jersey, and a mall food court counter on Long Island. This guide covers design, permits and build-out. For investment figures, read Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document, the estimated initial investment table required by the FTC Franchise Rule; the FTC’s guide to buying a franchise explains how to read it.

Formats and their paths

Freestanding, often with a drive-thru. The classic Wendy’s: its own building, parking and usually a drive-thru lane. Our Wendy’s in Passaic, New Jersey is freestanding, with the brand’s red tower, a wood-slat pergola at the entry, an open ceiling and tall windows over booth, table and counter seating, and a service counter under digital menu boards. A freestanding store answers to zoning and often a planning board before the building department sees it. The site plan carries the lane, the queue, the menu board, curb cuts, parking and the sign tower. See drive-thru design for the lane and queue.

Mall food court. Our Wendy’s in the Green Acres Mall food court in Valley Stream, New York, fits into 811 square feet with wood cladding, a geometric backsplash and suspended menu boards, laid out for food court orders and mobile pickup. Here the mall landlord reviews the drawings before the town does, and the landlord’s tenant criteria set the rules for grease, rooftop equipment, utilities and signs (see malls and transit hubs).

Inline stores. Our Jersey City store uses a straightforward service layout with branded finishes and customer seating. Inline and storefront units trade site plan review for building questions, chiefly where the kitchen exhaust can go (see getting exhaust out of a city storefront).

Finding a site that works

The most common early question on a freestanding site is whether a drive-thru can be permitted at all. A site where it cannot may still work, but it changes the store’s sales model, so it is best known before a purchase or lease is final.

Questions to answer before committing:

  • Does zoning allow a drive-thru restaurant, and does it need a variance, special permit or site plan approval?
  • Is there room for the drive-thru queue without blocking parking aisles or the street?
  • For an inline unit, can kitchen exhaust reach the roof?
  • For a mall unit, what do the landlord’s tenant criteria require for grease, rooftop units and signs?

Our site feasibility guide covers test fits and due diligence, and our lease checklist covers the building facts to confirm in writing.

Converting an existing building

A Wendy’s site may come with an existing restaurant building. A former restaurant can bring a usable kitchen layout, grease interceptor, gas service and a site already approved for food service. It can also bring an outdated hood, undersized utilities or a layout that fights the brand’s kitchen. We compare the existing building against the Wendy’s package and the current code before recommending reuse (see former restaurant or retail space).

A conversion also has to look like a Wendy’s. Expect new exterior finishes and signage to the brand standard, which can trigger the town’s sign and design review even if the building footprint does not change.

Working from the brand package

Wendy’s, like most national brands, provides a design package covering the kitchen, finishes, equipment and signage. Our job is to fit that package to the real building and to the local code, then carry it through the brand’s approvals (see how franchise brand approvals work). Packages change over time, so confirm which version is approved for your location before design starts, and keep the set on that version.

Each format uses the package differently. A freestanding store uses the full exterior, with the tower, canopy and sign package, and that exterior has to clear the town’s sign code. A food court unit uses little of the exterior and much more of the landlord’s storefront rules. In every format, the equipment schedule drives the power, gas, water and drains, so settle it before the engineering starts.

Code questions decided by the building

The brand package does not settle the code; the building does. A few examples of what changes from site to site:

  • Fire alarm. The International Building Code (2021 edition, section 907.2.1) ties the fire alarm requirement for restaurants to the occupant load, so the answer depends on the building’s size and layout, and local amendments can change it. See fire protection in a restaurant.
  • Restrooms and fixtures. The required count follows the occupant load (International Plumbing Code, section 403.1). Occupant load, the number of people the code assumes are inside, comes from the floor area of each part of the store and its use, not simply from the seat count.
  • Accessibility. The counter, seating, restroom and the route from parking are checked on every site (see accessibility in small restaurants).
  • Kitchen engineering. Hood, grease and gas depend on the building and the local utility as much as the brand (see kitchen engineering).

Permits in New York and New Jersey

Most of our Wendy’s work is in New York and New Jersey, and the two work differently. In New Jersey, zoning and any board approvals come first, then a construction permit under the statewide code (see New Jersey permits). In New York City, the Department of Buildings filing comes with its own set of related approvals (see NYC permits). Outside the city, each town or village runs its own building department (see New York outside the city).

Questions

Common Questions

Does every Wendy's need a drive-thru?

No. Our Wendy's work includes freestanding stores and a mall food court counter. Whether a drive-thru is possible depends on zoning and the lot, and some sites cannot permit one, so find out before you buy or lease.

Can an existing restaurant building be converted to a Wendy's?

Often, and it can save time if the building's use, kitchen infrastructure and site work suit the brand. The building still has to be brought to the brand's design package and checked against today's code.

Does a Wendy's need a fire alarm system?

It depends on the building, the occupancy, the occupant load and the local code, not the brand. Model codes tie the requirement for restaurants to the number of occupants, and local amendments can change it, so every site needs its own analysis.

How long does it take to permit a Wendy's?

It depends on the site. A food court unit answers to the mall landlord first, while a freestanding drive-thru often needs zoning or site plan approval before the building permit can be filed.
