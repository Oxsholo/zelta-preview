Do I Need an Architect to Open a Restaurant?

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

For a new restaurant, or a fit-out that changes the layout, kitchen or exits, usually yes: the building department will want drawings sealed by a design professional licensed in that state, and in New Jersey the building design must come from an architect. Exemptions exist, but most cover small, low-cost work or buildings that are not used for gatherings, and the local building official decides whether yours qualifies.

For most restaurants, yes. A new restaurant, a conversion of a retail space, or a remodel that moves walls, adds kitchen equipment or changes the exits usually needs construction drawings sealed by a design professional licensed in that state before the building department will issue a permit. In some states, New Jersey among them, the building design has to come from an architect.

The exemptions in state law are real, but they are narrower than most owners hope. Most cover small, low-cost work, buildings that are not used for gatherings, or alterations that leave structure and exits alone. Below is what the law says in six of the states where we work, with links to the official text, how to check that an architect can legally seal drawings in your state, and what to do if you think your project might be exempt.

Three separate questions

“Do I need an architect” is really three questions, and they have different answers.

  • Who may prepare the design? That is set by each state’s architecture and engineering licensing laws.
  • Do the drawings need a seal? That comes from the same licensing laws plus the state building code. A seal is a licensed professional’s signed statement that they prepared or supervised the drawings.
  • Will the permit be issued? That is the local building official’s decision, made inside the limits the state sets.

An exemption in one of those does not settle the others, which is why the building department’s answer matters more than anyone’s general rule.

Why restaurants usually need sealed drawings

The model International Building Code, which most states adopt with amendments, puts restaurants in A-2, the code’s class for places that serve food and drink. A small restaurant or tenant space with fewer than 50 occupants is classed as business instead, under section 303.1.1. That lighter class does not always remove the need for a design professional: New Jersey, for example, lists business buildings as architect work too.

The bigger driver is the scope of work. A cooking line needs an exhaust hood, make-up air (outside air replacing what the hood removes), fire suppression and often new gas and electrical service. New seats can change the exits and the restroom count. New sinks and floor drains usually bring a grease interceptor, which keeps fats out of the sewer (see kitchen engineering). Turning a shop or office into a restaurant reopens the code analysis for the whole space (see former restaurant or retail space). Changes to structure, exits and engineering systems are exactly what most exemptions leave out.

What the law says in six states

Each state sets its own rule. We checked these against the official text in October 2026. They are summaries, not legal advice, and in every case the local building official decides whether a particular project fits.

  • New Jersey. The Department of Community Affairs’ Bulletin 96-2, revised December 2023, lists A-2 assembly buildings and B business buildings as work for an architect. Either an architect or an engineer may sign and seal the engineering systems, such as structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing and fire protection. Each professional seals the part they prepared. Seals are not required when the construction official waives plans for minor work, for an owner’s own single-family house, or for what the code calls Class 3 structures, where licensed trade contractors may prepare plans for their own trade.
  • New York. Education Law Section 7307 exempts alterations costing $10,000 or less inside New York City and $20,000 or less elsewhere in the state, but only when they do not involve changes affecting structural or public safety. The state’s Office of the Professions says the dollar limit alone does not decide it, and that the authority having jurisdiction makes the call.
  • Pennsylvania. Under the Uniform Construction Code, 34 Pa. Code Section 403.42a requires construction documents to be prepared by a licensed architect or licensed professional engineer. An unlicensed person may prepare documents for a remodel or alteration only without compensation, and only when the work does not add to the building or change its structure or means of egress.
  • North Carolina. G.S. 83A-13 has several separate exemptions. Commercial buildings valued at no more than $200,000, or no larger than 3,000 square feet, are exempt from architectural licensing. A separate subsection exempts a commercial project from the architect’s seal only when it is both under $200,000 and under 3,000 square feet. Alterations that do not change the structure, the exits or the loads are also exempt. The engineering law, Chapter 89C, is separate, so engineering work can still need an engineer. Our North Carolina guide covers the rest of the state’s approvals.
  • Tennessee. The state board’s seal exemptions clarification lets some tenant improvements in business, mercantile, storage and similar occupancies be designed without a registered professional, within size and scope limits. It states that in no case can anyone other than a Tennessee registered architect or engineer prepare design documents for assembly occupancies.
  • Florida. Section 481.229 of the Florida Statutes exempts buildings costing less than $25,000 from needing an architect, except schools, auditoriums and other buildings intended for public use.

The pattern: every exemption is small in cost or size, limited to buildings that are not gathering places, or conditional on leaving structure, exits and public safety alone. A restaurant kitchen rarely meets all of those at once.

When you might not need one

Some restaurant work genuinely does not need a design professional. Repainting, new flooring, new furniture in the same layout and swapping a piece of equipment for an identical one often need no drawings, or only a trade permit. A sign is usually handled by the sign company under its own permit (see restaurant signage).

Before you rely on that, send the building department a short written description of the work and ask whether drawings are required. Keep the answer. Our guide to restaurant remodels explains which kinds of change trigger fuller review.

The seal must come from your state

Architects are licensed by each state, not nationally. A seal is valid only in the state that issued the license, so an architect licensed in New Jersey cannot seal drawings for a restaurant in Georgia until Georgia licenses them too. Engineers work the same way, so the engineers sealing your mechanical, electrical, plumbing or structural sheets need licenses in your state as well.

For an architect already licensed in one state, most states offer a faster route to a license in another, often through a certificate from the National Council of Architectural Registration Boards, the national body that keeps architects’ records. The certificate is not a license. The architect still applies to, pays and renews with each state, and some states also require the firm, not just the individual, to register before it offers architecture there. NCARB’s licensing requirements tool lists each jurisdiction’s rules.

To check an architect, search their name on your state architecture board’s license lookup and confirm the license is active. Then ask:

  • Which states are you licensed in today, and in whose name?
  • Is the firm registered in my state where that is required?
  • Who seals the engineering drawings, and are they licensed here?
  • If my rollout adds a state you are not licensed in, what is your plan, and when will the license be in place?

Our guides to choosing a restaurant architect and opening many locations go further.

What you get besides the seal

The seal is the legal minimum. Owners hire a restaurant architect for more: a survey of the real space before design, a layout that satisfies the brand, the landlord and the code together, coordinated engineering, answers to plan review comments, and support during construction. Questions

Common Questions

Can an engineer seal my restaurant drawings instead of an architect?
It depends on the state. New Jersey reserves restaurant buildings for architects, though either an architect or an engineer may seal engineering systems such as mechanical, electrical and plumbing. Pennsylvania and Tennessee accept either an architect or an engineer for the design documents.
Can an architect licensed in another state seal my drawings?
No. A seal counts only in the state that issued the license. An architect licensed elsewhere has to obtain a license in your state first, and some states also require the firm itself to register before offering architecture there.
How do I check whether an architect is licensed in my state?
Every state architecture board has a public license lookup on its website. Search the architect's name, confirm the license is active, and ask whether the firm also has to be registered in that state.
Who decides if my project is exempt?
The building official where the restaurant is, within the limits state law sets. New York's licensing office, for example, says the authority having jurisdiction makes that call. Ask in writing and keep the answer.
