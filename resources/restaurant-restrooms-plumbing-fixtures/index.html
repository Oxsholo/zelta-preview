Restaurant Restroom Requirements: When Is One Enough?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant's restroom count comes from the plumbing code your state adopted, which divides the total occupant load, staff included, by ratios set for restaurants. Under the model International Plumbing Code, one shared restroom is enough only when the whole space holds 15 people or fewer; above that, separate facilities are required, though two single-user rooms open to everyone usually satisfy it. The health code then adds its own sinks.

The number of restrooms a restaurant needs is a calculation, not a judgment call. The plumbing code takes the occupant load of the space, the number of people the building code says it can hold, and divides it by ratios set for restaurants. The result is how many toilets, restroom sinks and other fixtures you need.

So when is one restroom enough? Under the model code most states start from, only in very small spaces: 15 people or fewer in total, staff included. Most dining rooms are above that, and the usual answer for a small quick-service store is two single-user restrooms that anyone can use. The health code then adds its own list of sinks, and the brand may add standards of its own. All of it has to agree on one floor plan.

The inputs you need

The answer depends on four things, so gather them first:

  1. The plumbing code for your address. Most states adopt the International Plumbing Code (IPC), some use the Uniform Plumbing Code, and many amend either. New York City writes its own. The figures below are from the 2021 IPC.
  2. The total occupant load. It comes from the building code, room by room: dining, waiting and standing areas, the kitchen and back of house. Staff count. It is not your seat count.
  3. Who uses the restrooms. Customers, staff or both, and whether the route to them stays out of the kitchen.
  4. The building. In a mall or food court, shared restrooms may count toward your total, depending on the code and the lease.

How the count is worked out

Table 403.1 of the 2021 IPC sets these minimums for restaurants:

  • Water closets (toilets): 1 per 75 people of each sex.
  • Lavatories (restroom sinks): 1 per 200 people of each sex.
  • Drinking fountains: 1 per 500, though the code lets a restaurant that serves drinking water free of charge skip the fountain.
  • Service sink: 1, the mop sink.

Section 403.1.1 has you split the occupant load in half for each sex and round fractions up.

A worked example, under the 2021 IPC and the 2021 International Building Code’s load factors: a quick-service store with 600 square feet of net dining area (40 people at 15 square feet each), 100 square feet of queue (20 people at 5 square feet each) and 1,000 square feet of kitchen (5 people at 200 square feet each) has an occupant load of 65. Half is 32.5, rounded up to 33 per sex. That needs 1 water closet and 1 lavatory for each sex, a service sink, and a drinking fountain unless free water is served. Because 65 is over 15, one shared room is not enough. Two single-user rooms, each with a toilet and sink and open to anyone, meet it.

Adding a counter bar or a patio can push the occupant load past the next threshold, so the count is rerun whenever the layout changes.

When one restroom is enough

Section 403.2 of the 2021 IPC requires separate facilities for each sex, then lists exceptions. The one that matters for small restaurants: separate facilities are not required in structures or tenant spaces with a total occupant load, employees and customers together, of 15 or fewer. Few dining rooms qualify, but a small takeout counter can. On a recent takeout fit-out in West Virginia with no seating, our code analysis put the occupant load at 7.

Other exceptions help larger stores. The 2021 IPC lets fixtures in single-user rooms count toward the required total when the rooms are open to all, which ICC’s summary of the change explains, and it allows multi-user rooms designed for all genders with private compartments. States amend these: Ohio, for example, rewrote the all-gender exception in its plumbing code rule. Some states and cities also set rules for how single-user rooms are signed. Check before the signage is ordered.

A quick-transaction store may need no customer restroom at all. The 2021 IPC’s public toilet section exempts takeout, pickup and drop-off spaces with a public area of 300 square feet or less. Ohio’s rule removes that exemption, which shows why the adopted code matters. Staff still need a restroom either way.

Accessibility

Under section 213.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards, each toilet room provided must be accessible, with narrow exceptions. Where several single-user rooms are clustered in one location, at least half must comply. In a small store, plan on every customer restroom being accessible. See restaurant ADA requirements for what that means in a small room.

Where the restroom can go

Location rules catch more layouts than the count does.

  • Not through the kitchen. The IPC says the route to public toilet facilities shall not pass through kitchens, storage rooms or closets.
  • Enclosed, self-closing doors. The FDA Food Code, which states adopt in various editions, requires toilet rooms to be completely enclosed with a tight-fitting, self-closing door. Health inspectors check it.
  • Visible to staff. A restroom the crew can see from the counter is easier to keep clean and safe. That is a design choice, not a code rule.

The sinks the health code adds

The kitchen has its own fixture list, set by the food code your health department enforces rather than the plumbing table. This list is based on the FDA Food Code; your state’s adopted version can differ.

  • Hand sinks located for convenient use in food preparation, food dispensing and dishwashing areas, and in or right next to every restroom.
  • A three-compartment sink for washing, rinsing and sanitizing by hand. A dishwasher does not automatically replace it, so confirm with your health department before dropping it.
  • A food prep sink when the menu washes or thaws food, where the health department requires one.
  • A mop sink, which the plumbing code also counts as the service sink.

Some of these drain through the grease interceptor, and every hand sink and dish sink needs hot water, so the water heater is sized for the sinks and equipment together. See kitchen engineering and health department plan review.

Brand standards and existing spaces

The code sets a minimum, and brands often add their own rules about customer restrooms, or grant exceptions for tight urban sites. Any brand exception still has to satisfy the local code. See how franchise brand approvals work.

A former restaurant often comes with restrooms, and owners hope to keep them. That works only if the count still fits your occupant load and the rooms are accessible. More seats than the last tenant had, or restrooms that were never accessible, can mean rebuilding them. A retail conversion usually needs new restrooms outright. See former restaurant or retail space.

Questions

Common Questions

Can a small restaurant have just one restroom?
Under the 2021 International Plumbing Code, only when the total occupant load, employees and customers together, is 15 or fewer. Some states and cities amend that limit, and New York City writes its own plumbing code, so check the code for your address.
Do employees count toward the restroom total?
Yes. The fixture count starts from the total occupant load of the space, which includes staff, the kitchen and waiting areas, not just the seats. Customers and staff can usually share the same restrooms if the route to them works.
Can customers walk through the kitchen to reach the restroom?
Generally no. The model plumbing code says the route to public toilet facilities shall not pass through kitchens, storage rooms or closets, and food codes require toilet rooms to be enclosed with tight-fitting, self-closing doors.
Does a takeout-only store need a customer restroom?
Sometimes not. The 2021 model plumbing code exempts quick-transaction spaces such as takeout and pickup with a public area of 300 square feet or less, but some states, Ohio among them, removed that exemption. Staff still need a restroom.
