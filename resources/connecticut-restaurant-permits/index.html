Permits by State guide

Opening a Restaurant in Connecticut: Permits and Approvals

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

Connecticut has no county government, so a restaurant's zoning, building permit and fire marshal review all come from the town, under one statewide building code and fire safety code. The kitchen goes to the town health department or a regional health district that may sit in another town. On tribal land, the tribal nation's own agencies take the place of the town, and other approvals depend on the site and scope.

In Connecticut, the routing question is simple because there is no county layer: the town or city handles zoning, issues the building permit and runs fire marshal review. The building official enforces the Connecticut State Building Code, which is the same in every town, and the local fire marshal reviews the plans against the state fire safety code before the permit is issued. The kitchen goes to the town health department or, in many places, a regional health district that serves several towns. On tribal land, the tribal nation replaces all of these. Other approvals, such as wetlands or historic district review, depend on the site.

That does not make every town alike. Each of Connecticut’s 169 towns writes its own zoning, runs its own commissions and has its own habits at the permit counter. We are licensed in Connecticut and have completed restaurants from Fairfield County to central Connecticut, including our Dunkin’ in Stamford and our Popeyes drive-thru in Meriden. This is how the approvals fit together.

Step 1: Zoning and the land use commissions

Zoning in Connecticut is run by each town’s land use commissions. Most towns have a combined planning and zoning commission; some keep planning and zoning separate. Alongside it you may meet:

  • The zoning board of appeals, which hears variance requests.
  • A special permit or site plan review, which many towns require for restaurants, drive-thrus or changes to the site.
  • The inland wetlands commission, if the site is near a wetland or watercourse.
  • A historic district commission, in towns with local historic districts, for exterior changes and signs.

For a fit-out inside an existing shopping center unit, the zoning step is often a staff sign-off that the use is allowed. A freestanding building with a drive-thru is different: the site plan, the lane, the order point and the signs can all come before the commission. Our Meriden Popeyes, for example, has twin drive-thru order canopies in its lot.

Sign rules are local and specific. In Stamford, the city exempts unlit signs of 16 square feet or less (Stamford sign regulation, 11.C.5(a)) from a separate zoning permit, so on one Stamford storefront we specified an unlit face for a small logo to keep it within that exemption. The exemption is from the zoning permit, not the sign standards. Our signage guide covers what towns look at on the sign side.

Step 2: The building permit

The building permit comes from the town building official under the Connecticut State Building Code. The Office of the State Building Inspector publishes the code edition and its Connecticut amendments; confirm which edition applies on the date you file. Codes are updated periodically, so we confirm the edition in force on the filing date. Confirm whether this scope requires sealed drawings and which Connecticut-licensed professional may prepare them; state law includes exemptions (see the architecture statute and supplements). Separately, state law gives the building official 30 days from application to issue or refuse the permit, reviewing the plans within that period.

Older strip centers raise a recurring question: can the existing roof carry new rooftop equipment? On a Stamford fit-out, roof support was part of the design work before the drawings were final. In an older building, plan for a structural engineer to evaluate the framing and design any reinforcement, because the answer can change the equipment layout. Accessibility upgrades in an existing building depend on the scope of work and on federal as well as state rules; our accessibility guide explains how scope drives them.

Step 3: The fire marshal

In Connecticut, the local fire marshal is a plan reviewer, not just an inspector. The same section 29-263 provides that, where applicable, the local fire marshal reviews the plans for compliance with the Fire Safety Code before the permit is issued and within the same 30-day period. For a restaurant, expect attention on exits, the fire alarm, sprinklers where the building has them, and the kitchen hood and its fire suppression system.

The fire marshal also inspects before the restaurant opens. Coordinating the hood installer, the suppression contractor and the alarm contractor so all three are ready on the same visit saves a trip.

Step 4: Health review

Food establishment licensing in Connecticut is local. The Department of Public Health’s food protection program says the state uses the FDA Food Code, and that local health departments and districts issue licenses and inspect food establishments. Districts cover groups of towns, so the office may not be in your town at all. The health office reviews the kitchen layout, equipment, sinks and finishes, and licenses the establishment before opening. Our health department plan review guide covers what goes in the packet.

Tribal land: a different set of agencies

Two federally recognized tribal nations in Connecticut have reservation land, and a restaurant there is reviewed by the tribal nation rather than a town. At Mashantucket, the tribal Land Use Commission describes itself as a one-stop permitting body, and its environmental health program handles food service permits and restaurant plan review under the FDA Food Code.

On a quick-service fit-out we designed on tribal land, the path looked like this:

  • A land use permit from the commission, in place of a town permit, with its building code and fire safety reviewers commenting on the drawings. The commission asked for responses as a written narrative of the changes plus a PDF of only the affected sheets.
  • Health review by the tribal environmental health office, which requires one certified food protection manager to be present during all hours of operation. That is a staffing question for the operator, so raise it before the application is signed.
  • Trade permits filed separately by each contractor once the land use permit was issued.

The shopping center’s own landlord review sat on top of all of this. Our mall and transit guide covers how landlord criteria shape a project like this one.

Inspections and the certificate of occupancy

The building official inspects the work and issues the certificate of occupancy after final inspections. The fire marshal and the health office each do their own final inspection, and the food license must be in hand before you serve food. Our certificate of occupancy guide covers the closeout.

A checklist before you sign the lease

  • Confirm whether the use needs a special permit or site plan review from the town’s commission.
  • Check whether the site is near wetlands or in a local historic district.
  • Read the town’s sign rules before the brand’s sign package is final.
  • Find out whether the town has its own health department or belongs to a health district.
  • In an older building, ask whether the roof can carry new rooftop equipment.
  • On tribal land, get the tribal nation’s tenant construction requirements in writing.

Questions

Common Questions

Does Connecticut have county permits for restaurants?

No. Connecticut has no county government, so zoning, building permits and fire marshal review come from the town or city. Health review comes from the town's health department or a regional health district that serves several towns.

What building code does Connecticut use for restaurants?

The Connecticut State Building Code, which applies in every town. Confirm the code edition applicable to your filing with the Office of the State Building Inspector.

Does the fire marshal review a restaurant in Connecticut?

Where the Fire Safety Code applies, which includes most restaurants, yes. State law has the local fire marshal review the plans for fire safety code compliance before the building permit is issued, within the same 30-day window the building official has to act.

Who approves a restaurant on tribal land in Connecticut?

The tribal nation's own agencies. At Mashantucket, for example, the tribal Land Use Commission is a one-stop permitting body whose reviewers include building code, fire safety and environmental health, in place of a town and health district.
