Restaurant Pickup Design: Mobile Orders, Drivers and Kiosks

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A quick-service restaurant now serves several kinds of customer at once: people ordering at the counter or a kiosk, app customers collecting a bag, delivery drivers, and sometimes a drive-thru or walk-up window. Good design aims to give each group its own spot, so pickup traffic stays out of the ordering line as much as the room allows. The pickup shelf, the kiosks and the driver hand-off belong in the first test fit, not added at the end.

A restaurant used to have one line. Now it has several: the counter, the kiosks, app orders waiting on a shelf, delivery drivers who want to collect and leave quickly, and on some sites a drive-thru or a walk-up window. Each group wants something different from the room. The layouts that work best give every group its own place and keep their paths from crossing where the space allows.

That has to be planned in the first test fit. A pickup shelf squeezed in after the counter is drawn usually ends up in the line. This guide is about how customers and drivers move through the store. How to fit the equipment and seating into a very small plan is covered in our guide to small-footprint restaurants.

Name every customer stream first

Before drawing anything, list the ways a customer can get food at your store:

  • Counter ordering
  • Self-order kiosks
  • Mobile or app pickup
  • Third-party delivery drivers
  • Drive-thru lane
  • Walk-up or pickup window
  • Seated dine-in

Then decide where each one starts and ends. At our Taco Bell in Manville, counter, kiosk and drive-thru ordering are all built into the plan, with a mural reading Order Ahead beside the app icon. At our Taco Bell in North Arlington, order kiosks, counter service, a mobile and delivery shelf and a late-night pickup window are all separate stations in 1,548 square feet.

The pickup shelf

The pickup shelf is the most important object in the room for app and delivery orders, and it is easy to place badly. The patterns we use most:

  • Near the entrance. At our Taco Bell near Union Square, a rolling mobile-order pickup shelf sits near the entrance, so customers collect without walking into the dining room.
  • By a side door. At Dave’s Hot Chicken in Sumter, a separate pickup shelf by the side door keeps mobile orders apart from the counter.
  • Past the register, in cubbies. At our Buffalo Wild Wings GO in the East Village, a wall of numbered cubbies past the register separates mobile and delivery pickup from the ordering line.
  • In its own nook. At our Popeyes on Page Avenue in Staten Island, a separate pickup nook keeps mobile orders away from the ordering line.
  • At a second counter. At our Popeyes on East 14th Street in the East Village, a second pickup counter sits in a small back hallway.

Brands often set rules. On our Taco Bell in Kingsbridge, the brand’s direction was that the mobile pickup unit sit on the front counter or as a stand-up unit in the dining room. Ask for the brand’s current standard before the test fit.

Where a store has both a pickup window and an in-store pickup point, consider whether one set of shelving can serve both from the kitchen side. Staff then load one place, and the shelf takes less floor.

Delivery drivers

Drivers are a different customer from app pickups. They arrive in bursts, often carry several orders and want to be gone quickly. A hand-off that works has:

  • A defined spot that is not the ordering counter, so drivers do not join the customer line.
  • Waiting room for more than one driver at the busiest hour, clear of the entrance and the exit path.
  • A clear view from the kitchen to the hand-off, so staff can see who is waiting.
  • Order labeling and staging close to the hand-off, so bags are not searched for at the counter.

In tight urban stores, the common mistake is sizing the counter for the ordering line alone, so drivers end up standing in it. Draw the drivers into the test fit at peak, not as an afterthought.

On suburban sites, drivers also need to park. On the Manville Taco Bell, the brand asked for two or three dedicated parking stalls close to the side entry for delivery drivers and mobile pickup customers. That keeps drivers out of both the drive-thru lane and the fire lane.

Kiosks

Self-order kiosks move people off the counter line, but they create their own line, and they take floor space. Where we put them:

  • At the entry, with seating behind. At Taco Bell on Westchester Square, kiosks stand near the entrance and seating for 26 fills the back, keeping the ordering line apart from guests already eating.
  • Along a wall. At our Popeyes in the Bridgewater food court, an orange “Order here” wall holds a row of kiosks beside the counter.
  • Near the entrance in co-branded stores. At our Dunkin’ and Jimmy John’s near Herald Square, kiosks near the entrance spread out the lines for the two brands during rush periods.

Accessibility at every station

Each station is a separate service, and each has to work for a customer using a wheelchair. The 2010 ADA Standards say that at least one of each type of service counter must have an accessible portion (section 227.3), and the guidance for that section names order and pick-up as different services, so an accessible ordering counter does not cover an inaccessible pickup counter. Where customers serve themselves from shelves, at least half of each type, and at least one, must be within the reach ranges in section 308 (section 227.4). Controls that customers operate, such as a kiosk screen, generally need to be within reach ranges with clear floor space in front (sections 205 and 309). Queues serving accessible counters need an accessible route too.

Your state’s building code may add to these, so we confirm the counter, shelf and kiosk heights with the local reviewer. Our accessibility guide covers the rest.

Walk-up and pickup windows

A window lets a customer collect without entering the store at all, which can take real pressure off a busy urban location.

  • At our Dunkin’ just off Lexington Avenue, we added a walk-up window beside the main entrance so commuters can buy a coffee without stepping inside.
  • At our Dunkin’ in Washington Heights, a walk-up window pulls quick orders off the main line and keeps the handful of seats open.
  • At our Dunkin’ in Throggs Neck, a walk-up window beside the drive-thru lane lets guests on foot pick up without joining the car line.

Franchisees ask for windows often. The window has to fit the kitchen’s flow, not the other way round, so test it in the layout before committing. On a freestanding site, a drive-thru can also be reduced to a pickup window for orders placed ahead, with no menu board or speaker. See our drive-thru guide for the rest of the lane.

Stores built mostly for pickup

Some formats are designed around takeout and delivery from the start. Our Buffalo Wild Wings GO in Schenectady is arranged for takeout and delivery with limited seating, and our Buffalo Wild Wings GO on Park Avenue South runs from the order counter past a sauce bar to a small seating area at the back. Pizza Hut in Jackson Heights has no dining room and is built for pickup and delivery.

Markings and wayfinding

Small cues do real work. At our Dunkin’ on East 170th Street in the Bronx, pink circular floor graphics mark the pickup area. At our Dunkin’ and Baskin-Robbins in Ozone Park, a marked queue line, stanchions and a mobile pickup sign keep coffee and ice cream customers in their own lines.

Questions

Common Questions

Where should the mobile order pickup shelf go?

Close to the entrance and clear of the ordering line, so an app customer can walk in, collect and leave without crossing the queue. Brands often have a rule; on our Taco Bell in Kingsbridge, the brand wanted the mobile pickup unit on the front counter or as a stand-up unit in the dining room.

Do delivery drivers need their own space in a restaurant?

They need a hand-off point that does not block the counter, with room for several drivers waiting at once, and on suburban sites somewhere to park. Size it for your busiest hour, not an average one.

Can a walk-up window be added to an existing restaurant?

Often, yes, as part of a remodel. It changes the storefront, so it needs the landlord's approval and is shown on the permit drawings, and in a historic district or landmarked building it may need preservation review as well.
