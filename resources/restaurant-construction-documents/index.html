What's in a Restaurant Construction Document Set?

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026

The short answer

A restaurant construction document set is the sealed package of drawings a building department reviews and a contractor builds from. It covers architecture plus the structural, mechanical, plumbing, electrical, sprinkler and fire alarm work the space needs, and every sheet has to agree with every other sheet.

A restaurant construction document set, often called the CDs, is the full package of drawings that gets a restaurant permitted and built. The building department reviews it for code. The health department checks the kitchen against it. The contractor prices from it and builds from it. If something is missing or contradictory, it shows up later as a plan review comment, a change order or a failed inspection.

A typical set for a restaurant fit-out includes a title sheet, architectural plans and details, and engineering sheets for the mechanical, plumbing, electrical, sprinkler and fire alarm work. Structural sheets are added when the project touches the structure, such as a new rooftop unit, a mezzanine or an addition.

How a set is organized

We organize every set by discipline, in the same order on every project:

  • Structural, when the work needs it
  • Architectural
  • Mechanical: heating, cooling, ventilation and the kitchen hood exhaust
  • Plumbing: fixtures, sinks, water heater, gas piping and grease handling
  • Electrical: power to every piece of equipment, lighting and the panel
  • Sprinkler
  • Fire alarm

Each sheet has a number and a name, such as “A1.0 Partition Plan”. The letter tells you the discipline and the number tells you where it sits in the set. A sheet index on the title sheet lists every sheet, so a plan reviewer can confirm nothing is missing.

What the architectural sheets cover

The architectural sheets are the core of the set. For a typical restaurant they include:

  • Title sheet: project information, the code analysis (occupancy class, construction type, occupant load, exits and plumbing fixture count), the sheet index and general notes. It also states who is responsible for what. On one of our Jersey City projects, the title sheet made the general contractor responsible for designing, filing and installing the fire alarm system.
  • Demolition plan: what comes out of the existing space.
  • Partition plan: new walls, with dimensions and wall types.
  • Floor plan and seating plan: the layout guests and staff will use, with accessible routes and clearances.
  • Equipment plan and schedule: every piece of kitchen and front counter equipment, tagged and listed with its utility needs. Health departments rely heavily on this sheet.
  • Reflected ceiling plan: ceiling heights, lighting, diffusers and access panels, seen from above.
  • Finish plan and schedule: floors, walls, base and ceilings by room.
  • Elevations and sections: storefront, counters, millwork and walls drawn straight on.
  • Details and door schedule: how specific conditions are built, and what hardware goes on each door.

What the engineering sheets cover

Restaurants put more load on engineering than almost any other small tenant. The mechanical sheets carry the kitchen hood, exhaust fan and makeup air, which is fresh air replacing what the hood removes. The plumbing sheets show the grease interceptor, hand sinks, prep and mop sinks and gas service. The electrical sheets have to match the equipment schedule outlet by outlet.

Some work is handled as a deferred submittal, meaning a piece filed separately after the main permit. Sprinkler and fire alarm drawings are often prepared by specialty contractors this way, and we have also carried mechanical as a deferred submittal on a mall project when the landlord’s process called for it. The title sheet and general notes should say clearly which items are deferred and who owns them.

How big is a set?

Size follows scope. Recent sealed sets of ours ran 31 sheets for a sandwich shop fit-out in Pennsylvania and 41 sheets for a bagel shop fit-out in central Florida. A small counter-service storefront with no cooking can be much leaner. A freestanding drive-thru with site plans, a new building and full engineering is larger.

Sheet count is not a measure of quality. What matters is that the set answers every question a reviewer or contractor will ask.

Where the prototype ends and the site begins

Most franchise projects start from the brand’s prototype drawings. We work from the prototype version the brand has agreed for that project. A newer release does not automatically replace a set that is already in progress, which keeps a mid-design project from being redrawn without a decision.

Then the prototype is made site specific. We keep only the details that apply to the scope, and replace every inherited address, equipment item and note with confirmed information about the real space. The brand usually reviews the set too. On one of our projects, the brand’s approval of every finish was required before the construction set could be finalized.

How we check a set before it goes out

A set that looks complete can still fail plan review if its parts disagree. Before a set is issued, we check that:

  • Every change is followed through. If a sink moves on the plan, the change also has to appear in the elevations, details, tags, schedules and the plumbing and electrical sheets.
  • Every reference leads somewhere real. A tag on a plan should point to a detail that exists, on the sheet it names, describing that exact condition, and match the schedule.
  • Notes say something. Each note names the action, the item and the location. Boilerplate that does not apply is removed.
  • The exported PDF is right. We check sheet count, order, index and revision information, and read the sheets at printed size so notes and dimensions are legible.
  • Open items are stated, not guessed. If a dimension or product is still unconfirmed, the set says so. We never invent a value to close a question.

The responsible licensed professional then applies the seal and signature. Every issued set carries its date, and superseded versions are kept on file, so there is always a record of exactly what was sent to the building department, the landlord or the contractor.

Agencies ask for more than drawings

Plan review requirements vary by jurisdiction, and some reviewers want more than the standard set. A transit authority reviewing one of our Manhattan projects required digital signatures and seals from both the architect and the engineer of record, plus the full menu with the permit submission. A county health department in Tennessee required the plans and proposed menu to be delivered in paper. A historic commission in New Jersey wanted construction document level drawings, because it was judging how the finished work would affect a historic building.

Knowing these requirements before submission is part of producing the set, not an afterthought.

Questions

Common Questions

How many sheets are in a restaurant drawing set?
It depends on scope. Recent sealed sets of ours ran 31 sheets for a sandwich shop fit-out in Pennsylvania and 41 sheets for a bagel shop in central Florida. A ground-up drive-thru with site work runs larger.
Can I use the brand's prototype drawings as my permit set?
No. A prototype is a starting point. The set has to be redrawn around the real space, with the site's own dimensions, utilities, code analysis and equipment, and sealed by an architect licensed in your state.
Who stamps the construction documents?
A licensed architect seals the architectural sheets, and licensed engineers seal the engineering sheets they are responsible for. Some agencies also ask for digital signatures from both the architect and the engineer of record.
What happens if the drawings change after they are issued?
We revise the affected sheets, follow the change into every related plan, detail and schedule, and reissue the set with a new date. Superseded versions are kept on file so there is always a record of what was issued when.
