Restaurant Permit Drawings: What's in the Set

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant construction document set is the sealed package of drawings a building department reviews and a contractor builds from. It covers the architecture plus the engineering the space needs, and every sheet has to agree with every other sheet. Some parts, often sprinkler and fire alarm drawings, may be prepared by specialty contractors and filed separately, so the set should say exactly who provides what.

A restaurant construction document set, often called the CDs, is the full package of drawings that gets a restaurant permitted and built. The building department reviews it for code. The health department checks the kitchen against it. The contractor prices from it and builds from it. If something is missing or contradictory, it shows up later as a plan review comment, a change order or a failed inspection.

A typical set for a restaurant fit-out includes a title sheet, architectural plans and details, and engineering sheets for the mechanical, plumbing and electrical work. Structural sheets are added when the project touches the structure, such as a new rooftop unit, a mezzanine or an addition. Sprinkler and fire alarm drawings are part of the permit picture too, but they are often prepared by specialty contractors and filed separately.

What the building code asks for

Most states base their building code on the International Building Code, whose chapter 1 says construction documents must show the work in enough detail to prove it meets the code, and must be prepared by a registered design professional where state law requires one. The same chapter allows deferred submittals, meaning parts of the design filed after the main permit, but only with the building official’s approval, and the deferred items have to be listed on the drawings. Local amendments change the details, so the reviewer’s own checklist is the final word.

Who may seal what is set by each state’s licensing law and board, not by the building code. Zelta Design is licensed today in 25 jurisdictions and works nationwide outside California, Alaska and Hawaii. When a project needs a new state, we get licensed there before any drawing needs a seal.

How a set is organized

We organize sets by discipline, in a consistent order, so reviewers and contractors can find things:

  • Structural, when the work needs it
  • Architectural
  • Mechanical: heating, cooling, ventilation and the kitchen hood exhaust
  • Plumbing: fixtures, sinks, water heater, gas piping and grease handling
  • Electrical: power to every piece of equipment, lighting and the panel
  • Sprinkler and fire alarm, when they are part of the main filing

Each sheet has a number and a name, such as “A1.0 Partition Plan”. The letter tells you the discipline and the number tells you where it sits in the set. A sheet index on the title sheet lists every sheet, so a plan reviewer can confirm nothing is missing.

What the architectural sheets cover

The architectural sheets are the core of the set. For a typical restaurant they include:

  • Title sheet: project information, the code analysis (occupancy class, construction type, occupant load, exits and plumbing fixture count), the sheet index and general notes. It also states who is responsible for what, including which items are deferred and which contractor designs and files them.
  • Demolition plan: what comes out of the existing space.
  • Partition plan: new walls, with dimensions and wall types.
  • Floor plan and seating plan: the layout guests and staff will use, with accessible routes and clearances.
  • Equipment plan and schedule: every piece of kitchen and front counter equipment, tagged and listed with its utility needs. Health departments rely heavily on this sheet.
  • Reflected ceiling plan: ceiling heights, lighting, diffusers and access panels, seen from above.
  • Finish plan and schedule: floors, walls, base and ceilings by room.
  • Elevations and sections: storefront, counters, millwork and walls drawn straight on.
  • Details and door schedule: how specific conditions are built, and what hardware goes on each door.

What the engineering sheets cover

Restaurants put more load on engineering than most small tenants. The mechanical sheets carry the kitchen hood, exhaust fan and make-up air, the outdoor air that replaces what the hood removes. The plumbing sheets show the grease interceptor, hand sinks, prep and mop sinks and gas piping. The electrical sheets have to match the equipment schedule outlet by outlet. Our guide to kitchen engineering explains those systems.

Deferred submittals need clear ownership. Sprinkler and fire alarm drawings are often prepared by specialty contractors this way, and on an outlet center project we carried the mechanical design as a deferred submittal when the property’s process called for it.

What the set includes versus what the proposal includes

A permit set shows everything the building department needs to see. That is not the same as everything the architect is hired to design. Sprinkler design, fire alarm design, civil site work, signage and kitchen equipment drawings may come from the architect’s team, the landlord, the brand’s vendors or specialty contractors, depending on the contract. Our guide to restaurant architect proposals explains how to read the scope, and our guide to who does what on a restaurant project covers the roles.

A quick scope-to-drawing check for owners:

  • Does the title sheet list every discipline the project needs, and who provides each one?
  • Are deferred items named, with the responsible contractor?
  • Does the equipment schedule match the brand’s current equipment list?
  • Are the landlord’s work and the tenant’s work drawn as separate scopes?
  • Is anything shown as “by others” that nobody has actually been hired to do?

Where the prototype ends and the site begins

Most franchise projects start from the brand’s prototype drawings. We work from the prototype version the brand has agreed for that project. A newer release does not automatically replace a set already in progress, which keeps a mid-design project from being redrawn without a decision.

Then the prototype is made site specific. We keep only the details that apply to the scope, and replace every inherited address, equipment item and note with confirmed information about the real space. The brand usually reviews the set too, and its approval of finishes and layout often has to land before the construction set is finalized.

How we check a set before it goes out

A set that looks complete can still fail plan review if its parts disagree. Before a set is issued, we check that:

  • Every change is followed through. If a sink moves on the plan, the change also has to appear in the elevations, details, tags, schedules and the plumbing and electrical sheets.
  • Every reference leads somewhere real. A tag on a plan should point to a detail that exists, on the sheet it names, describing that exact condition, and match the schedule.
  • Notes say something. Each note names the action, the item and the location. Boilerplate that does not apply is removed.
  • The exported PDF is right. We check sheet count, order, index and revision information, and read the sheets at printed size so notes and dimensions are legible.
  • Open items are stated, not guessed. If a dimension or product is still unconfirmed, the set says so. We never invent a value to close a question.

The responsible licensed professional then applies the seal and signature. Every issued set carries its date, and superseded versions are kept on file. When comments come back, our guide to answering plan review comments covers the response.

Agencies ask for more than drawings

Plan review requirements vary by jurisdiction, and some reviewers want more than the standard set: digital signatures from each design professional, the menu with the submission, paper copies, or forms of their own. A historic commission in New Jersey wanted construction document level drawings for our Taco Bell in Jersey City, because it was judging how the finished work would affect a historic building. Knowing these requirements before submission is part of producing the set.

Questions

Common Questions

How many sheets are in a restaurant drawing set?
It depends on scope, not on quality. A small counter-service storefront with no cooking can be lean. A cooking kitchen adds mechanical, plumbing and electrical sheets, and a ground-up drive-thru with site work, a new building and full engineering runs much larger.
Can I use the brand's prototype drawings as my permit set?
No. A prototype is a starting point. The set has to be redrawn around the real space, with the site's own dimensions, utilities, code analysis and equipment, and sealed by design professionals licensed in your state.
Who stamps the construction documents?
A licensed architect seals the architectural sheets, and licensed engineers usually seal the engineering sheets they are responsible for. State law and the local building department decide exactly which sheets need which seal, and some agencies want digital signatures from each design professional.
What happens if the drawings change after they are issued?
We revise the affected sheets, follow the change into every related plan, detail and schedule, and reissue the set with a new date. Superseded versions are kept on file so there is always a record of what was issued when.
