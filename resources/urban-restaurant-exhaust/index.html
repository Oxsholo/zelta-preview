Getting Kitchen Exhaust Out of a City Storefront

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

In a city storefront with other floors above, a kitchen that needs a grease hood also needs a legal path for its exhaust: up through a shaft to the roof, up the outside of the rear wall, or out through a facade louver where the code and the reviewing agencies allow it. Which path works depends on the building, the landlord and, in historic districts, the preservation reviewers. Confirm it before you sign the lease, because without one the site cannot run a menu that needs a hood.

A kitchen that fries, grills or otherwise produces grease-laden smoke normally needs a grease-rated hood, and the hood is only as good as the duct behind it. In a freestanding building the duct goes straight up through the roof to a fan. In a city storefront with apartments or offices above, there is no straight up. The exhaust has to find a legal path through or around a building you do not control. If there is no path, the site cannot run a menu that needs a hood.

That is why we look for the exhaust route before almost anything else in an urban project. Our kitchen engineering guide is the overview of the hood, make-up air, fire suppression, grease and utilities. This guide is only about the route out.

The rules behind the route

The duct route is governed by the mechanical code your jurisdiction adopted. In most states that is a version of the International Mechanical Code, whose chapter 5 covers grease ducts, the fire-rated enclosures around them where they pass through a building, and where an exhaust outlet may end relative to roofs, walls, openings, air intakes and property lines. Many jurisdictions also apply NFPA 96, the national fire safety standard for commercial cooking ventilation. New York City writes its own construction codes, including its own mechanical code, so a route that works in one city may not work in the next.

Those rules depend on the cooking equipment, the duct type and local amendments. Each route below is a question for the mechanical engineer and the reviewing agency, not an assumption.

The three ways out

Most city projects end up choosing between two of these.

Up through the building. The duct rises from the kitchen through the floors above, inside a fire-rated enclosure, to a fan on the roof. It is the cleanest answer for the neighbors and the reviewers, but it needs a shaft or a place to build one, and every floor it passes through belongs to the landlord or another tenant.

Up the outside of the building. The duct leaves the kitchen through the rear wall and climbs the back of the building to the roof. This avoids the upper floors, but it needs access to the rear of the building, the landlord’s consent, protection where the code requires it and, in a historic district, a design the preservation reviewers will accept.

Out through the facade. The exhaust leaves through a louver in the storefront or a side wall. Where it is allowed, it can save a project that has no other route. On one Queens storefront, the kitchen exhaust went out through the front louvers. Whether a wall discharge is acceptable depends on the cooking, the equipment and the code your jurisdiction adopted.

Facade louvers: less room than you think

A storefront looks wide until you try to fit the mechanical openings into it. The kitchen needs an exhaust outlet, and it also needs outdoor air coming in, both as make-up air, which replaces what the hood removes, and for general ventilation. The mechanical code keeps exhaust outlets a set distance from air intakes, operable windows and doors.

On a recent Manhattan storefront, our mechanical engineer found that the louver space along the storefront could not hold both the exhaust and the outdoor air intake while keeping the required separation between them. One of them had to find another way out. That is a common outcome on narrow frontages, and it is far cheaper to discover at the test fit than after the storefront is ordered.

Louvers are also part of the facade design. Brands care where they sit: on one Manhattan storefront, the brand’s design reviewer asked for an elevation showing the final louver location relative to the upper bulkhead, the signage and the canopy before approving the design.

Historic districts and landmarked buildings

In a historic district, the exhaust route is reviewed as architecture. Louvers, rear-wall ducts and rooftop fans all change how the building looks.

In New York City, the Landmarks Preservation Commission says that any change to the exterior of a storefront on a designated building requires a permit, and on one of our Manhattan projects, louvers in a landmarked storefront meant an application covering their style and effect on the building. In Jersey City, the preservation commission asked for risers showing where the kitchen would exhaust as part of the application for our Taco Bell on Montgomery Street. Our guide to restaurants in historic buildings covers that review in full, and our NYC permits guide covers how Landmarks fits with the building permit.

Existing shafts and flues

An existing shaft or flue stack can look like a shortcut. It can be one, but only if the engineer confirms it is built and rated for the type of exhaust, the building official accepts it and the landlord agrees to share it. Building management may refuse a new connection to an existing stack, especially one already serving other tenants. Get the shaft’s construction, its current users and the landlord’s consent in writing before the design depends on it.

Reusing a former restaurant’s hood and duct can also work, if the system suits your cooking line and passes current code. Our guide to second-generation spaces covers what to check.

Where the route ends: the roof

Whichever route the duct takes, the fan and much of the other kitchen equipment usually end up on the roof. City landlords often set where that equipment may sit, whether it must stand on dunnage, a raised steel frame, and how the roof is reached for cleaning. The structure has to carry it too. Our guide to adding rooftop units covers the roof framing, curbs, dunnage and access, so plan the route and the roof together.

Lock the route into the lease

The time to solve exhaust is before the lease is signed, while you still have leverage. Ask the landlord to confirm in writing:

  • The route the duct may take, through or along the building, ideally on a sketch.
  • Where on the roof the fan, make-up air unit and other equipment may sit, and on what support.
  • Your right to reach the duct and roof equipment for cleaning and repair.
  • Who maintains the shaft or enclosure, and who pays to repair the roof around new penetrations.

Your attorney should turn those answers into lease language. We can tell you whether the route works.

If no route can be agreed, the menu may have to change. Some cooking produces little or no grease, and some equipment is listed to run without a conventional hood, but whether that is acceptable is decided by the code and the local fire and building officials, not by the equipment brochure. Ask before you plan a menu around it.

Questions

Common Questions

Can a restaurant vent its kitchen exhaust through the front of the building?

Sometimes. We have designed storefronts where the exhaust left through front louvers, but the mechanical code sets separations between exhaust outlets and air intakes, windows, doors and property lines, and a narrow storefront may not have room for both the exhaust and the fresh air intake. In a landmarked building the louver also needs preservation approval.

Can I connect my kitchen exhaust to the building's existing flue or shaft?

Only if the engineer confirms the shaft is built and rated for grease exhaust under the code your jurisdiction adopted, the building official accepts it and the landlord agrees in writing. Building managers can and do refuse new connections to an existing stack.

What should a restaurant lease say about kitchen exhaust?

It should give you the right to run the duct along an agreed route, to place the fan and other equipment on the roof, and to reach them for cleaning and repair. Ask for the route to be confirmed in writing, ideally with a sketch, and have your attorney put it in the lease.
