Adding Rooftop Units to a Restaurant: Curbs, Dunnage, Structure

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant adds weight and holes to a roof: heating and cooling units, a kitchen exhaust fan, make-up air and often walk-in condensers. Before any of it is set, someone has to confirm the roof framing can carry the load where the equipment sits, decide whether each unit goes on a curb or on raised steel dunnage, and follow the landlord's roof rules. Where the framing is hidden, plan a field check once demolition exposes it, and get any added support designed and accepted before the units are set.

Adding rooftop units to a restaurant is a structural question first and a mechanical one second. A kitchen brings heavy equipment to the roof: heating and cooling units, an exhaust fan over the hood, make-up air to replace what the hood pulls out, and often the condensers for the walk-in cooler and freezer. Each one adds weight at a specific point and a hole or support in the roof. Before any of it is set, the framing has to be shown to carry it, the mounting has to be chosen, and the landlord has to agree.

Brand construction teams ask about this often. One brand construction manager put it directly: make sure the roof trusses can handle the rooftop unit and make-up air load if they are not in the planned location. Another asked whether we had photos above the ceiling to understand the truss layout for unit placement and duct routing. This guide covers how those answers are reached. For the kitchen systems themselves, see our kitchen engineering guide.

Why location matters as much as weight

A roof can carry a heavy unit in one spot and fail to carry the same unit a few feet away. The check is about the specific beams, joists or trusses under each piece of equipment.

Work on an existing roof falls under the existing-building code your state adopts, often a version of the model International Existing Building Code with amendments. The structural engineer checks the applicable existing-building code method, changes in load and member capacity, and specifies strengthening where required. That evaluation starts from what each member carries today and what the new unit adds.

Weight is not the whole story. As STRUCTURE magazine explains using the 2018 code, moving an unchanged unit from near a support to the middle of a span can raise the bending on a beam sharply, even though nothing got heavier. Your state’s adopted edition and amendments govern, so the structural engineer confirms them for the address. We fix every rooftop location on the roof plan before the engineers design, and a unit that moves late goes back to the structural engineer.

Find out what is holding up the roof

You cannot check framing you cannot see. In an existing building we look for the original structural drawings, look above the ceiling during the survey, and record anything hidden as unknown rather than assumed.

When the framing stays hidden until construction, we say so on the drawings and plan the check for the right moment. On a recent fit-out in West Virginia, the county’s plan reviewer asked for structural documentation for the new rooftop equipment. The roof structure was concealed except at the far rear, so our response committed to field verify the framing once demolition exposed it and to submit any required roof support to the building inspector before any rooftop equipment was set.

Field verification is a plan for when the check happens, not permission to install first. The units stay off the roof until the framing has been checked and any reinforcement has been designed by a structural engineer licensed in that state and accepted by the building department. See our guide to the existing conditions survey for how we record unknowns.

Sometimes the roof is rebuilt instead, as in a building conversion or a new building. New framing can then be designed for the equipment from the start.

Curbs, dunnage and other mounts

Each unit needs a mount, and the choice affects the structure, the roof warranty and the landlord’s approval. The three common options:

  • Roof curb. A raised frame built into the roof deck, flashed into the roofing, with the unit on top and the duct passing through. Common for packaged rooftop units and exhaust fans on low-slope roofs.
  • Dunnage. A raised steel frame that holds equipment above the roof surface and carries its load to the walls or main structure. It keeps units off the roofing and lets roofers work underneath.
  • Wall or grade mounting. Some equipment never goes on the roof. Condensers and supplemental units can sit on a wall or on the ground where the landlord allows.

Landlords often decide. At one Bronx building, the landlord’s building manager would not allow rooftop units to sit directly on the roof; they had to be on dunnage, and the walk-in condensers on a roof setback had to go on dunnage too, matching the existing equipment already on angled steel. On a Manhattan storefront, the landlord chose the location for a supplemental unit and required it to be mounted at least above the height of the door. Dunnage carries its own load to the structure, so it goes through the same structural check as the units. Who pays for roof work and supports is usually set in the lease; see landlord vs tenant work.

Exhaust fans and make-up air

The kitchen exhaust fan is the piece most likely to set the roof layout. Its duct has to rise from the hood to the fan, and the fan has to sit where its discharge is clear of air intakes and neighbors.

Brands usually fix the hood and fan in the prototype. On our Taco Bell in Morristown, New Jersey, the hood and fans followed the brand’s prototype with the fans on the roof, and the rooftop units were gas-fired as the prototype called for. Make-up air adds another unit or more capacity in an existing one, which is exactly the load brand teams ask us to check. In a city storefront where the duct cannot easily reach the roof, the route itself becomes the problem; see getting kitchen exhaust out of a city storefront.

Reuse before you add

The lightest rooftop unit is the one already there. Where an existing unit is in good condition and sized close to the need, keeping it can reduce new roof openings, new supports and added load. Reuse still has to be evaluated: the unit is checked against the new equipment list and the mechanical design, and the permit scope depends on what else changes.

On one Queens store, Erik Liepins, our Principal Architect, kept the existing rooftop unit and added a supplementary system only where it was needed.

Access, safety and inspection

The mechanical code also asks how a technician reaches and services the equipment. The International Mechanical Code sets rules for ladders or permanent access to rooftop equipment, guards near roof edges and a service outlet near the units, and states and towns amend them. In the 2021 edition, a climb of more than 16 feet calls for a permanent ladder or other permanent access, and guards are required where equipment needing service sits within 10 feet of a roof edge that is more than 30 inches above the surface below. These can add a roof hatch, a fixed ladder or a guard rail, so they belong in the first roof layout.

Paperwork matters at inspection. For a new rooftop unit in Queens, Erik advised keeping the unit’s certificate of compliance on site, because the fire department would ask for it if inspectors stopped in. In a historic district, the commission may review rooftop equipment and ducts that can be seen from the street (see historic buildings).

Before you commit to a roof

  • Who owns the roof, and what are the landlord’s rules for equipment, mounting and roofers?
  • Are the original structural drawings available, and can we see above the ceiling?
  • Where will each unit, fan and condenser sit, and is that fixed on the roof plan?
  • Can existing units be reused?
  • Who designs and seals any reinforcement, and is that engineer licensed in the state?

Questions

Common Questions

Do I need a structural engineer to add a rooftop unit?

Usually, yes. Someone has to establish what the roof framing carries now and what the new equipment adds at its exact location. Where that check shows the framing needs help, the building department will expect reinforcement designed and sealed by an engineer licensed in that state.

What is the difference between a roof curb and dunnage?

A curb is a raised frame built into the roof that the unit sits on, with the duct passing through it. Dunnage is a raised steel frame that holds equipment above the roof surface. Some landlords require dunnage so nothing sits directly on their roof.

Can I put a heavier rooftop unit in the same spot as the old one?

Sometimes, but not without a check. The structural engineer checks the applicable existing-building code method, changes in load and member capacity, and specifies strengthening where required. Moving a unit can overload framing even if it weighs the same.

Who decides where rooftop units go in a shopping center?

The landlord, together with your design team. Landlords commonly set locations, mounting methods and the roofer who may make penetrations, so get their rules before the mechanical design starts.
