Opening a Dave's Hot Chicken: Design, Permits and Build-Out

Zelta Design · 5 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A Dave's Hot Chicken is a frying kitchen wrapped in a bold, mural-heavy interior, and it opens in city storefronts, strip centers and freestanding drive-thru buildings. Many of the stores we design are conversions of former retail space, so the hood, make-up air, grease control and change of use decide the schedule as much as the design does.

A Dave’s Hot Chicken is a compact frying kitchen behind a loud, mural-covered front of house. The design work splits in two. The brand’s look, with its murals, red seating and neon, is set by the brand and adapted to each space. The kitchen, with its hood, make-up air and grease control, is set by the menu and by the building you choose, and it is usually what decides how long design and permitting take.

We have designed Dave’s Hot Chicken stores since 2022, in New York, New Jersey, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia and Tennessee. Many of ours are conversions of spaces that were never restaurants. This guide covers design, permits and build-out. For investment figures, read Item 7 of the brand’s Franchise Disclosure Document, the estimated initial investment table required by the FTC Franchise Rule; the FTC’s guide to buying a franchise explains how to read it.

The formats

  • City storefronts. Narrow, deep spaces where ordering and pickup sit near the door. Our Park Slope store seats 16 on stools. Our Harlem store on 125th Street fits into 3,061 square feet, and our Upper East Side store keeps ordering, pickup and a small dining area close together so the line does not spill onto the sidewalk.
  • Strip center units. Our Ocean Township, New Jersey store sits in a strip center unit with a counter that includes soft-serve machines.
  • Freestanding buildings. Our Sumter, South Carolina store has a drive-thru and a covered patio, and its layout keeps dine-in, walk-up pickup and drive-thru customers from crossing paths. Our Florence, South Carolina store sits on a corner site with a red neon roofline, and our Greenville, North Carolina store covers its patio with red shade sails.
  • Larger dining rooms. Our White Plains, New York store is 4,060 square feet, with full-height murals and neon throughout.

Murals and signs

The murals are the brand. Our Hell’s Kitchen store and our Bay Ridge, Brooklyn store carry graffiti-style murals from the interior onto the facade. In Bay Ridge the artwork covers the upper facade of a walk-up apartment building, so the shop reads far wider than its storefront. On our freestanding store in Shelby, North Carolina, the murals cover the outside walls of the building.

Inside, the murals are a brand decision. Outside, they can become a town decision. Depending on the local code, a painted facade can count as signage, as a facade alteration, or both, and in a historic district it may need its own review. Exterior neon raises the same question: many sign codes regulate lit elements on the outside of a building, so check how your town classifies it. We check the local sign rules before the exterior artwork is finalized, so a mural is not designed, approved by the brand and then turned down by the town. Our signage guide covers those approvals.

Layout details the brand reviews

The brand’s development team reviews the layout, and its comments are practical. Settle the details that affect the line at the layout stage: keep tables out of the path in front of the doors, so the entry stays clear for the queue and for the accessible route, and keep pickup close to the door. Changing them on site costs more than changing a drawing.

Converting retail space

Many of the Dave’s Hot Chicken stores we design go into space that was a shop or an office. That turns the project into a change of use, from retail to a restaurant, and it usually means building the kitchen from nothing. Our guide to former restaurant versus retail space explains the tradeoff in detail. Two recent examples:

  • A former phone store in eastern North Carolina. About 2,400 square feet in a sprinklered shopping center building, classified as A-2 assembly, the building code’s class for restaurants. The building and zoning permits were filed together. Health review was a separate filing: North Carolina sends plans for chain and franchise restaurants with ten or more locations to the state’s Plan Review Unit. North Carolina also requires the owner to designate a lien agent, a registered firm that receives notices from contractors, for most commercial improvements costing $40,000 or more (G.S. 44A-11.1), and that designation is tied to the building permit.
  • A larger space in Tennessee. The existing space was larger than the store needed, so the plan was to demise it, meaning to split off the part the restaurant would use, down to roughly 2,500 square feet during early design. We started from a 3D scan of the existing space converted into a measured drawing.

Our survey guide explains why every project starts with measured existing conditions.

The kitchen

A frying kitchen drives the engineering.

  • The hood and its maker. The hood, its fire suppression and its exhaust fan are usually chosen early, because the rest of the mechanical design depends on them. Some jurisdictions review the hood and its suppression system separately from the building permit.
  • Make-up air in hot climates. The hood pulls out air that must be replaced. In the South, decide early whether the make-up air should be cooled as well as heated, because untreated outside air makes a hot, humid kitchen worse.
  • Grease. Local sewer departments set their own rules for the grease interceptor. On one South Carolina store, the city sewer department required the installed grease control device to be serviced and cleaned before opening. Ask the sewer authority what it needs at closeout.

Our guides to kitchen engineering and urban exhaust routes cover hoods, ducts, grease and gas in more depth.

From permit to opening

Local steps vary, and small ones can hold up an opening. Many building departments ask for the estimated construction value on the permit application, so have your contractor’s number ready. On one South Carolina store, the city needed no separate occupancy inspection, because the inspections on the fit-out permits covered it. Once the work was finished, the city issued the certificate of occupancy and only then told the business license office that the license could be issued. Planning the license around the certificate of occupancy, not around the construction schedule, avoids a store that is built but cannot open. Our certificate of occupancy guide covers closeout in more depth.

Questions

Common Questions

How do I know whether a space can take a Dave's Hot Chicken kitchen?

Check the exhaust route before you sign. The fryer hood needs a duct to an exhaust fan, usually on the roof, plus make-up air, gas and a place for grease control. If the landlord will not allow a duct through the building or a fan on the roof, the space may not work at all.

Can a Dave's Hot Chicken go into a former retail store?

Yes, and many of ours do. Expect a full restaurant fit-out: a new hood, make-up air, grease control and plumbing, plus the review that comes with turning a shop into a restaurant. On one eastern North Carolina store, a former phone store, the building and zoning permits were filed together and the state health review ran separately.

Do the murals need approval?

Inside, the brand sets them. Outside, depending on the local code, they can count as signage or a facade change, and in a historic district they may need their own review. Check before the artwork is designed.

What has to happen before a new store can get its business license?

In some places the business license waits for the certificate of occupancy. On one South Carolina store, the city issued the certificate of occupancy when the fit-out was finished and only then told the business license office the license could be issued.
