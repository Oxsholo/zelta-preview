What's in a Restaurant Architect's Proposal (and What Isn't)

Zelta Design · 6 min read · Updated October 11, 2026 · Reviewed by Erik Liepins, RA

The short answer

A restaurant architect's proposal should say what will be delivered in each phase, from the site survey to sealed permit drawings, which engineering is included, what the schedule depends on, how the fee is paid and what is excluded. Exclusions and assumptions matter as much as the scope: they show which other consultants your site may need and which events would change the fee.

A good restaurant architect’s proposal tells you what you will receive in each phase, which engineering is included, what the schedule depends on, how the fee is paid and what is left out. Read the last part twice. Exclusions are not hidden extras; they mark the edge of the architect’s work and show which other consultants your site may need.

This guide walks through the parts of a proposal using the structure of our current template, without fee amounts, so you can compare proposals line by line. If you are still deciding whether you need an architect at all, start with do I need an architect to open a restaurant. For what to ask a firm beyond the paperwork, see how to choose a restaurant architect.

The overview: what, where and for which permit

The first paragraph should describe your project precisely. Ours state the address, the approximate size of the space, what it was before, what the work does and which building department the sealed drawings are for. A typical overview describes converting a former restaurant to a brand’s prototype, reusing existing building systems where practical, with signed and sealed drawings for permit with the city.

Check it carefully. If the overview describes the wrong condition, such as an existing building on what is really a ground-up site, the scope and fee that follow will not fit your project. Raise it before signing.

Scope, phase by phase

The scope should be broken into phases, each with a list of deliverables. A typical restaurant fit-out proposal from us has four:

  • Existing conditions survey. A site visit and full 3D scan of the space, existing floor plan, ceiling plan and exterior elevations drawn from the scan, and a record of the existing electrical service, water, sewer, gas, heating and cooling and rooftop equipment for reuse or replacement. See why every project starts with a site survey.
  • Architectural design and construction documents. A preliminary floor plan and elevations adapting the brand’s prototype to the space, for approval by you and the brand. Then the code review (occupant load, exits, plumbing fixture count, accessibility), demolition, floor, ceiling and finish plans, the accessible restroom, door, finish and equipment schedules, and sealed drawings for permit.
  • Structural engineering. A review of the roof framing that will carry the exhaust fan, make-up air unit and other rooftop equipment, with sealed structural drawings.
  • MEP engineering. Heating and cooling, the kitchen hood exhaust and make-up air, power for kitchen equipment, lighting and emergency lighting, panel schedules and load calculations, plumbing for sinks and equipment, water heater sizing, grease waste connections and gas piping, all with sealed drawings.

If a proposal lists only “construction documents”, ask what they contain. See what’s in a restaurant construction document set.

The schedule, and what it depends on

A useful schedule is written as durations from events you can see, not as calendar dates. Ours tie the preliminary floor plan to receiving the brand’s prototype drawing files and finishing the survey, and tie the construction documents to approval of that preliminary plan by you and the brand.

That wording is honest about where time goes. If the prototype files or the brand’s approval are late, the drawings are late. Permit review time is set by the agencies, so a proposal should not promise a permit date. See the restaurant build-out timeline.

How the fee is structured

Common structures are a fixed fee for a defined scope, a fee per site on a multi-location program, or hourly billing. Ours are usually one fixed fee for the scope, paid in installments at three milestones: contract signing, the start of construction documents and permit approval.

It helps to sort every service in a proposal into one of three groups:

GroupWhat it meansExamples to look for
IncludedCovered by the feeSurvey, architecture, named engineering disciplines, sealed permit set
ContingentPriced only if a condition occursStructural work once hidden framing is exposed, redesign for a new prototype version
Additional servicesBilled when you request and approve themConstruction support, extra site visits, extra resubmittals

Construction support deserves a direct question. Some proposals include it; ours treat contractor questions, submittal review and field visits as services agreed separately. See what your architect does during construction.

Exclusions: the list to read twice

The exclusions in our current template are:

  • Zoning, planning board and zoning board applications, site plan design, and town hearings or meetings.
  • Civil engineering, including site grading, drainage, grease interceptor location and sizing, and boundary surveys.
  • Fire alarm and hood fire suppression system design, by others.
  • Fire sprinkler system design.
  • Kitchen equipment selection and specification, which follows the brand’s equipment package.
  • Exterior sign design and sign permits.

Not every project needs every item. A fit-out in a former restaurant may need no zoning board and no civil engineer, while a freestanding drive-thru needs both. Each item your site does need maps to a person or firm; see who does what on a restaurant project. If your site needs one, ask early whether the architect can quote it separately or who usually provides it.

Assumptions: what the fee depends on

Assumptions are the conditions the price relies on. Ours typically include:

  • Owner decisions and equipment information arrive on time to hold the schedule.
  • The design is based on a named version of the brand’s prototype, with its revision number and date, supplied by the brand in drawing and PDF formats.
  • Existing plans from the owner or landlord are for reference only and are verified in the survey.
  • Work begins once the lease is signed and the brand has approved the site, unless the client asks in writing to start earlier.

If an assumption turns out false, such as a newer prototype version arriving mid-design, the proposal should tell both sides what happens next.

Caps on revisions, resubmittals and site visits

Owners often ask how many site visits, design revisions and plan review resubmittals the fee covers. A proposal can state a number for each, with anything beyond it needing your written approval first. That keeps cost predictable for you and scope clear for the architect.

Other details worth checking

  • Who signs. Ours are signed by Erik Liepins, RA, our Principal Architect.
  • How long the offer holds. Our current template states 30 days.
  • Reusing a set. When a new tenant takes over a site with a permitted design, a proposal can license the existing documents for that one address, with the original architect remaining architect of record.

Questions

Common Questions

Does an architect's proposal include the MEP engineering?
It should say so either way. Ours list structural engineering and MEP (mechanical, electrical and plumbing) engineering as their own sections with sealed drawings. If a proposal is silent, ask, because a separate engineering contract changes both cost and coordination.
What do restaurant architects usually exclude?
Commonly zoning board applications and site plans, civil engineering, fire sprinkler and fire alarm design, hood fire suppression design, kitchen equipment selection and sign design. Not every project needs each of these, but the ones your site does need will come from someone else.
How are restaurant architecture fees paid?
Often as one fixed fee for a defined scope, paid in installments tied to milestones such as signing, the start of construction documents and permit approval. Work outside the scope is usually billed separately once the owner approves it.
Can I ask for caps on revisions and site visits?
Yes, and it is reasonable. A proposal can state how many site visits, design revision rounds or plan review resubmittals the fee covers, with anything beyond that needing your written approval first.
